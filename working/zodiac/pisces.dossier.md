# Pisces: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact**: directly attested by a dated text, artifact, observation, or institutional record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction**: a modern inference from incomplete evidence.
- **Tradition**: a historically attested belief or practice, without implying factual truth.
- **Disputed claim**: supported by competing interpretations or uncertain texts.
- **Myth/legend**: a narrative presented by its source as sacred, fabulous, or etiological.
- **Modern invention**: a recent claim lacking ancient or medieval attestation.
- **Unverified claim**: encountered in modern literature but not traceable here to an adequate source.

“Pisces” denotes two related but different objects:

1. **The sign Pisces**: an ideal, exactly 30° sector of the zodiac.
2. **The constellation Pisces**: a historically changing star-picture and, since 1930, an irregularly bounded region of the sky.

Confusing them produces most popular disputes about dates, precession, and “thirteenth signs.”

# Basic identification

## The sign

**Documented astronomical/astrological convention.** Tropical Pisces occupies ecliptic longitude **330°–360°**, measured from the March equinox. It is the twelfth tropical sign. Its boundaries are mathematical and do not follow visible stars.

Its conventional modern civil-calendar dates are approximately **19 February–20 March**, varying by year, time zone, and ephemeris. The Sun enters tropical Aries at the March equinox, so tropical Pisces ends at that instant.

In most Indian sidereal systems, **Mīna rāśi** also spans 30°, but its zero point is displaced from the tropical zodiac by an **ayanāṃśa**. There is no single universal sidereal offset. Lahiri/Chitrapaksha, Raman, Krishnamurti, Fagan–Bradley, and other systems consequently give different ingress times. In a typical modern Lahiri calculation the Sun is in sidereal Mīna roughly from **mid-March to mid-April**. These are sign dates, not IAU-constellation dates.

## The constellation

**Documented fact.** Pisces is one of the 88 IAU constellations:

- Latin nominative: **Pisces**
- Genitive: **Piscium**
- Abbreviation: **Psc**
- Conventional meaning: **the Fishes**
- Position: largely north of the celestial equator, adjoining Andromeda, Pegasus, Aquarius, Cetus, and Aries
- Area: approximately 889 square degrees, conventionally ranked fourteenth in size
- Visibility: broadly visible from about 90° N to 65° S
- Best evening visibility: Northern Hemisphere autumn

The IAU defines it as an **area**, not merely the traditional two-fish line drawing. The formal boundary system was adopted in principle by the IAU in 1928 and published by Eugène Delporte in 1930; the IAU now supplies J2000 boundary coordinates and charts. [IAU constellation definitions](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

The Sun currently crosses the IAU constellation approximately **12 March–18 April**, with a day’s possible variation depending on year and computational convention. This interval is neither tropical Pisces nor a 30° sidereal Pisces.

## Names

### Mesopotamian

**Documented fact with disputed mapping.**

- Sumerogram **MUL SIM.MAḪ**, “the Great Swallow” or “Swallow.”
- **MUL A-nu-ni-tu₄ / Anunītu**, the goddess Anunitum, associated with another neighboring star figure.
- **MUL KUN.MEŠ**, “the Tails.”
- A fuller expression in *MUL.APIN* is commonly normalized as **KUN.MEŠ ša MUL SIM.MAḪ A-nu-ni-ti**, approximately “the tails of the Swallow, Anunitum.”

These are not straightforward ancient names for “two fishes.” The equation with later Pisces is positional and developmental. The Babylonian material appears to combine the tail regions of the Swallow and Anunitum; how this became the Greek two-fish-and-cord picture remains uncertain. A late Babylonian/Uruk image sometimes described as a swallow-fish composite is relevant but does not settle the transformation. [All Skies: Pisces and the Babylonian evidence](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Pisces)

Akkadian **nūnu**, “fish,” occurs in stellar names such as *Kullat Nūnu*, but claims that the canonical Babylonian constellation was simply “Nūnu and Šinūnūtu” are modern simplifications.

### Egyptian

**Documented fact.** In pharaonic Egypt before the Hellenistic period there is no securely attested native constellation identical to Greco-Roman Pisces. Indigenous Egyptian astronomy used decans and native divine/stellar figures.

On the circular Dendera zodiac, however, Pisces appears as **two fish joined by a line**, rendered within an Egyptian religious monument of the late Ptolemaic/early Roman period. This is evidence for adoption and Egyptianization of the Babylonian-Greek zodiac, not proof that Pisces originated in Old Kingdom Egypt. [Louvre: Dendera zodiac](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010028871)

No single pharaonic Egyptian spoken name for Pisces can be securely reconstructed from Dendera. Modern Arabic Egyptian uses **al-Ḥūt** or **al-Samakatan/al-Samakayn**, depending on register.

### Greek

- **Ἰχθύες, Ikhthýes**, “Fishes,” plural of **ἰχθύς**, “fish.”
- A dual form, **Ἰχθύε**, also occurs.
- Aratus calls the connecting star or joining point the **Σύνδεσμος ὑπουραῖος**, “bond beneath/by the tails,” while later authors shorten this to **Σύνδεσμος**, “Bond/Knot.”

### Latin

- **Pisces**, plural of **piscis**, “fish.”
- Indo-European etymology: conventionally connected with reconstructed **\*pisk-**, “fish,” also behind English *fish* through Germanic. [Etymonline: Pisces](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Pisces)
- Classical descriptive expressions include **Nodus Piscium**, “Knot of the Fishes”; **Commissura Piscium**, “junction of the Fishes”; and **Vinculum Piscium**, “bond of the Fishes.”

### Arabic

- **الحوتان, al-Ḥūtān**, “the two fishes,” a dual.
- **الحوت, al-Ḥūt**, “the fish,” is also used for the zodiacal sign in ordinary astrological naming.
- **السمكتان, al-Samakatān**, “the two fish,” occurs descriptively.
- Alpha Piscium: **الرشاء, al-Rishāʾ/al-Rashāʾ**, “the cord,” source of **Alrescha**.
- Fumalsamakah: **فم السمكة, fam al-samakah**, “mouth of the fish.”
- Medieval Arabic star books normally preserve the Ptolemaic figure while comparing its stars with indigenous Arabic asterisms.

### Persian

- **ماهی, māhī**, “fish.”
- **دو ماهی, do māhī**, “two fish.”
- **حوت, Ḥūt**, the Arabic loanword, is standard in learned Persian astrological nomenclature.
- Middle Persian zodiacal terminology is preserved imperfectly; surviving schemes largely reflect Greek zodiac names transmitted through late antique astronomical astrology. [Encyclopaedia Iranica: zodiac](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zodiac/)

### Hebrew

- **דגים, Dagim**, “fishes,” plural of **דג, dag**, “fish.”
- Rabbinic and later Jewish calendrical astrology associates Dagim with **Adar**.
- Medieval Hebrew astronomical works also transliterate Arabic and Latin terminology.

### Sanskrit and India

- **Mīna**, “fish,” and **Mīna-rāśi**, “the Fish sign.”
- Indian iconography often uses a pair of fish.
- **Documented transmission; details disputed.** The twelve-sign rāśi system and much horoscopic vocabulary show strong Hellenistic influence. The *Yavanajātaka* tradition and Varāhamihira demonstrate naturalized Indian use, but Indian astral science was not simply a translation of one Greek book: it combined imported sign astrology with older nakṣatra, calendrical, and omen systems.

### Medieval Latin and European vernaculars

- Medieval Latin: **Pisces**, sometimes **Duo Pisces**.
- English: **Pisces**, traditionally **the Fishes**.
- Spanish: **Piscis**, also explanatory **los Peces**.
- Portuguese: **Peixes**.
- French: **Poissons**.
- Italian: **Pesci**.
- German: **Fische**.
- Dutch: **Vissen**.
- Catalan: **Peixos**.
- Galician: **Peixes**.

These are inherited from Latin either as learned loans or literal vernacular translations.

## The glyph ♓

**Documented tradition, incompletely reconstructed.** The modern glyph consists of two opposed curves joined by a transverse stroke. Its conventional interpretation is two fish facing opposite directions, joined by a cord.

The ancient star-picture is much older than the standardized typographic glyph. Roman-period Greek horoscopes used shorthand signs, but forms varied. Medieval manuscripts commonly drew actual fish rather than relying exclusively on ♓. Renaissance almanacs, astronomical tables, and movable type gradually regularized the abstract sign. Unicode now encodes it as **U+2653 PISCES**.

Claims that ♓ is demonstrably:

- a Babylonian cuneiform ligature,
- two lunar crescents,
- a Christian cross between two souls,
- the Tigris and Euphrates,
- a vaginal or specifically “vesica piscis” diagram,

are **later symbolic interpretations or unverified derivations**, not securely documented etymologies of the glyph.

# Stars and boundaries

## Traditional anatomy

The customary figure contains:

1. A **northern or eastern fish**, near Andromeda.
2. A **western or southern fish**, marked by the Circlet below Pegasus.
3. Two long cords extending from their tails.
4. The cords’ junction at **α Piscium, Alrescha**.

Aratus already describes one fish as higher and more northerly than the other and says their tail-chains meet at one conspicuous star (*Phaenomena* 239–247). [Aratus, *Phaenomena*](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AratusPhaenomena.html)

Because atlas orientation varies—sky view versus globe view, north up versus traditional pictorial orientation—the same fish may be called “northern,” “eastern,” “western,” or “southern” in different sources.

## Principal stars

Pisces is large but faint. Its Bayer alpha is not its brightest star.

| Star | Approx. visual magnitude | Historical role and name |
|---|---:|---|
| **η Piscium** | 3.6 | Brightest star. IAU proper name **Alpherg**, ultimately from Arabic terminology usually connected with a “pouring/outflow” or water-related point; older *Kullat Nūnu* traditions are philologically uncertain. |
| **γ Piscium** | 3.7 | Bright member of the Circlet/western fish. No universally dominant ancient proper name. |
| **α Piscium** | 3.8 combined | **Alrescha**, Arabic *al-rishāʾ*, “cord”; the Knot joining the bands. A multiple-star system. |
| **ω Piscium** | 4.0 | Near the present March-equinox region; ordinary Bayer designation is more secure than various modern name claims. |
| **ι Piscium** | 4.1 | Circlet member in many modern definitions. |
| **ο Piscium** | 4.3 | IAU name **Torcular**, from medieval Latin *torcular*, “winepress,” reflecting a later Latin description rather than a classical Greek fish name. |
| **β Piscium** | 4.5 | IAU name **Fumalsamakah**, from Arabic “mouth of the fish.” |
| **ζ Piscium** | 5.2 combined | Important in Indian astronomy as **Revatī**, whose name the IAU has assigned to a component. |

The present IAU list also contains names conferred through modern NameExoWorlds campaigns—such as **Bélénos, Citadelle, Ebla**, and **Parumleo**—which are official proper names but not ancient Pisces lore. The historical provenance of a proper name must therefore be distinguished from the date of IAU standardization.

## Alrescha: the Knot

**Documented continuity with linguistic change.** Aratus’ beautiful joining star is the Greek **Syndesmos**. Latin writers translated it as *Nodus*, *Commissura*, or related terms. Arabic astronomical transmission called it *al-rishāʾ*, “cord,” which entered European star nomenclature as **Alrescha**, with spelling variants Alrisha and Alrischa. It marks the cords’ meeting more exactly than either fish’s body.

## The Circlet

**Modern asterism with older pictorial roots.** The Circlet is the oval/pentagonal western fish. Modern lists vary, usually including **γ, κ, λ, ι, and θ Piscium**, sometimes adding **TX Piscium** or another faint star. Robert Burnham’s 1978 handbook helped stabilize “Circlet of Pisces” in anglophone observing culture, but the fish-loop itself appears in much older atlases. Variation in membership is real; it is not an IAU-defined asterism. [Royal Astronomical Society of Canada asterism handbook](https://www.rasc.ca/sites/default/files/World%20Asterisms%20Project%20Handbook%20V%202024.4.pdf)

## Ptolemy

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogued **38 stars** in Pisces, describing positions anatomically rather than drawing polygonal borders. A Ptolemaic constellation was a set of formed stars plus sometimes “unformed” neighboring stars, not a bounded territory.

Ptolemy’s western fish loop is commonly identified with stars around β, γ, θ, ι, and nearby objects, but individual identifications can be disputed because:

- coordinates carry observational and copying errors;
- precession must be accounted for;
- Greek anatomical descriptions can fit more than one faint star;
- manuscript numerals were corrupted.

## Bayer, Hevelius, Flamsteed, and Bode

- **Johann Bayer, *Uranometria* (Augsburg, 1603):** retained the classical figure and assigned Greek letters. Letter order was only approximately by brightness and sometimes followed anatomy or atlas sequence, explaining why α Psc is not brightest.
- **Johannes Hevelius, *Firmamentum Sobiescianum/Uranographia* (Gdańsk, 1690):** presented Pisces in the globe-view tradition; historical counts give him approximately 39 constituent stars.
- **John Flamsteed, *Atlas Coelestis* (London, 1729):** supplied a more observation-driven telescopic atlas and the numerical designations now called Flamsteed numbers.
- **Johann Elert Bode, *Uranographia* (Berlin, 1801):** retained an elaborate pictorial Pisces, its cords and knot spread over a dense telescopic star field. His plate illustrates the late culmination of artistic constellation anatomy before official geometric borders.

Bayer, Hevelius, Flamsteed, and Bode changed which faint stars were emphasized and how the figure was drawn; none established the present IAU territory.

## Modern boundaries

**Documented fact.** The IAU boundaries divide the entire sky into non-overlapping regions. They were originally drawn along lines of right ascension and declination for epoch B1875.0; precession makes them look slightly oblique on J2000 charts. Stars and galaxies inside the polygon “belong” to Pisces whether or not they formed part of either ancient fish.

This produces a crucial change:

> Ptolemy’s Pisces was a star list and picture; IAU Pisces is a legal-like celestial parcel.

## Ecliptic, equinox, and precession

The ecliptic passes through Pisces. The **March equinox point** is currently within the IAU constellation, south of ω Piscium, and moves westward relative to the stellar background because of axial precession.

Hipparchus, in the second century BCE, is conventionally credited with recognizing precession from comparisons of longitudes and equinoctial observations. Ptolemy discusses it in the *Almagest*. The equinox’s movement from the traditional region of Aries into Pisces did not occur on one universally agreed date because ancient constellations lacked exact borders. Using reconstructed figure boundaries may place it around the last centuries BCE; imposing modern IAU borders retroactively gives a different answer.

The equinox retains the astronomical name **First Point of Aries** even though it is no longer inside the IAU constellation Aries.

### Tropical versus sidereal

- **Tropical zodiac:** 0° Aries is always the March equinox. Signs remain tied to seasons; Pisces is always 330°–360° tropical longitude.
- **Sidereal zodiac:** signs are held approximately fixed relative to stars through an ayanāṃśa.
- **IAU constellations:** irregular regions, not equal signs.

Thus “precession moved everyone’s sign” is true only if “sign” means the background constellation. It is false as a description of how tropical astrology defines signs.

## The “Age of Pisces”

**Astronomical premise, astrological historical scheme.** The March equinox’s location in stellar Pisces is observable. Turning that into a world-historical “Piscean Age” governed by Piscean meanings is an astrological doctrine.

An equal-sign age would last about **2,150 years**, one twelfth of the approximately 25,772-year precessional cycle. Constellation-based ages are unequal because constellations have unequal widths. Start dates proposed for the Piscean Age range from the second century BCE to the early Common Era; proposed ends range from the eighteenth century to well after 2500.

The identification of the Piscean Age with Christianity is **not an ancient Christian doctrine**. It became prominent in modern esotericism, Rosicrucianism, Theosophical and post-Theosophical writing, Rudolf Steiner’s anthroposophy, Max Heindel, Alice Bailey’s milieu, and C. G. Jung’s *Aion* (1951). Jung explicitly treated the fish symbolism as a meaningful historical-psychological synchronicity, not as a conventional historical proof that Christianity was created from Pisces. [Theosophical Society discussion of Jung and astrological ages](https://www.theosophical.org/publications/quest-magazine/c-g-jung-s-vision-of-the-aquarian-age)

# Chronology of attestation

## Before the uniform zodiac

### Egyptian decans, third–second millennia BCE

**Documented fact.** Egyptian decans were groups of stars used for calendrical and nocturnal timekeeping. They are attested on Middle Kingdom coffin lids and later astronomical ceilings.

**Negative finding:** they do not constitute an early native twelve-sign zodiac, and no early Egyptian “Pisces” can safely be inferred from the mere occurrence of fish imagery.

### Mesopotamian star lists, second–first millennia BCE

**Documented fact.** Mesopotamian scribes maintained star lists, omen series, “paths” of Anu, Enlil, and Ea, and lunar-path constellations. Some future zodiac figures existed long before twelve equal signs, but the system was not yet the mathematical zodiac.

## *MUL.APIN*, compiled around the early first millennium BCE

**Documented text, dating approximate.** *MUL.APIN* places the Swallow and Anunitum in the relevant sky and refers to their tails in the lunar path. This is the principal antecedent for Pisces.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** later Babylonian **MUL KUN.MEŠ**, “the Tails,” condensed or reorganized these neighboring figures into the zodiacal position later occupied by Pisces.

**Not documented:** a *MUL.APIN* story in which Aphrodite, Eros, or two fish are tied together.

## Late fifth century BCE: the uniform Babylonian zodiac

**Strong scholarly consensus.** Babylonian astronomers developed a 360° zodiac of twelve equal 30° signs near the end of the fifth century BCE. The sign in the Pisces position was **the Tails**. The equal signs were computational sectors, related to but not identical with visible constellation outlines. [Mathieu Ossendrijver on the uniform zodiac](https://doi.org/10.1177/00218286241233769)

The oldest preserved Babylonian horoscopes belong to this general late-fifth-century development. [Francesca Rochberg, *Babylonian Horoscopes*](https://sites.dlib.nyu.edu/viewer/books/isaw_aphs000011/1)

## Eudoxus, fourth century BCE

Eudoxus’ prose *Phaenomena* and *Enoptron* are lost but substantially reflected in Aratus. **Documented through later testimony:** his Greek sphere already included the Fishes and their cord. Exactly how much derives from Eudoxus rather than later Aratean presentation is uncertain.

## Aratus, early third century BCE

**Earliest surviving extended Greek description.** *Phaenomena* 239–247 places the two Fishes before Aries, one higher than the other; chains extend from both tails and meet at the bright Knot. Aratus supplies no myth explaining them. [Greek/English *Phaenomena*](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AratusPhaenomena.html)

## Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterisms*, Hellenistic core, surviving epitomes later

The work attributed to Eratosthenes is textually complex; the original is lost and surviving recensions are epitomes and fragments. “Eratosthenes says” should therefore often be read as “the pseudo-Eratosthenic catasterism tradition says.”

For Pisces, the tradition makes the two Fishes descendants or offspring of the **Great Fish**, Piscis Austrinus. The Great Fish saved Derceto near Bambyce/Hierapolis, explaining Syrian reverence for fish. The two Pisces are thus genealogically attached to that saving fish.

## Hipparchus, second century BCE

Hipparchus wrote a surviving commentary on Aratus and Eudoxus, criticizing inaccurate rising and setting statements. His work anchors Greek mathematical criticism of literary constellation astronomy. He did not preserve a new Pisces catasterism comparable to Hyginus.

## Dendera, approximately 50 BCE

**Documented artifact.** The circular zodiac from an Osiris chapel on the roof of Hathor’s temple depicts the zodiac, planets, Egyptian figures, and thirty-six decanal beings. Astronomical analysis of planetary and eclipse configurations places its conception around **50 BCE**, though precise interpretive details have been debated.

Pisces appears as two opposed fish connected by a cord. This is a hybrid Egyptian monumental reception of the Hellenistic zodiac. [Louvre record](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010028871)

## Manilius, early first century CE

In the Latin *Astronomica*, Pisces is a watery, paired, mute, fertile sign associated with marine livelihoods. Manilius’ chief contribution is astrological and occupational, not a full independent catasterism. At 4.273–291 and related passages, the Fishes grant affinity with the sea, fishing, navigation, and preparation of fish.

## Ovid, *Fasti*, early first century CE

At *Fasti* 2.453–474 Ovid marks the Sun’s entry into Pisces and tells how two fish carried Dione/Venus and Cupid while they fled Typhon. The fishes receive the stars as reward; Syrian abstention from fish is the cultic consequence. [Ovid, *Fasti* 2](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti2.html)

## Ptolemy, second century CE

- *Almagest* VII–VIII: star catalogue, including 38 stars in Pisces.
- *Tetrabiblos*: Pisces as a feminine, bicorporeal, watery sign; Jupiter’s house; Venus exalted; Mercury depressed/fallen; member of the watery triplicity.

Ptolemy clearly knew precession and used a tropical framework tied to equinoxes and solstices.

## Vettius Valens, second century CE

Valens’ *Anthologies* I.2 characterizes Pisces as feminine, moist, fecund, scaly, mute, mobile, bicorporeal, and internally opposed because one fish is northern and one southern. It signifies the feet and related sinews and occupations involving water. [Valens translation](https://www.skyscript.co.uk/pdf/pubs/texts/valens/griscti/docs/Valens-Anthologies.pdf)

## Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.30, probably second century CE in surviving form

Hyginus attributes to **Diognetus of Erythrae** the story that Venus and Cupid reached the Euphrates, Typhon appeared, and they leapt into the river and changed into fish. Syrians thereafter avoided eating fish lest they attack divine protectors or the gods themselves.

He adds that Eratosthenes called the pair offspring of the Great Fish. [Latin text, *Astronomica* 2.30](https://www.lateinlex.de/?call=Puc&permalink=Hyg_astr_2_30)

## Hyginus, *Fabulae* 197

A huge egg falls into the Euphrates. Fish roll it ashore; doves incubate it; the Syrian Goddess/Venus is born. Jupiter places the fish among the stars. Syrians consequently treat fish and doves as divine and do not eat them.

This is related to, but not identical with, the Typhon escape. It makes the fish rescuers of the goddess’s **egg**, not transformed gods.

## Firmicus Maternus, fourth century CE

*Mathesis* II.11.5 calls Pisces feminine, double, humid, watery, biform, fertile, scaly, spotted, bent, mute, and mobile. One fish travels south, the other north. It is Jupiter’s house; Venus has exaltation near 27°; Mercury is dejected near 15°. [Firmicus, *Mathesis* II](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/l/roman/texts/Firmicus_Maternus/Mathesis/2%2A.html)

## Nonnus, fifth century CE

Nonnus’ *Dionysiaca* I–II gives an elaborate Typhonomachy and memorably imagines Typhon dragging or attacking constellations, including Pisces. It is a late poetic expansion of the cosmic battle, not a clear independent account of Venus and Eros becoming the two Pisces. Claims that Nonnus supplies the canonical transformation story should therefore be qualified. [Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* I](https://www.theoi.com/Text/NonnusDionysiaca1.html)

## Greek, Syriac, Persian, and Arabic transmission

From late antiquity through the Abbasid translation movement, Ptolemaic astronomy and Hellenistic astrology entered Syriac and Arabic. Persian scholars participated centrally, but “Arabic astronomy” describes a language of scholarship, not a single ethnicity.

### Abū Maʿshar, ninth century

Abū Maʿshar of Balkh synthesized Greek, Iranian, and Indian materials in Arabic. His works on nativities, conjunctions, and historical cycles became exceptionally influential after Latin translation. His importance to Pisces lies less in inventing a new fish myth than in transmitting dignities, triplicities, mundane cycles, and sign doctrine. [Metropolitan Museum: medieval Islamic astronomy and astrology](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/astronomy-and-astrology-in-the-medieval-islamic-world)

### Al-Ṣūfī, 964

The Persian astronomer ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* revised Ptolemy’s catalogue, reported Arabic stellar knowledge, and illustrated every constellation twice: as seen in the sky and on a globe. Pisces appears as two fish and cords. [Qatar Museums manuscript record](https://collections.qm.org.qa/en/objects/copy-of-the-kitab-suwar-al-kawakib-al-thabita-the-book-of-fixed-stars-ms21998)

Claims that the Oxford manuscript Marsh 144 was copied by al-Ṣūfī’s son in 1009/10 depend on a colophon now suspected of later alteration. [Al-Ṣūfī manuscript census](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_manuscripts.htm)

### *Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm* / *Picatrix*, tenth–eleventh-century Arabic compilation; thirteenth-century Castilian and Latin translations

The *Picatrix* systematizes astral magic, planetary invocations, images, elections, and zodiacal correspondences. Pisces is used within electional and talismanic schemes, but many Internet lists of “the Picatrix image of Pisces” combine different recensions or later magical tables. A claim should be tied to a specific book, chapter, manuscript, and translation. [Latin/English *Picatrix* copy consulted](https://www.krasiancientastrology.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/picatrix-complete.compressed.pdf)

## Indian transmission

### *Yavanajātaka*

The work survives in a difficult Sanskrit recension traditionally attributed to Sphujidhvaja. David Pingree proposed that it versified a lost Sanskrit translation made in 149/150 CE of a Greek astrological text. Both the dating and degree of direct dependence have been challenged in later scholarship, so “the first Sanskrit horoscope book of 149 CE” is too categorical.

Mīna is nevertheless fully integrated into Indian horoscopy: two-bodied, watery, and ruled by Jupiter. [Digital Sanskrit/English *Yavanajātaka*](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yavanajataka-by-sphujidhvaja-sanskrit/d/doc1501212.html)

### Varāhamihira, sixth century

Varāhamihira’s *Bṛhajjātaka*, *Bṛhatsaṃhitā*, and *Pañcasiddhāntikā* display a mature synthesis of Indian and “Yavana” sciences. Mīna is a recognized rāśi; Venus’ exaltation there is listed in the inherited planetary-dignity system.

## Latin translations of the twelfth century

Arabic versions and commentaries on Ptolemy and astrological works by Abū Maʿshar entered Latin through translation centers including Toledo and Barcelona. Plato of Tivoli translated the *Tetrabiblos* from Arabic in 1138. Pisces consequently re-entered Latin Europe through both surviving Latin classical materials and Arabic technical astronomy.

## Dante, early fourteenth century

Dante uses Pisces astronomically as a time marker. At *Inferno* XI.112–115, Pisces is rising before Aries and the Wain lies northwest, indicating the approach of dawn. This is observational-poetic use, not a claim that Dante was a Pisces or that the sign governed the *Comedy*.

## *Très Riches Heures*, c. 1412–1416 and later completion

The March miniature shows the solar chariot passing from Pisces into Aries in its zodiacal arc. The famous Zodiac Man associates Pisces with the feet. [March folio](https://www.moleiro.com/en/books-of-hours/the-trs-riches-heures-du-duc-de-berry/miniatura/4b7abef23e660)

## Palazzo Schifanoia, Ferrara, 1468–1470

The Hall of the Months combined planetary triumphs, zodiacal signs, decans, and Este court life. The preserved March panel centers on Aries; Pisces belongs to the damaged/lost February program rather than the celebrated surviving March fish image sometimes alleged online. Surviving “Pisces decans” must be identified cautiously because much of the February field is damaged.

## Dürer’s celestial maps, 1515

Albrecht Dürer, working with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, produced printed northern and southern celestial hemisphere maps. The northern map shows Pisces as two fish connected by a ribbon and knot, synthesized from classical, Arabic-Latin, and Renaissance cartographic traditions. The British Museum dates the companion maps to 1515. [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P_E-2-416)

## Agrippa, 1531/1533

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* incorporated planetary, zodiacal, elemental, bodily, magical, and cabalistic correspondences. Later occult lists often cite “Agrippa” loosely; exact Pisces talismans must be checked against the Latin edition because nineteenth-century occultists expanded his tables.

## Schiller, 1627

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, with assistance from Bayer and engravings by Lucas Kilian, replaced pagan constellations with Christian figures. Pisces became **Saint Matthias the Apostle**, *Sancti Matthiæ Apostoli*. It was one part of Schiller’s systematic replacement of the zodiac by the Twelve Apostles; the scheme did not displace conventional astronomy. [Library of Congress digitization](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/)

## Kepler

**Documented general context; Pisces-specific restraint.** Johannes Kepler cast horoscopes, issued prognostications, accepted some celestial-terrestrial correspondences, and criticized what he regarded as vulgar sign astrology. No distinctive, historically influential “Kepler doctrine of Pisces” emerged. Modern quotations making Kepler endorse twentieth-century Piscean personality stereotypes are generally decontextualized or unverified.

## Seventeenth–nineteenth-century atlases

Flamsteed and Bode retained the fishes as pictorial conventions while improving stellar positions. During the Enlightenment, astronomy increasingly separated predictive celestial mechanics from judicial astrology, though almanac astrology survived widely.

## Theosophy, occult revival, and twentieth-century astrology

Nineteenth-century occultism linked zodiacal signs to spiritual evolution, colors, rays, tarot, alchemy, and world ages. Alan Leo and other popular astrologers shifted emphasis from concrete event prediction toward personality and spiritual development. Neptune, discovered in 1846, was gradually assigned modern rulership of Pisces.

In twentieth-century psychological astrology, Pisces became characterologically associated with imagination, sympathy, mysticism, permeability, escapism, sacrifice, addiction, and artistic receptivity. These are layered modern developments, not a single doctrine found unchanged in Ptolemy.

Newspaper Sun-sign columns simplified astrology further: “Pisces” came to mean anyone born while the tropical Sun occupied the sign, whereas ancient practice judged an entire chart and often gave the Ascendant greater descriptive weight.

# Myths and legends by culture

## Mesopotamia: the Swallow, Anunitum, and the Tails

**Documented astral tradition; narrative reconstruction uncertain.** The Pisces region joined portions of the Great Swallow and Anunitum. Anunitum was a goddess associated with Akkad and often understood as a form or epithet of Ištar. The “Tails” lay in the lunar path.

No surviving Babylonian myth states that these objects were Aphrodite and Eros, that two fish escaped Typhon, or that a cord was tied so they would not lose one another. The attractive theory that the Greek cord visually preserves the merger of two Babylonian star figures is a **scholarly hypothesis**, not a cuneiform explanation.

## Derceto/Atargatis and the saving Great Fish

**Myth; Hellenistic synthesis of Syrian cult and Greek catasterism.** Ctesias, as transmitted by Diodorus Siculus 2.4.2–5, told of Derceto at Ashkelon: ashamed after a love affair and childbirth, she threw herself into a lake and became partly fish-shaped. Her daughter Semiramis was exposed and fed by doves.

The pseudo-Eratosthenic *Catasterisms* connects the **Great Fish**—more properly Piscis Austrinus—with the fish that saved Derceto near Bambyce. Pisces’ two fish are its offspring. This is not quite the same as saying Derceto herself is Pisces.

Later Greek and Roman writers identified Derceto with **Atargatis/Dea Syria**, and often with Aphrodite. The identifications are historically real but do not prove all names originally denoted one goddess.

## Aphrodite and Eros transform into fish

**Myth, first securely preserved in Latin mythography.** Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.30, citing Diognetus of Erythrae, says Venus and Cupid encountered Typhon by the Euphrates, leapt into the river, and changed themselves into fish. Their escape explains Syrian abstinence from fish.

Who placed them in the sky is not explicitly elaborated in the surviving Hyginus passage in the same manner as some other catasterisms; later summaries commonly assign the act to the gods, Zeus, or Aphrodite. Those additions should not be silently placed in Hyginus’ mouth.

## Two fish rescue Aphrodite and Eros

**Myth, distinct variant.** Ovid, *Fasti* 2.453–474, makes the fishes external rescuers. Dione/Venus, carrying Cupid, invokes the nymphs and leaps into the Euphrates; two fish receive the gods on their backs. They are rewarded with stellar immortality. Syrian refusal to eat fish commemorates the rescue.

This is the clearest ancient source for the popular variant “two fish carried them to safety.”

## The cord that prevents separation

**Later explanatory tradition.** Ancient astronomy unquestionably depicts cords joining the fishes. However, the sentimental story that Aphrodite tied Eros to herself so they would not be separated in the river is not explicit in Aratus, Ovid, or Hyginus’ surviving Pisces chapters. It is widespread in modern retellings and may elaborate the visual cord, but its ancient source could not be securely traced.

## The Euphrates egg and the Syrian Goddess

**Myth.** Hyginus, *Fabulae* 197, says a marvelous egg fell into the Euphrates; fish rolled it ashore; doves incubated it; Venus, later called the Syrian Goddess, hatched from it. Jupiter placed the fish in the stars, and Syrians avoided eating fish and doves.

Scholia to Germanicus’ Aratus preserve related material. This version explains both sacred fish and sacred doves and belongs to the wider Atargatis/Astarte complex.

The constellation rewarded may be interpreted as Pisces, though the ancient Syrian fish traditions also intersect with Piscis Austrinus. Modern retellings often erase this ambiguity.

## Syrian fish taboo

**Documented ancient ethnographic tradition, reasons mythologized.** Fish abstention or restrictions connected with Atargatis are reported in several Greco-Roman sources, including Athenaeus and Lucian’s *De Dea Syria*. Practices differed by place and cult; “all Syrians never ate any fish” is an exaggeration generated by universalizing local ritual.

The constellation myths function as etiologies for an existing sacred-food practice.

## Typhon and the flight of the gods

**Wider mythic context.** Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.6.3, tells how the gods fled Typhon to Egypt and assumed animal shapes. It does not specifically establish the Pisces catasterism. Antoninus Liberalis 28, from Nicander, also describes divine transformations. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 5.321–331, has the Muse narrate how the gods hid in Egypt in animal forms; Venus became a fish. Again, this is a general divine-flight tradition rather than the detailed two-Pisces story of the *Fasti*.

Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* I–II, vastly expands Typhon’s assault on heaven. Pisces is threatened within the cosmic disorder, but Nonnus should not be cited as the earliest source for Venus-and-Cupid Pisces.

## Isis and the fish

A late or secondary tradition states that a fish saved Isis in childbirth and was placed in heaven with its young. This appears in modern constellation literature attributed to “Eratosthenes, Catasterism 38,” but Catasterism 38 concerns the Southern Fish, and the transmission is unstable. It may represent a syncretic recasting of Derceto/Atargatis as Isis.

**Status: disputed attribution.** It should not be presented as an uncontested pharaonic Egyptian myth of Pisces.

## What Eratosthenes does—and does not—say

- The surviving pseudo-Eratosthenic tradition makes the Pisces pair offspring of the Great Fish.
- The Great Fish is connected with Derceto’s rescue.
- The text is not the autograph of Eratosthenes.
- It does not supply the familiar full scene of Aphrodite tying herself to Eros.
- “Eratosthenes said Pisces was Venus and Cupid transformed” is therefore at best a conflation with Hyginus.

## What the scholia add

The scholia to Aratus and Germanicus preserve overlapping explanations: Derceto, the saving fish, the Syrian egg, Venus, and sacred-fish abstention. Because scholia accumulated over centuries and vary between manuscripts, “the scholia say” is not a single dated authorial statement. Each scholion ideally requires manuscript and recension identification.

## Pausanias

Pausanias mentions Syrian cults, Aphrodite, and local fish traditions in several geographical contexts, but no independent Pausanian Pisces catasterism comparable to Ovid or Hyginus was found. Modern compilations that list Pausanias among “sources for the Pisces myth” often cite him only for associated cult geography.

## Pseudo-Apollodorus

The *Bibliotheca* provides the Typhon battle and the gods’ flight but not a full Pisces placement narrative. It is relevant background, not evidence for every detail of the constellation legend.

## Manilius

Manilius develops sign doctrine and marine occupations rather than narrating the constellation’s origin. Lists claiming that he tells the egg-of-Atargatis story commonly confuse him with Hyginus or later commentary.

## Christian fish traditions

### ΙΧΘΥΣ

**Documented Christian symbol; astrological connection modern or speculative.** Greek **ἰχθύς**, “fish,” became an acrostic for *Iēsous Christos Theou Huios Sōtēr*—“Jesus Christ, Son of God, Savior.” Fish appear in early Christian art and literature through:

- disciples who were fishermen;
- “fishers of men” (Mark 1:17; Matthew 4:19);
- multiplication of loaves and fishes;
- baptismal symbolism;
- eucharistic meals;
- the acrostic itself.

No early Christian source securely says the fish was adopted because the equinox had entered Pisces.

### The 153 fish

John 21:11 gives a catch of **153 large fish**. Patristic writers developed mathematical and ecclesial interpretations, notably Augustine’s triangular-number reading in *Tractates on John* 122. The idea that 153 secretly encodes the astrological Age of Pisces is **modern esoteric interpretation**, not the Gospel’s stated meaning.

### Vesica piscis

The *vesica piscis* is the almond-shaped intersection of two circles, important in geometry and later art-historical terminology. It resembles a fish or mandorla and frames Christ or Mary in medieval art.

The assertion that the Pisces glyph derives from the vesica, or that every Christian mandorla secretly depicts Pisces, is **unverified**. The motifs can be compared symbolically, but their formal genealogies are different.

### Christ as ruler of the Piscean Age

This is a **modern astrological-historical doctrine**, developed especially in nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultism and Jungian reception. Early Christian fish symbolism is authentic; its dependence on zodiacal Pisces is not demonstrated.

## Jewish and Kabbalistic tradition

### Tribe

Later Jewish zodiac systems correlate the twelve signs/months with tribes. Pisces/Adar is assigned differently in competing schemes, often to **Joseph**, **Ephraim and Manasseh**, or **Naphtali**. There is no universally authoritative biblical assignment.

### Letter

The *Sefer Yetzirah* recension tradition differs. In a common short-recension arrangement, Pisces/Adar is formed through **qoph (ק)** and associated with laughter or mirth; some later tables give **tzaddi (צ)** and a foot. The disagreement reflects variant texts and later diagrammatic systems, not a single unchanged “ancient Kabbalah” table. A consulted translation assigns Pisces, Adar, and the left foot within its own recension. [*Sefer Yetzirah* translation consulted](https://www.onesaint.org/uploads/1/1/9/4/11942290/sefer_yetzirah1.pdf)

### Adar fish symbolism

**Tradition.** Dagim suits Adar because fish are hidden beneath water and prolific; later homiletic interpretations connect this with concealed fortune, Purim, fertility, and protection from the evil eye. These are Jewish exegetical developments, not Babylonian explanations of the constellation.

## Christianized constellation: Saint Matthias

**Documented early-modern invention.** Schiller assigned Pisces to Matthias because his apostolic zodiac needed twelve faithful apostles, excluding Judas Iscariot and including his replacement. This was a deliberate Christian remapping, not an ancient hagiographic belief that Matthias became two fish.

## Folklore, calendars, and weather

Medieval European calendars paired Pisces with February or early March and with rain, pruning, warming by the fire, plowing, or late-winter food. Isidore of Seville explained the February association through wet weather. “February fill-dyke” continues this seasonal watery character. [Metropolitan Museum medieval Pisces](https://blog.metmuseum.org/cloistersgardens/2009/02/06/february-fill-dyke/book_bottom_200/)

## Alchemy

**Later occult correspondence, not a universal alchemical canon.** Modern Hermetic and occult tables often give Pisces:

- **dissolution** or *solutio*;
- water;
- return of differentiated matter to a fluid matrix;
- occasionally projection, fermentation, or multiplication, depending on the twelve-stage scheme.

Classical alchemical texts did not maintain one standard zodiac-operation table. Assigning Pisces uniquely to “dissolution” is principally a modern occult synthesis, sometimes retroactively attributed to Paracelsus or Agrippa without a precise citation.

# Doctrine and uses

## Classical qualities

Pisces is traditionally:

- feminine;
- nocturnal;
- watery;
- cold and moist;
- mutable or common;
- bicorporeal/double-bodied;
- mute or voiceless;
- fecund;
- bestial;
- associated with the north/south opposition of its two fish.

“Mutable” is the modern English equivalent of Greek/Latin concepts of common or double-bodied signs. Babylonian precursors to quadruplicity doctrine existed, but the standard cardinal/fixed/mutable scheme is Hellenistic in its familiar form.

## Planetary rulership

- **Classical domicile:** Jupiter.
- **Detriment:** Mercury, by opposition to Virgo.
- **Exaltation:** Venus, conventionally at 27° Pisces.
- **Fall/depression:** Mercury, conventionally at 15° Pisces in several late-antique tables.
- **Modern ruler:** Neptune.
- **Hybrid modern practice:** Jupiter as traditional ruler, Neptune as modern ruler or co-ruler.

Neptune’s rulership is necessarily post-1846. Claims that ancient priests knew Neptune ruled Pisces are **modern invention** unless offered as explicitly occult revelation rather than history.

Ptolemy explains Venus’ exaltation through her moist nature and the moist beginning of spring. [Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos*](https://www.astroccult.net/tetrabiblos_by_claudius_ptolemy.pdf)

## Element and modality

- Element: **water**
- Modality: **mutable/common**
- Triplicity: Cancer–Scorpio–Pisces

Triplicity rulers vary by system:

- Dorothean/later medieval: Venus by day, Mars by night, Moon participating.
- Ptolemy: Mars and Venus associated with the watery triplicity through geographical and climatic logic.
- Modern simplified astrology often suppresses participating rulers.

## Decans/faces

In the common Chaldean planetary order, Pisces’ decans are:

- 0°–10°: **Saturn**
- 10°–20°: **Jupiter**
- 20°–30°: **Mars**

Other systems assign sign-based “sub-rulers,” producing Neptune, Moon, and Mars or Jupiter, Moon, and Mars. Those are modern decan schemes and should not be confused with the Hellenistic faces.

Egyptian decans were originally star groups and calendar/timekeeping units. The later astrological division of every sign into three ten-degree faces incorporated Egyptian material but is not identical to the earlier coffin-clock system.

## Bounds or terms

Two widely transmitted systems differ.

### Egyptian bounds

A common late-antique table gives Pisces:

- 0°–12° Venus
- 12°–16° Jupiter
- 16°–19° Mercury
- 19°–28° Mars
- 28°–30° Saturn

### Ptolemaic bounds

Common modern editions give:

- 0°–8° Venus
- 8°–14° Jupiter
- 14°–20° Mercury
- 20°–26° Mars
- 26°–30° Saturn

Manuscripts and printed tables sometimes vary by a degree. Ptolemy himself criticizes the rationale of inherited “Egyptian” terms and says he found a more defensible ancient table.

## Anatomy and medical astrology

Pisces governs:

- the feet;
- toes;
- in some texts, sinews and bodily extremities;
- by sympathy, conditions involving moisture, swelling, or difficulty in walking.

In the zodiacal man, Aries begins at the head and Pisces completes the sequence at the feet. Medieval and Renaissance physicians used planetary and lunar positions in selecting times for bloodletting, surgery, purging, and medication.

**Historical fact:** these practices were real.  
**Scientific status:** their causal efficacy is not established by modern medicine.

## Agriculture, weather, and elections

Pisces was treated as moist and fertile, hence favorable in many almanacs for:

- watering;
- planting moisture-loving crops;
- fishing;
- working near water;
- sometimes pruning or grafting.

Electional rules are not uniform. A fertile sign could favor sowing but a mutable or watery Moon could be judged poor for permanence, travel, or dry storage. Medieval Arabic and Latin manuals assessed the Moon, rulers, aspects, and local horizon—not the sign alone.

## Characterology

### Ancient

Valens and Firmicus emphasize physical constitution, fertility, water occupations, mutability, internal conflict, and possible bodily afflictions. Manilius stresses seafaring and fishing skills. Ancient texts do not give the modern list “empathetic, dreamy, psychic, artistic” in that form.

### Medieval and Renaissance

Character judgments combined sign, ascendant, planetary lord, temperament, and physiognomy. Pisces could indicate quietness because it was mute, fertility because it contained two fish, instability because they moved in contrary directions, or maritime professions.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries

The sign acquired spiritualized and psychological qualities:

- compassion and self-sacrifice;
- imagination and mediumship;
- artistic sensitivity;
- weak boundaries;
- deception or escapism;
- addiction, martyrdom, or withdrawal.

These meanings were promoted through occultism, Alan Leo-style character astrology, Theosophy, Jungian symbolism, and mass-market Sun-sign books.

### Present

Contemporary practice is plural:

- traditional astrologers foreground Jupiter and sect-based dignity;
- modern astrologers emphasize Neptune and psychology;
- evolutionary astrologers stress karmic completion;
- sidereal Western astrologers relocate sign positions;
- Indian jyotiṣa retains Jupiter as ruler of Mīna;
- popular social media produces February/March and “three types of Pisces” stereotypes based loosely on decans.

# Iconography

## Dendera zodiac, c. 50 BCE, Dendera; now Louvre, Paris

Two fish face opposite directions and are linked. The scene belongs to an Egyptian sacred ceiling that combines Egyptian decans and deities with the imported zodiac. It is the earliest securely dated monumental Pisces image treated here.

## Greco-Roman gems, mosaics, coins, and reliefs

Pisces appears as paired fish, often opposed or connected. On zodiac mosaics and coins it can indicate a month, planetary domicile, civic horoscope, or cosmic order. Individual coin claims require numismatic identification; two fish alone may instead refer to a harbor, river, deity, or local emblem.

## Aratea manuscripts, ninth–fifteenth centuries

Illustrated copies of Aratus and Latin translations show the two fish and their cords, but orientation varies. Some attach the cord to tails, others to mouths. This reflects copying, artistic convenience, and ambiguity in textual transmission. [Study of illustrated Aratea](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/downloads/hs2/Aratus/Dolan%20-%20Astronomical%20Knowledge%20Transmission%20Through%20Illustrated%20Aratea%20Manuscripts%20%282017%29.pdf)

## Bamberg Star Mantle, early eleventh century

The *Sternenmantel* associated with Henry II includes Pisces, with the surviving title **PISCES**. [Bavarikon object record](https://www.bavarikon.de/object/bav:DMB-KAI-000000000STM0068)

## Cathedral portals

- **Chartres, early thirteenth century:** zodiac and labors of the months appear in portal sculpture and glazing.
- **Amiens, c. 1220–1230:** the Saint-Firmin portal’s quatrefoils pair zodiac signs with agricultural labors; Pisces is two fish. [Amiens zodiac](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiaque_de_la_cath%C3%A9drale_d%27Amiens)
- Comparable cycles occur at Vézelay, Autun, Parma, Modena, and elsewhere, although survival and sign-month ordering differ.

Their presence in churches signifies the divinely ordered calendar and cosmos; it does not by itself imply ecclesiastical endorsement of natal determinism.

## Medieval almanac, England, c. 1383–1400

A folded parchment almanac shows the two fish facing opposite ways, joined at their mouths by a green line. [Royal Society collection](https://pictures.royalsociety.org/image-rs-21063)

## *Très Riches Heures*, Chantilly, c. 1412–1416

The calendar arcs show the Sun’s movement through Pisces and Aries; the Zodiac Man places Pisces at the feet.

## Morgan MS M.785, Bruges, by 1403

Two fish face opposite directions with mouths attached by a cord, explicitly inscribed **PISCES**. [Morgan Library manuscript](https://ica.themorgan.org/manuscript/page/46/144038)

## Palazzo Schifanoia, Ferrara, 1468–1470

Its month cycle united planets, signs, decans, and courtly labors. Pisces’ February sector is not among the best-preserved panels; reconstructions must not substitute surviving Aries imagery for Pisces.

## Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

The northern celestial map depicts a ribboned pair of fish in the star-map tradition. Its significance lies in printing a learned synthesis for wide circulation.

## Regiomontanus tradition, Strasbourg edition of 1528

A printed astrological work shows the Pisces glyph together with two fish in water. [Yale Medical Astrology exhibition](https://onlineexhibits.library.yale.edu/s/medicalastrology/item/15295)

## Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

*Uranometria* combines more accurate stellar plotting with a traditional, strongly drawn pair of fishes and cords.

## Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

Pisces’ stars are redrawn as Saint Matthias. The plates appear reversed because the atlas presents globe orientation. [ETH digitization](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720)

## Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Pisces is rendered naturalistically in the grand baroque globe-view atlas, with star positions superimposed upon the fish bodies and cords.

## Flamsteed, London, 1729

The pair remains pictorial, but observational cataloguing increasingly dominates over mythographic anatomy.

## Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s *Uranographia* supplies the archetypal Enlightenment plate: two sizable fish, long knotted ribbons, and numerous telescopic stars. It demonstrates that scientific precision and elaborate allegorical drawing still coexisted at the beginning of the nineteenth century.

## Modern imagery

Modern horoscope art often turns the pair into:

- koi arranged like yin-yang;
- mermaids or human-fish hybrids;
- a vesica or infinity sign;
- two souls or lovers;
- Neptune-themed ocean fantasy.

Koi and yin-yang Pisces are **modern cross-cultural designs**, not classical Greek iconography.

# People, events, and works

## Births conventionally claimed as Pisces

The following are **tropical Sun-sign classifications**, not ancient “nativities,” and boundary cases require exact time and ephemeris:

- Michelangelo, born 6 March 1475.
- Frédéric Chopin, birth traditionally recorded as 1 March 1810, though a family record gives 22 February.
- Victor Hugo, 26 February 1802.
- Pierre-Auguste Renoir, 25 February 1841.
- Albert Einstein, 14 March 1879.
- George Washington, 22 February 1732 New Style; his Old Style birth date was 11 February.
- Nina Simone, 21 February 1933.
- Gabriel García Márquez, 6 March 1927.
- Rihanna, 20 February 1988.

Their fame does not validate sign characterology; their inclusion documents modern cultural attachment.

## Political and military astrology

Ancient and medieval rulers timed undertakings astrologically, but no securely sourced major battle or foundation was found whose decisive elected condition was specifically “Pisces” alone. Claims that Christianity, Rome, Jerusalem, or the United States was “founded under Pisces” generally rely on selecting one retrospective chart from several possible dates.

## Dante

Pisces functions as a dawn clock in *Inferno* XI. It demonstrates practical literary knowledge of stellar risings.

## Schiller’s Saint Matthias

The apostle’s replacement of Pisces is the most explicit Christian work attached to the constellation as a whole.

## Jung’s *Aion* (1951)

Jung made the two opposed fishes a major image of Christ/Antichrist polarity and the psychological history of the Christian aeon. This profoundly influenced later esoteric readings of Pisces.

## Popular music and modern culture

“Aquarian Age” culture routinely defines its predecessor as Piscean. The musical *Hair* (1967) popularized the transition formula. Numerous musical works and songs titled “Pisces” use the sign as a metaphor for sensitivity, duality, or water; these are independent modern receptions rather than continuations of one ancient myth.

## Astronomical objects

The IAU constellation contains:

- **M74**, a nearly face-on spiral galaxy known popularly as the Phantom Galaxy;
- numerous fainter NGC galaxies;
- exoplanet-host stars;
- the March-equinox point.

These objects belong to Pisces only under modern boundary convention and had no role in the ancient picture.

# Controversies and disputes

## Was Babylonian Pisces already two fish?

**Disputed; usually overstated.** The relevant Babylonian sign was the **Tails**, derived from the Swallow/Anunitum region. A fish element may have existed in adjacent imagery, but the exact Greek pair cannot simply be read back into *MUL.APIN*. “Pisces is the oldest zodiac figure unchanged for 4,000 years” is false as stated.

## Did Pisces originate in Egypt?

**Unsupported in strong form.** Egyptian decans are ancient, but the twelve-sign zodiac appears in Egypt after Hellenistic contact. Dendera proves Egyptian adoption, not pharaonic invention of Pisces.

Claims that Pisces occurs on a Middle Kingdom coffin lid require object number, photograph, and accepted Egyptological identification. No adequate evidence for such a claim was verified here.

## Dendera’s age

Nineteenth-century polemics sometimes dated Dendera thousands or tens of thousands of years earlier than classical civilization, making it evidence against biblical chronology. Champollion and subsequent Egyptology placed it in the Greco-Roman period; astronomical work supports approximately 50 BCE.

The artifact became famous partly through this modern science-and-religion controversy.

## Was the vernal point in Pisces at Christ’s birth?

**Approximately, but boundary-dependent.** Precession placed the equinox in the traditional Pisces region around the late Hellenistic/early Roman period. Ancient figures had no official borders, so a precise year cannot be recovered objectively.

“Exactly 1 CE” is a symbolic convention, not an astronomical measurement.

## Is Christianity secretly Piscean astrology?

**Disputed modern interpretation.**

Evidence for comparison:

- early Christian fish symbolism;
- first disciples as fishermen;
- fish miracles;
- approximate coincidence with the equinox’s movement into Pisces;
- later esoteric and Jungian interpretations.

Evidence against historical derivation:

- early Christians do not explain the fish through Pisces;
- Jewish and Mediterranean fish symbolism had independent sources;
- the date of an astrological age is elastic;
- no ancient Christian council or theologian establishes a Piscean Age doctrine.

The responsible conclusion is that the parallel is historically influential in modern esotericism but not proven as Christianity’s origin.

## The 153 fish as zodiac code

**Modern speculative numerology.** Ancient Christian authors interpreted 153 mathematically and ecclesiologically, but no early evidence securely connects it with Pisces or a 2,160-year age.

## The vesica piscis

**Terminological trap.** The Latin phrase means “fish’s bladder.” The geometric lens is real and important in sacred art. Deriving ♓ or Christian fish symbolism directly from it is not demonstrated.

## “NASA changed the zodiac”

**Modern hoax/misreporting.** NASA and astronomers did not change astrological signs. Viral stories confused:

- twelve equal astrological signs;
- thirteen IAU constellations crossed by the ecliptic;
- precession;
- modern constellation boundaries.

The Sun has always crossed the region now called Ophiuchus. The IAU formalized borders; it did not invent a new tropical sign.

## Ophiuchus as the thirteenth sign

**Category dispute.** Ophiuchus is an ecliptic constellation but not one of the twelve traditional equal signs. If astrology is defined by IAU constellations, it must confront thirteen unequal regions. If it is a twelvefold longitude system, Ophiuchus is irrelevant as a “sign.”

Steven Schmidt’s *Astrology 14* (1970) proposed adding both Ophiuchus and Cetus. The widespread “thirteenth sign” story suppresses this fourteen-sign origin and falsely attributes the change to NASA.

## The “Age of Aries” and “Age of Pisces”

The sequence follows precession backward: Taurus → Aries → Pisces → Aquarius. But dates depend on:

- equal 30° sectors versus visible constellation widths;
- which fiducial star or boundary is chosen;
- whether ages begin at a boundary, center, or symbolic epoch;
- which ayanāṃśa is used.

Therefore “Age of Aries 2000 BCE–1 CE, Pisces 1–2000 CE” is a convenient esoteric table, not an exact historical chronology.

## Proper-name etymologies

- **Alrescha** from Arabic “cord” is secure.
- **Fumalsamakah** from “mouth of the fish” is secure in broad form.
- **Alpherg** has variant explanations and complicated manuscript transmission.
- **Kullat Nūnu** is especially uncertain: *nūnu* is “fish,” while *kullat* has been explained as bucket, cord, or another object. Confident Internet translations should be treated cautiously.
- **Torcular** is Latin “winepress,” but its precise route from manuscript description to a proper star name requires more documentation than popular lists usually provide.

## Is α Piscium the brightest?

No. Bayer letters are not a strict magnitude ranking. η Piscium/Alpherg is brighter. Calling Alrescha “the brightest star of Pisces” confuses its pictorial importance as the Knot with brightness.

## Which stars form the Circlet?

There is no official answer. Modern observers differ over whether the oval contains five, six, or more stars and whether TX Piscium belongs. It is an informal asterism.

## Fish tied at mouths or tails?

Aratus describes chains from the tails. Many medieval manuscripts connect the mouths. This is a genuine iconographic variation, probably owing to copying and composition rather than a separate classical myth.

## Venus and Cupid: transformed or carried?

Both are ancient:

- Hyginus’ Diognetus tradition: they **become fish**.
- Ovid: two fish **carry them**.
- Later retellings often combine the variants.

## Atargatis, Derceto, Astarte, Aphrodite, and “Syrian Venus”

These figures were historically syncretized, but they are not automatically interchangeable in every time and place. Derceto’s fish body, Atargatis’ cult, Astarte’s West Semitic history, and Greek Aphrodite’s Typhon story must be separated before tracing their fusion.

## Fabricated antiquity

The following recurrent claims were not verified in primary ancient sources:

- Pisces was originally two dolphins that survived Atlantis.
- The glyph records the Tigris and Euphrates.
- Jesus was born “under Pisces”; the traditional December birth date would not give a tropical Pisces Sun, and the historical date is unknown.
- Early Christians selected 12 apostles to imitate the zodiac.
- The Vatican deliberately suppressed Ophiuchus.
- Sumerians taught an “Age of Pisces” beginning with Jesus.
- Pisces was assigned Neptune in Babylon.
- Koi were the classical fishes.
- The Pisces cord originally signified DNA.
- Dendera depicts the sky of 10,500 BCE.
- Every fish-bodied Near Eastern deity is an early Pisces.

These are **modern inventions, speculative comparisons, or unverified claims**, unless a specific evidentiary chain is supplied.

# Open questions

1. **Transformation from Tails to Fishes.** How exactly did the Babylonian Swallow–Anunitum tail complex become the Greek two-fish figure? Positional continuity is strong; narrative and iconographic continuity remain incompletely documented.

2. **Diognetus of Erythrae.** Hyginus preserves his name, but the author’s date, work, and exact cultural sources are poorly known. If genuinely early Hellenistic, his testimony could push the Typhon variant back substantially.

3. **Pseudo-Eratosthenic recensions.** The relationship between the original Hellenistic work, the Epitome, the *Fragmenta Vaticana*, and later scholia complicates “first attestation” claims.

4. **Syrian cult practice.** Ancient writers generalize. Archaeological and local textual evidence may distinguish temple-specific abstinence from regional dietary practice.

5. **Egyptian identification.** The proper Egyptian reading of every nearby Dendera figure and decan remains specialized and sometimes disputed; the Greek Pisces figure is clearer than claims of a native antecedent.

6. **Glyph history.** A complete palaeographic history requires systematic comparison of dated Greek horoscope papyri, Byzantine manuscripts, Arabic tables, medieval Latin manuscripts, and early typefaces. Popular symbol dictionaries rarely do this.

7. **Alpherg/Kullat Nūnu.** The philology and chain of assignment among η Piscium, Babylonian terminology, Arabic manuscripts, and modern IAU naming deserve a dedicated source study.

8. **Piscean Age genealogy.** The path from eighteenth-century precessional religion theories through nineteenth-century occultism to Heindel, Steiner, Bailey, Jung, and popular New Age culture is clearer in outline than in every textual link.

9. **Alchemy.** No universal premodern list equating Pisces with one operation was found. A reliable history would collate specific manuscripts rather than merge modern Golden Dawn, Rosicrucian, Paracelsian, and Jungian tables.

10. **Battles and foundations.** No major event securely documented as deliberately timed specifically to Pisces was identified. General astrological election is abundant; Pisces-specific claims require further archival work.
