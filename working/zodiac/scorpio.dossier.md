# Scorpius: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — directly attested by a primary text, object, catalogue, or official astronomical record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern historical inference from surviving evidence.
- **Tradition** — a durable attribution within astrology, religion, or folklore, whether or not empirically demonstrable.
- **Disputed claim** — supported by competing interpretations or uncertain textual evidence.
- **Myth / legend** — a narrative transmitted as mythic history.
- **Modern invention** — a recent claim advertised as ancient without ancient documentation.
- **Unverified claim** — a claim encountered in later literature but not traced here to the alleged source.

“Scorpio” below normally means the **thirty-degree astrological sign**. “Scorpius” means the **constellation defined by stars or, after 1930, by an IAU region of sky**. Ancient sources did not always maintain that distinction.

# Basic identification

## Sign and constellation

**Documented fact.** Scorpius is one of the 88 modern constellations and one of Ptolemy’s 48. Its official IAU abbreviation is **Sco** and its Latin nominative is **Scorpius**, genitive **Scorpii**. It lies chiefly in the southern celestial hemisphere, between Libra and Sagittarius, with Ophiuchus extending southward across the ecliptic immediately north and east of it.

**Documented fact.** Scorpio, the astrological sign, is the eighth of twelve equal thirty-degree divisions of ecliptic longitude:

- Tropical Scorpio: **210°–240°** from the tropical vernal point.
- Conventional modern dates: approximately **23 October–21/22 November**; ingress times vary by year and time zone.
- Common Lahiri-style sidereal dates: approximately **16 November–15 December**, varying with ayanāṃśa and year.
- Other sidereal systems use different zero points and may differ by a day or more.

**Documented astronomical fact.** Under present IAU boundaries the Sun is physically within Scorpius for only about a week, approximately **23–29 November**, before entering Ophiuchus. Exact instants change slightly with year and with the adopted apparent/geometric solar position.

This short transit has no power to shorten the tropical sign: tropical Scorpio remains exactly thirty degrees because signs and constellations are different kinds of object.

## Names

### Mesopotamian

- **Sumerian/logographic:** **MUL.GÍR.TAB** or normalized **MUL.GIR₂.TAB**, “the Scorpion.”
- **Akkadian:** **zuqaqīpu**, “scorpion,” with the constellation also written by the inherited Sumerogram.
- **Babylonian zodiacal sign:** normally represented by **GÍR.TAB** in astronomical texts.

**Documented fact.** The scorpion was already a named Mesopotamian constellation before the equal-sign zodiac was created. It belonged to the longer sequence of ecliptic constellations preserved in *MUL.APIN*.

**Caution.** Internet claims that *GÍR.TAB* literally means “the creature with a burning sting” or “the cutter” are speculative folk etymologies. It is the conventional logographic spelling of “scorpion”; the sign values should not be freely rearranged into an esoteric sentence.

### Egyptian

Egyptian names for the ordinary scorpion included:

- **wḥꜥt**
- **ḏꜣrt**, especially relevant in later Egyptian and Demotic usage.
- The goddess **Srḳt / Serqet**, Greek **Selkis**, bears a scorpion emblem.

**Documented fact.** Egypt had indigenous scorpion cults and scorpion deities many centuries before it adopted the twelve-sign zodiac.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The recognizable Scorpio on the Dendera zodiacs belongs to the imported Babylonian–Greek zodiacal scheme, translated into an Egyptian religious environment. It should not be assumed that the classical zodiacal Scorpion originated independently from Serqet.

### Greek and Latin

- Greek: **Σκορπίος, Skorpíos**, “scorpion.”
- Greek alternative for the enlarged figure: **Σκορπίος** together with **Χηλαί, Chēlaí**, “Claws.”
- Latin: **Scorpius**, **Scorpio**; occasionally **Scorpios** in transliteration.
- Latin for the claws: **Chelae**.
- Medieval Latin retained *Scorpio/Scorpius*, often alongside *Scorpii*, *Scorpionis*, or explanatory *scorpio caelestis*.

The ultimate ancestry of Greek *skorpíos* is uncertain. Direct descent from Akkadian *zuqaqīpu* is not linguistically demonstrated; similarities of meaning show cultural transmission of the figure more securely than transmission of the word.

### Arabic and Persian

- Arabic: **العقرب, al-ʿAqrab**, “the Scorpion.”
- Full constellation caption in al-Ṣūfī: **ṣūrat al-ʿaqrab**, “the image/constellation of the Scorpion.”
- Persian: **کژدم, každom** or **عقرب, ʿAqrab**; the Arabic loan became standard in Persian astronomical and astrological usage.
- Medieval Latin translations produced forms such as **Alacrab**, **Alacrap**, and **Acrab** from Arabic *al-ʿaqrab*.

Spanish preserves two useful lexical histories:

- Zodiacal sign: **Escorpio**.
- Animal: **escorpión** or **alacrán**; *alacrán* derives from Arabic **al-ʿaqrab**, probably through Andalusi Arabic.

### Hebrew

- Hebrew: **עַקְרָב, ʿAqrāv**, “scorpion.”
- Zodiacal designation: **מזל עקרב, mazzal ʿAqrāv**.
- The word is biblical: *ʿaqrāv* denotes the animal, not necessarily the constellation, in passages such as Deuteronomy 8:15 and Ezekiel 2:6.

### European vernaculars

- English: **Scorpio** for the sign; **Scorpius** for the constellation; older **Scorpion**.
- French: **Scorpion**.
- Italian: **Scorpione**.
- German: **Skorpion**.
- Spanish: **Escorpio**; constellation also **Escorpión**.
- Portuguese: **Escorpião**.
- Dutch: **Schorpioen**.

The IAU’s choice of *Scorpius* is a nomenclatural convention, not a declaration that Latin *Scorpio* is incorrect.

## Glyph: ♏

**Documented fact.** The modern glyph is a cursive, m-like form ending in a pointed tail or arrow: **♏**.

**Probable history.** Zodiacal shorthand symbols arose from late-antique and medieval scribal notation. Forms ancestral to ♏ occur in horoscope manuscripts and Byzantine astronomical traditions, then became increasingly standardized in Renaissance almanacs and printed ephemerides.

**Traditional explanation.** The loops represent the scorpion’s body or legs; the terminal arrow represents the sting. A modern anatomical interpretation reads the same mark as male genitalia, in accord with Scorpio’s melothesic rulership. This is a visual interpretation, not a securely documented origin.

**Unverified claim.** The common assertion that ♏ is an ancient Egyptian hieroglyph, or a monogram of “Mars,” could not be traced to an ancient source. No evidence was found that the present glyph occurs on Babylonian zodiac tablets or the Dendera relief.

# Stars and boundaries

## Present astronomical position

Scorpius crosses the Milky Way in a rich region toward the Galactic center. Approximate modern span:

- Right ascension: about **15h 47m–17h 59m**
- Declination: about **−8° to −46°**

Its exact polygon is defined by the IAU boundary system derived from Eugène Delporte’s work and formally adopted in 1930. The boundaries follow lines of right ascension and declination referred to epoch B1875.0; precession makes them appear oblique in present coordinate grids.

The constellation borders:

- Ophiuchus
- Libra
- Lupus
- Norma
- Ara
- Corona Australis
- Sagittarius

## Principal stars

### Antares — α Scorpii

- Apparent visual magnitude varies around **0.9–1.1**.
- Spectral type: red supergiant, approximately **M1.5 Iab-Ib**.
- Traditional position: the Scorpion’s heart.
- Greek: **Ἀντάρης, Antárēs**, conventionally interpreted as “rival of Ares [Mars]” because of its red color.
- Arabic traditional description: **قلب العقرب, qalb al-ʿaqrab**, “heart of the Scorpion.”
- Latin descriptions include **Cor Scorpii** and Pliny’s *rutilans*, “glowing red.”

**Documented fact.** Antares’ color generated comparison with Mars in Greco-Roman astronomy. The frequently repeated derivation from Arabic *Antar* is secondary and unsupported.

Antares is a multiple stellar system; the visible red supergiant has a hot blue companion.

### Shaula — λ Scorpii

Arabic **الشولة, al-shawla**, “the raised [tail/sting].” One of the close pair at the tip of the tail. It is among the constellation’s brightest stars, magnitude about **1.6**, and is a multiple system.

### Sargas — θ Scorpii

The name is of uncertain origin. Attempts to derive it directly from Sumerian **ŠAR.GAZ** are modern and disputed. The star is a bright southern giant, magnitude about **1.9**.

### Dschubba — δ Scorpii

From Arabic **الجبهة, al-jabha**, “the forehead.” It marks the forehead/head of the Scorpion. The spelling reflects European transliteration conventions. It is a variable multiple star and underwent a marked brightening beginning around 2000.

### Acrab / Akrab — β Scorpii

From Arabic **العقرب, al-ʿaqrab**, “the Scorpion.” The system is also historically called **Graffias**, a name with a confused transmission sometimes connected with Greek words for claw or crab. Acrab is a multiple system.

### Fang — π Scorpii

“Fang” is a modern IAU-approved proper name derived from a Chinese asterism, not from the western Scorpion tradition.

### Larawag — ε Scorpii

An IAU-approved Indigenous Australian name. It belongs to a separate sky tradition and is not an ancient western name.

### Lesath — υ Scorpii

The received name arose through corrupt Latin transmission of Arabic material. It is commonly glossed “the sting” or “the bite of a poisonous animal,” but its philological history is less transparent than popular star-name lists imply. Together with Shaula it forms the stinger.

### Paikauhale — τ Scorpii

An IAU-approved Hawaiian name. Like Fang and Larawag, it should not be projected backward into the Greco-Arabic tradition.

## Internal asterisms

- **Heart of the Scorpion:** Antares, often with neighboring σ and τ Scorpii.
- **Head / forehead:** β, δ and π Scorpii, with variants.
- **Tail:** the conspicuous curve through ε, μ, ζ, η, θ, ι and κ toward λ and υ.
- **Stinger:** Shaula and Lesath.
- **Fishhook:** the hooked tail inspired Polynesian identifications, discussed below.
- **The Claws:** originally the stars now assigned principally to Libra, especially α and β Librae.

## The lost Claws and Libra

**Documented fact.** In Aratus the region preceding the Scorpion is normally **the Claws**. Pseudo-Eratosthenes states that the creature’s great size occupied two twelfths: one for the claws, one for body and sting. Ptolemy continued to use claw terminology for stars formally catalogued in Libra.

The names preserve this history:

- **Zubenelgenubi**, α Librae: Arabic *al-zubānā al-janūbiyya*, “the southern claw.”
- **Zubeneschamali**, β Librae: *al-zubānā al-shamāliyya*, “the northern claw.”

**Documented fact.** Libra was established as an independent zodiacal figure in late Babylonian and Greco-Roman astronomy, but “Claws” and “Balance” coexisted for centuries. It is too simple to claim that “the Romans invented Libra by cutting off Scorpio’s claws.” A balance-sign existed in Babylonian zodiacal material; Roman writers helped normalize *Libra* in Latin.

## Ptolemy’s catalogue

Pseudo-Eratosthenes counts nineteen stars in the enlarged Scorpion: four in the claws, three in the forehead, three on the back, two in the belly, five in the tail and two in the sting.

Ptolemy separated Libra as a constellation but retained claw descriptions. His Scorpius catalogue maps the forehead, body, back, tail and sting. This makes Ptolemy’s Scorpion smaller than the older “superscorpion.”

## Bayer, Hevelius, Flamsteed and Bode

- **1515 — Dürer/Stabius:** printed northern and southern celestial hemisphere maps continued the Ptolemaic figural tradition.
- **1603 — Johann Bayer, *Uranometria*:** plotted Scorpius with data informed by Tycho Brahe and introduced the familiar Greek-letter designation system. The letters are not a strict brightness ranking in every constellation.
- **1690 — Johannes Hevelius, *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*:** engraved the constellation in globe-view orientation and incorporated improved early-modern stellar positions.
- **1729/1753 — John Flamsteed, *Atlas Coelestis* and French revision:** expanded the telescopic star field and helped stabilize numbered stars, though “Flamsteed numbers” were largely assigned editorially after his death.
- **1801 — Johann Elert Bode, *Uranographia*:** presented an exceptionally dense engraved Scorpion amid the Milky Way. The plate preserves the classical figure while plotting many telescopic stars.
- **1930 — IAU/Delporte:** replaced pictorial edges with exact geometric boundaries.

**Important distinction.** These atlases altered cataloguing, projection, stellar density, and sometimes the artistic outline. They did not successively “move the same official boundary”; no internationally binding polygon existed before the IAU system.

## Precession and the drift of sign from constellation

Earth’s rotational axis precesses with a cycle of roughly 25,700–25,800 years. The equinoctial point therefore moves westward against the stars by about **50.3 arcseconds per year**, roughly one degree in 71.6 years.

Tropical astrology fixes 0° Aries to the March equinox. Sidereal systems fix it relative to stars and apply an **ayanāṃśa**, the angular offset between the tropical and sidereal zodiacs.

Consequences:

- Tropical Scorpio remains 210°–240° tropical longitude.
- Its background stars change over millennia.
- A tropical Scorpio Sun is now generally projected against Libra or the western edge of astronomical Scorpius/Ophiuchus depending on date and boundary convention.
- A sidereal Scorpio Sun occurs several weeks later than a tropical Scorpio Sun.

## “Age of Aries” question

**Tradition.** Astrological ages divide a precessional cycle into twelve ideal periods of approximately 2,150 years. The “Age of Aries” is usually placed before the “Age of Pisces.”

**Disputed reconstruction.** Proposed starting dates range widely—often around the second millennium BCE—because authors choose different reference points: constellation boundaries, equal sidereal signs, bright stars, or religious symbolism.

**Modern invention when overstated.** Claims that the Age of Aries began on one exactly known ancient date, or that ram religion universally replaced bull religion because of precession, exceed the evidence. Hipparchus documented precession in the second century BCE; that does not prove that Bronze Age religious change was organized around later astrological ages.

# Chronology of attestation

## Before the zodiac

### Third–second millennia BCE: Mesopotamian scorpion imagery

**Documented fact.** Scorpions and scorpion-human guardians appear in Mesopotamian glyptic and literary traditions long before horoscopic astrology. A celestial or divine scorpion complex therefore predates the equal zodiac.

**Caution.** Visual scorpions on seals are not automatically depictions of the later constellation.

### Old Babylonian and later star traditions

A Scorpion constellation is attested in the Mesopotamian astronomical tradition. The Sumerogram **MUL.GÍR.TAB** was read in Akkadian as the Scorpion.

### ca. 1000 BCE, with later copies: *MUL.APIN*

**Documented fact.** *MUL.APIN* preserves an organized celestial system including a “path of the Moon” of seventeen or eighteen constellations. The Scorpion appears in rising-and-setting and calendrical schemes.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The tablets compile material older than the surviving first-millennium copies. Exact dating of each component remains debated.

## Formation of the twelve-sign zodiac

### Fifth century BCE

**Strong scholarly consensus.** Babylonian astronomers developed the mathematical zodiac of twelve equal thirty-degree signs during the fifth century BCE. The earliest securely dated use of zodiacal longitudes appears in Babylonian astronomical texts of this period.

The equal zodiac was not simply a list of twelve pictures. It was a coordinate system superimposed on an inherited belt of unequal constellations.

### Fourth century BCE

Text **BRM 4.20**, usually dated to the later fourth century BCE, is important evidence for the twelve zodiacal constellations in astrological-magical use.

## Greek adoption

### Eudoxus, fourth century BCE

Eudoxus’ lost *Phaenomena* described the constellations. Its substance survives partly through Aratus and Hipparchus. No complete Eudoxan Scorpio myth survives.

### Aratus, ca. 275 BCE

In *Phaenomena*, Aratus describes the Scorpion and its huge Claws. He repeatedly uses their risings and settings as weather and calendrical markers. His poem is astronomical and didactic; it does not give a full Orion catasterism for Scorpio.

### Pseudo-Eratosthenes, Hellenistic material surviving in later epitomes

*Catasterismi* 7 describes Scorpio as so large that it occupies two twelfths. Artemis raises a scorpion from a hill or mound on Chios after Orion sexually assaults or attempts to violate her during a hunt. The scorpion strikes him; Zeus places it among the brilliant stars as a memorial of its strength.

Attribution to Eratosthenes in its present wording is uncertain. The original work is lost; the surviving epitome and related recension preserve Hellenistic mythographic material.

### Hipparchus, second century BCE

Hipparchus’ commentary on Aratus and Eudoxus criticized inaccurate stellar descriptions and used observed risings and settings. His work is central to the history of precession, though the familiar Scorpio myth is not derived from him.

## Egypt

### Ptolemaic and Roman periods

Greek astrology took root in Egypt alongside Egyptian calendrical and decanal systems.

The **36 Egyptian decans** began as stellar timekeepers, not thirds of zodiacal signs. In Hellenistic astrology they were fitted into the imported zodiac as three ten-degree divisions per sign.

### Dendera

The circular zodiac from a chapel of Osiris at the temple of Hathor, Dendera, is generally dated to the late Ptolemaic period, approximately the first century BCE. Rectangular zodiacs at the temple belong to the Greco-Roman period.

**Documented fact.** A recognizable scorpion appears among the twelve signs.

**Scholarly consensus.** Dendera combines Egyptian iconography, decanal figures and the imported zodiac. It is not evidence that the complete twelve-sign zodiac existed in pharaonic Egypt thousands of years earlier.

## Rome and the imperial Mediterranean

### First century BCE–first century CE

- **Cicero and Germanicus** translated/adapted Aratus.
- **Virgil** refers to the Scorpion’s great space and claws in *Georgics* 1.32–35.
- **Ovid**, *Fasti* 5.493–544, gives a distinctive Orion story involving the scorpion.
- **Manilius**, *Astronomica*, treats Scorpio astrologically, associating it with martial, dangerous and commanding qualities and with parts of the human body.

### First–second centuries CE

- **Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.26:** gathers competing Orion–Scorpion stories.
- **Ptolemy, *Almagest*:** catalogues the stars.
- **Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* 1.17–24:** classifies Scorpio as Mars’ house and the Moon’s depression/fall, and records triplicities, terms and stellar influences.
- **Vettius Valens, *Anthologies*:** preserves working horoscopic doctrine, including sign qualities and bounds.

### Fourth century CE

**Firmicus Maternus**, *Mathesis*, transmits Latin astrological doctrine: Scorpio is Mars’ sign; the Moon falls there; signs are divided into decans; detailed natal outcomes are assigned to planets and angles within it.

## India

### *Yavanajātaka*

The text attributed to Sphujidhvaja is a Sanskrit adaptation of Greek-derived horoscopic material. Its dating and textual history are disputed: David Pingree proposed a Greek original translated in 149/150 CE and versified in 269/270, while later scholarship has challenged the certainty of that reconstruction and even aspects of the received date.

Scorpio became **Vṛścika**, “scorpion.” Indian astrology retained an equal twelve-sign zodiac alongside the older nakṣatra system.

### Varāhamihira, sixth century

In the *Bṛhajjātaka* and *Bṛhat Saṃhitā*, Varāhamihira synthesizes Indian and Yavana-derived astrology. Vṛścika comprises the last quarter of Viśākhā and all of Anurādhā and Jyeṣṭhā in the standard nakṣatra mapping.

Indian sidereal Scorpio is therefore historically related to the western sign but embedded in a distinct technical system.

## Sasanian, Arabic and Persian transmission

### Sasanian period

Greek, Indian and Mesopotamian astronomical traditions were received and recombined in Middle Persian scholarly culture. Much is known through later Arabic reports because many Pahlavi astronomical works are lost.

### Eighth–ninth centuries

Under the Abbasids, Greek and Persian astronomical and astrological works were translated into Arabic.

**Abū Maʿshar** (787–886) systematized doctrines of conjunctions, nativities and historical astrology. His works later became exceptionally influential in Latin Europe.

### al-Ṣūfī, 964

In the *Book of the Fixed Stars*, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī revised Ptolemy’s star descriptions and illustrated each constellation twice: as seen on a globe and as seen in the sky. Scorpio appears as an articulated scorpion with labelled stellar positions. The earliest surviving illustrated manuscript is dated **1009–1010**.

### *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm* / *Picatrix*

The Arabic *Ghāyat al-ḥakīm*, probably composed in al-Andalus in the tenth or eleventh century, associates zodiacal degrees, faces and images with astral magic.

**Disputed attribution.** Its traditional attribution to Maslama al-Majrīṭī is generally rejected or qualified.

It was translated into Castilian under Alfonso X, reportedly **1256–1258**, and thence into Latin as *Picatrix*. Scorpio images are used for operations involving scorpions, poisons, protection, sexuality, conflict and related sympathetic correspondences, depending on recension.

## Latin Europe

### Twelfth-century translation movement

Translations by figures such as John of Seville, Hermann of Carinthia and others brought Arabic astrology, including Abū Maʿshar, into Latin. Scorpio became established in computistical calendars, medical astrology, agricultural election, natal doctrine and monumental art.

### Dante

Dante uses zodiacal and planetary imagery throughout the *Commedia*. Scorpio belongs to this learned cosmological framework, although it is less programmatically central than Gemini, Dante’s claimed natal sign. Later allegorical attempts to make Scorpio secretly govern the poem are interpretive traditions, not explicit claims by Dante.

## Renaissance

### Palazzo Schifanoia, Ferrara, ca. 1469–1470

The Salone dei Mesi integrates zodiac signs, decanal figures and courtly monthly scenes. Its iconographic program was famously interpreted by Aby Warburg through Abū Maʿshar and the migration of Egyptian–Greek–Indian–Arabic images.

Scorpio belongs to the autumnal sector. Identification of individual decan figures and their exact textual source remains partly disputed.

### Dürer, 1515

Albrecht Dürer’s celestial hemisphere woodcuts, designed with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, presented the inherited Ptolemaic constellations in a printed humanist framework.

### Agrippa, 1531/1533

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* organized zodiacal correspondences, images, body parts, spirits and magical operations. It became a major vehicle for Renaissance occult Scorpio doctrine.

### Bayer, 1603

*Uranometria* gave Scorpius a precise engraved form and lettered its stars.

### Kepler

Johannes Kepler rejected much rule-bound popular astrology but continued to cast charts, write prognostications and investigate aspects. He did not simply “abolish astrology.” His reform proposals emphasized physical celestial relationships over inherited sign characterology.

### Schiller, 1627

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaced the twelve pagan zodiacal figures with apostles. Scorpius became **Saint Bartholomew**, *Sancti Bartholomaei Apostoli*. The reason was structural—twelve signs matched twelve apostles—not a traditional identification of Bartholomew with a scorpion. The atlas was published in Augsburg with engravings by Lucas Kilian and assistance from Bayer.

## Enlightenment and modernity

- Hevelius, Flamsteed and Bode progressively expanded and regularized stellar cartography.
- Nineteenth-century popular astrology developed increasingly psychological Sun-sign descriptions.
- Theosophy and occult revival movements recast Scorpio as a sign of initiation, death, regeneration and hidden sexuality.
- Newspaper Sun-sign columns in the twentieth century detached characterology from the full natal horoscope.
- Pluto, discovered in 1930, was rapidly proposed as Scorpio’s modern ruler.
- Psychological and evolutionary astrologies made transformation, trauma, shadow and rebirth central Scorpio themes.
- Astronomy retains Scorpius as an IAU constellation; astrology uses tropical or multiple sidereal Scorpios.

# Myths and legends by culture

## Mesopotamia

### The celestial Scorpion

**Documented mythic tradition.** Mesopotamian star lists identify the constellation as the Scorpion, but no surviving text supplies a Greek-style catasterism explaining who placed it in heaven. Its existence is presented as part of the ordered divine sky.

Claims that a named Babylonian god “became Scorpio” are generally later harmonizations.

### The Scorpion-men at Mount Māšu

In the Standard Babylonian *Epic of Gilgamesh*, Tablet IX, Gilgamesh reaches the twin-peaked mountain Māšu, where the Sun enters and exits. **Scorpion-men**, Akkadian *aqrabbū-amēlū*, guard its gate. Their glance is death; their radiance is terrifying. The male recognizes Gilgamesh’s partly divine nature, and the pair permit him to enter the Sun’s dark road.

**Status:** documented Mesopotamian myth.

**Relationship to the constellation:** probable thematic association but not explicitly a zodiacal catasterism. The text does not say, “these guardians are the constellation GIR.TAB.” The connection is therefore a **scholarly comparison**, not a direct identification.

### Išḫara

The goddess Išḫara was associated with oaths, sexuality and, in later iconography, the scorpion. She has sometimes been described as “goddess of Scorpio.”

**Caution.** Her scorpion emblem and the zodiacal constellation overlap culturally, but the surviving sources do not establish a single simple equation Išḫara = the constellation.

## Egypt

### Serqet

Serqet is an ancient protective goddess associated with scorpions, venom, healing, breath and funerary protection. She appears among the four protective goddesses guarding coffins and canopic equipment, together with Isis, Nephthys and Neith.

**Documented religious tradition.** Her priests were associated with protection or treatment against venomous creatures.

**Etymological caution.** Her name is often rendered “she who causes the throat to breathe,” but Egyptian analysis and vocalization are uncertain. The opposite gloss, “she who tightens the throat,” reflects the paralysing action of venom. Neither English slogan should be treated as a certain literal translation.

### Isis and the seven scorpions

In the Egyptian magical narrative preserved on the Metternich Stela and related texts, Isis travels with seven scorpions—Tefen, Befen, Mestet, Mestetef, Petet, Thetet and Matet. A wealthy woman refuses Isis shelter; a poor woman receives her. The scorpions combine their venom, Tefen stings the rich woman’s child, and Isis, moved by the mother’s grief, recites a healing spell and restores him.

**Status:** documented Egyptian myth and magical precedent.

**Relationship to zodiacal Scorpio:** none stated in the text. Later occult writers often merge the story with the sign; that is a secondary synthesis.

### Dendera’s Scorpion

The Dendera zodiac shows the familiar animal. It participates in an Egyptian temple cosmos connected with Osiris, lunar cycles and decans.

**Status:** documented image.

**Disputed interpretation.** Specific claims that the Scorpion encodes an exact eclipse, a secret date for Atlantis, or an age tens of thousands of years ago are not supported by the mainstream archaeological dating of the temple relief.

## Greek and Roman myths

## Orion: Artemis raises the Scorpion after an assault

Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 7, says Artemis caused a scorpion to rise from a hill on Chios and strike Orion because, while hunting, he violently assaulted or attempted to violate her. Zeus placed the creature among the bright stars so later people would know its strength.

**Status:** documented Hellenistic catasterism, surviving through a later epitome.

**Who placed it:** Zeus.

**Who generated or summoned it:** Artemis.

**Motive:** punishment for Orion’s sexual violence against Artemis.

This is the fullest independent Scorpio catasterism in the surviving Eratosthenic tradition.

## Orion: boast against all animals; Earth sends the Scorpion

A competing narrative, reported by Hyginus and connected in ancient scholarship with Hesiodic astronomy, says Orion boasted that he would kill every animal on earth. **Earth/Gaia** sent a giant scorpion, which killed him.

Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.26, explains that Jupiter admired the antagonists and placed both in the sky so that the Scorpion rises as Orion sets.

**Status:** documented ancient variant.

**Who sends it:** Gaia/Terra.

**Crime:** destructive boast or threat against the world’s animals.

**Who places it in heaven:** Zeus/Jupiter.

The attribution to Hesiod depends on fragmentary and indirect evidence; it should be cited as “Hesiodic tradition” rather than treated as a securely preserved passage from a complete *Astronomy*.

## Orion: attempt against Diana/Artemis

Hyginus also reports that Orion tried to violate Diana while hunting. She sent or produced the scorpion that killed him.

**Status:** documented Roman mythographic variant, closely related to Pseudo-Eratosthenes.

The relative agency varies: Artemis may summon the creature; Earth may produce it; Jupiter may perform the catasterism.

## Orion: Apollo’s jealousy and Artemis’ fatal shot

In another Orion cycle, Apollo disapproves of Artemis’ affection for Orion. Seeing Orion’s distant head in the sea, Apollo challenges Artemis to strike the remote target. She kills Orion unknowingly and then places or obtains his placement among the stars.

This appears in Hyginus’ Orion discussion and later mythography.

**Status:** documented ancient Orion myth.

**Relationship to Scorpio:** indirect. The scorpion does not cause the death in this version, although later summaries often combine Apollo’s trick and the scorpion into one continuous story. Ancient variants should remain separate unless a source explicitly joins them.

## Ovid’s *Fasti*: Orion protects Leto

Ovid, *Fasti* 5.493–544, presents a markedly different story. Orion is born from the urine/semen of Jupiter, Neptune and Mercury deposited in a bull-hide for the hospitable Hyrieus. Later, while Orion is accompanying Diana and Leto, Earth sends a scorpion against Leto. Orion places himself in danger and saves her. Leto then arranges for Orion to become a constellation.

**Status:** documented Augustan poetic variant.

Here the scorpion is not primarily Orion’s executioner but an attacker whose threat displays Orion’s heroism. Modern retellings often replace Ovid’s actual narrative with the more familiar fatal-sting version.

## Why Orion and Scorpius are “never together”

**Documented ancient explanation.** Hyginus says their placements ensure that when Scorpius rises, Orion sets.

**Astronomical qualification.** They are placed broadly opposite in the sky, so their seasonal prominence alternates. “Never visible together” is poetic shorthand, not a universal geometrical fact: at suitable latitudes and times, portions of both constellations may be above the horizon simultaneously.

## Phaethon frightened by the Scorpion

In Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 2.195–200, Phaethon loses control of the Sun’s chariot. When he sees the Scorpion curving its arms and tail across two signs and threatening with its hooked sting, terror makes him drop the reins.

**Status:** documented Roman mythic-poetic use of the celestial figure.

This passage preserves the idea of the enormous Scorpion occupying the space of Scorpio and the Claws/Libra. It is not a creation myth for the constellation.

## Mars and the dangerous Scorpion

Manilius, *Astronomica*, treats Scorpio less through catasterism than through astrological character. The sign produces people suited to war, cities, weapons, command, hunting or dangerous action, depending on degree and rising portion.

**Status:** documented Roman astrological poetry, not a myth of origin.

## Nonnus

Nonnus’ *Dionysiaca* uses zodiacal and celestial imagery lavishly. Scorpions and Orion can appear as poetic emblems of poison, war or cosmic opposition.

**Finding.** No independent new Scorpio catasterism comparable to the Eratosthenic or Hyginian versions was securely identified. Assertions that Nonnus names a different god who created Scorpio should be treated as unverified unless a book-and-line citation is supplied.

## Pseudo-Apollodorus

Pseudo-Apollodorus records important Orion and Artemis traditions but does not preserve a full independent zodiacal Scorpio catasterism. His mythographic corpus should not be cited automatically for the Gaia-scorpion version without a precise passage.

## Pausanias

Pausanias discusses Orion’s tombs, local cults and associated places. He does not provide the canonical Scorpio catasterism. Later constellation books sometimes cite “Pausanias on Scorpio” through conflation with his Orion material.

## Scholia to Aratus and Germanicus

The scholia preserve and recombine variants:

- Orion’s universal hunting boast and Gaia’s retaliation.
- Orion’s sexual aggression toward Artemis.
- The opposed stellar placement of hunter and Scorpion.
- Explanations of the Claws’ exceptional size.

Because scholia are layered compilations, “the scholiast” is not necessarily one author or one date. Individual readings require manuscript and edition identification.

## Scriptural and Jewish traditions

### Scorpions in scripture

Scorpions symbolize danger, punishment or hostile power:

- Deuteronomy 8:15 — wilderness of fiery serpents and scorpions.
- 1 Kings 12:11, 14 / 2 Chronicles 10:11, 14 — “scorpions” as harsher instruments of punishment.
- Ezekiel 2:6 — dwelling among scorpions as a metaphor for hostile people.
- Luke 10:19 — power to tread on serpents and scorpions.
- Revelation 9 — locusts with scorpion-like power and tails.

**Documented fact.** None of these passages names the zodiacal sign.

### Scorpio and Dan

**Tradition, not biblical fact.** Jewish, Christian and occult schemes correlate the twelve tribes with zodiac signs. Scorpio is frequently assigned to **Dan**, partly because Genesis 49:17 compares Dan to a serpent on the road and because later symbolic systems connect serpent, eagle and scorpion.

But tribal-zodiac mappings differ between authors. Dan is also assigned to Libra or other signs. The Bible itself does not say “Dan is Scorpio.”

### Scorpio and the eagle

Ezekiel 1 and Revelation 4 describe four living creatures or faces: man, lion, ox/bull and eagle. Christian and occult commentators associated them with the four fixed signs:

- Aquarius — man
- Leo — lion
- Taurus — bull
- Scorpio — eagle

**Tradition.** This correspondence is late-antique or medieval interpretive symbolism, not an explicit biblical zodiac.

It helped make the eagle an alternative or “higher” Scorpio emblem.

## Sefer Yetzirah tradition

In later recensions and commentaries on *Sefer Yetzirah*, the twelve simple Hebrew letters correspond to months, zodiac signs, bodily organs and faculties. A widespread scheme assigns:

- Scorpio / ʿAqrāv
- Month **Marḥeshvan**
- Letter **נ, Nun**
- Faculty sometimes translated **smell**
- Bodily correspondence often the intestines or bowels

**Textual caution.** *Sefer Yetzirah* exists in divergent short, long, Saadia and later recensions. Exact tables differ. Presenting one correspondence chart as “the original Kabbalah” conceals this manuscript history.

## Christianization: Saint Bartholomew

Julius Schiller’s 1627 atlas turns Scorpius into Saint Bartholomew.

**Documented fact.** The plate and catalogue explicitly make the substitution.

**No ancient lineage found.** Bartholomew’s martyrdom by flaying and his knife may resonate retrospectively with Mars or the sting, but Schiller supplies a systematic apostolic replacement. There is no evidence of an early Christian legend identifying the apostle with Scorpio.

## Polynesia: Māui’s fishhook

Across Polynesia, the conspicuous hooked tail of Scorpius is identified with a fishhook connected with Māui. Hawaiian names include **Ka Makau Nui o Māui**, “the great fishhook of Māui,” and the hook-name **Manaiakalani** in Hawaiian tradition.

**Documented indigenous tradition.** The star pattern’s curved tail naturally supports this figure.

**Important limitation.** This is not part of Scorpius’ western lineage and was not derived from Babylonian Scorpio. It belongs here only because European astronomy later overlaid the IAU constellation on Polynesian sky traditions.

Treating every Polynesian version as one uniform “Māui myth” is misleading: island groups have different names, stars, narratives and hook orientations.

# Doctrine and uses

## Element, modality and polarity

In classical and medieval western astrology Scorpio is:

- **Water**
- **Fixed**
- **Feminine/nocturnal**
- **Cold and moist** by elemental classification, though Mars adds heat and dryness
- One of the three signs of the water triplicity, with Cancer and Pisces

These are traditional symbolic classifications, not astronomical properties.

## Planetary ruler

### Mars

**Documented astrological tradition.** Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* 1.17, assigns Aries and Scorpio to Mars as domiciles. In the symmetrical classical system each of the five non-luminary planets rules two signs.

Scorpio is commonly called Mars’ nocturnal or feminine house, though terminology varies by school.

### Pluto

**Modern astrological tradition.** After Pluto’s discovery in 1930, astrologers debated its domicile. Proposals included Scorpio, Aries, Leo and Cancer. Scorpio quickly gained the broadest acceptance because Pluto’s underworld name seemed consonant with existing themes of death, secrecy and regeneration.

Some of the earliest published proposals appeared in **1930–1931**. Pluto’s rulership is therefore not ancient, Babylonian or Renaissance doctrine.

Traditional astrologers generally retain Mars; many modern astrologers use Pluto as ruler and Mars as co-ruler.

## Exaltation, fall and detriment

- Scorpio is the **fall/depression of the Moon**, opposite the Moon’s exaltation in Taurus.
- Venus is in **detriment/exile** in Scorpio, opposite Taurus, one of Venus’ domiciles.
- No planet has a universally recognized classical exaltation in Scorpio.
- Modern claims that Uranus or another outer planet is exalted there are school-specific innovations.

Ptolemy explicitly explains the Moon’s depression by opposition to Taurus.

## Decans

Two major systems must not be confused.

### Chaldean-order faces

Using the repeating planetary order Saturn–Jupiter–Mars–Sun–Venus–Mercury–Moon, Scorpio’s three faces are conventionally:

1. **0°–10° Scorpio: Mars**
2. **10°–20° Scorpio: Sun**
3. **20°–30° Scorpio: Venus**

This table is firmly embedded in medieval and Renaissance astrology, though its exact ancient origin is more complex than the label “Chaldean” suggests.

### Triplicity decans

Modern popular astrology sometimes assigns:

1. Mars/Scorpio
2. Jupiter/Pisces
3. Moon/Cancer

This is a later sign-triplicity system and should not be retrojected into Ptolemy or the Egyptian decans.

## Egyptian terms or bounds

A widely transmitted Egyptian table gives Scorpio’s five bounds as:

- Mars: 0°–7°
- Venus: 7°–11°
- Mercury: 11°–19°
- Jupiter: 19°–24°
- Saturn: 24°–30°

Ptolemy supplies a different table of his own. Manuscripts and editions should be consulted before using exact degrees, since tables were prone to scribal corruption.

## Body and medical astrology

In zodiacal melothesia Scorpio governs:

- Genitals
- Reproductive organs
- Bladder
- Groin
- Excretory passages
- Sometimes the anus or lower bowel

Mars separately rules blood, bile, cutting, fevers and injuries in several medical-astrological systems.

**Tradition.** Medieval physicians consulted the Moon’s sign before bleeding, surgery, purging or treating a body region. The usual rule cautioned against operating on a body part while the Moon occupied its governing sign.

**Historical fact, not medical endorsement.** These practices are documented cultural history; they are not validated modern medicine.

## Agriculture and weather

Scorpio’s water classification made it variously “fertile” or “fruitful” in electional agriculture. Almanacs recommended or prohibited planting, pruning, castration, slaughter, grafting and medicinal gathering according to the Moon in Scorpio.

Rules varied sharply. A statement such as “never plant under Scorpio” cannot be generalized across European almanac traditions.

Aratus and later parapegmata used the heliacal risings and settings of the Scorpion or its stars for weather and seasonal prediction; that observational practice predates Sun-sign characterology.

## Characterology

### Ancient

Ancient texts generally do not describe every person with the Sun in Scorpio as a modern “Scorpio personality.” Character resulted from the entire horoscope: rising sign, rulers, planets, lots, aspects and subdivisions.

Nevertheless Scorpio accumulated qualities from:

- Mars: courage, violence, command, metal, blood.
- Fixed modality: endurance and persistence.
- Water: secrecy, moisture and fertility.
- Animal form: poison, defensiveness and concealed danger.
- Genital melothesia: sexuality and generation.

### Medieval and Renaissance

Astrologers elaborated bodily appearance, occupation, diseases, moral tendencies, weather and political significations. Scorpio could signify surgeons, soldiers, executioners, miners, poisoners, alchemists, physicians and people dealing with dangerous substances.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Theosophical and psychological writers emphasized:

- Sexual intensity
- Secrecy
- Magnetism
- Jealousy
- Crisis
- Death and rebirth
- Occult knowledge
- Transformation

Newspaper astrology popularized a compressed Scorpio “type” derived from solar position alone.

## Scorpion, eagle and phoenix

### Scorpion

The ancient and primary emblem.

### Eagle

The eagle is supported by the fixed-sign interpretation of the biblical four living creatures. It existed as an esoteric alternative to Scorpio before the modern “three evolutionary stages” formula.

### Phoenix

The phoenix as regenerated Scorpio became prominent in modern occult and psychological astrology. Manly P. Hall, among others, described multiple Scorpio symbols, including eagle and phoenix, in twentieth-century esoteric writing.

### The staged hierarchy

**Modern construction.** The formula:

1. low Scorpio = scorpion,
2. evolved Scorpio = eagle,
3. highest Scorpio = phoenix,

is not found in Babylonian star lists, Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Ptolemy, Manilius, al-Ṣūfī or medieval Latin astrology. It appears to synthesize biblical fixed-sign symbolism, occult initiation and modern psychological evolution.

An exact first inventor was not securely established. Claims attributing the complete three-stage system to ancient Egypt or Native American tradition are unsupported.

## Alchemy

Scorpio is associated in later occult tables with:

- Putrefaction
- Fermentation
- Separation
- Mortification
- Dissolution
- Regeneration

**Disputed tradition.** There was no single universal alchemical zodiac. Different authors assign different operations. The much-repeated equation “Scorpio = putrefaction” is especially compatible with early-modern and modern occult symbolism but should not be presented as an uncontested ancient rule.

# Iconography

## Mesopotamian seals and boundary stones

Scorpions appear on Mesopotamian seals, kudurrus and divine-symbol registers. Some may have celestial significance.

**Caution.** Without inscriptions, identification as the zodiacal constellation is often uncertain.

## Dendera zodiac

- Place: Temple of Hathor, Dendera, Egypt.
- Date: late Ptolemaic/early Roman era, commonly first century BCE for the circular zodiac.
- Current location of circular relief: Louvre, Paris.
- Depiction: recognizable scorpion among mixed Greco-Egyptian zodiacal and decanal imagery.

## Greco-Roman monuments and coins

Scorpio appears on:

- Zodiac rings and mosaics.
- Astrological gems.
- Mithraic monuments.
- Provincial coins and imperial types.

Roman military and political imagery sometimes used Scorpio in connection with Mars or an emperor’s natal claims. Claims about a coin’s “Scorpio horoscope” require attention to mint, legend and date; an isolated scorpion can also be a local emblem or fertility/protection symbol.

## Manuscripts of Aratus

Medieval *Aratea* manuscripts depict an enlarged scorpion, frequently including long claws. Copyists sometimes make it resemble a crab, reflecting artistic unfamiliarity with scorpion anatomy and the visual proximity of Cancer imagery.

Notable traditions include:

- Carolingian *Aratea* manuscripts.
- The Leiden Aratea, ninth century, derived from late-antique models.
- Later French and German astronomical miscellanies.

## Cathedral portals and calendars

Scorpio occurs in monumental zodiac cycles associated with the labors of the months, including Romanesque and Gothic portals, pavements and rose-window programs.

Typical placement is October–November, with scenes of:

- Sowing
- Ploughing
- Gathering or processing autumn produce
- Slaughtering livestock
- Preparing for winter

The precise labor assigned to Scorpio varies geographically.

## *Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry*

- Place of manufacture: France.
- Date: principally ca. 1412–1416, with later completions.
- Artists: Limbourg brothers and later contributors.
- Scorpio appears in the calendar’s semicircular celestial bands spanning the relevant month divisions.

It is an astrological-calendar emblem, not an illustration of the Orion myth.

## Palazzo Schifanoia

- Ferrara, Italy.
- ca. 1469–1470.
- Scorpio appears within the planetary-zodiacal-decanic organization of the Hall of the Months.
- The images exemplify the movement of astral iconography from antiquity through Arabic and medieval textual traditions into Renaissance court culture.

## Dürer’s map

Dürer’s 1515 celestial maps place the classical constellations in a humanist reconstruction attributed to Ptolemaic authority. Scorpio retains its expansive animal form.

## Bayer

*Uranometria* (Augsburg, 1603) depicts a naturalistic scorpion and establishes a durable stellar-letter framework.

## Schiller

*Coelum Stellatum Christianum* (Augsburg, 1627), plate XXIX, replaces the animal with Saint Bartholomew. The atlas shows the stars in reverse/globe orientation in its main plate tradition.

## Hevelius

*Firmamentum Sobiescianum* (published 1690) gives an elegant engraved scorpion. Hevelius normally chose the external globe view, explaining why his figures appear reversed relative to earth-view atlases.

## Flamsteed

The posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* (1729) expands the stellar field while retaining the classical creature.

## Bode plate

Bode’s *Uranographia* (Berlin, 1801) provides one of the most elaborate Scorpius plates:

- Dense telescopic star field.
- Prominent classical scorpion.
- Milky Way environment.
- Neighboring Libra, Ophiuchus, Sagittarius, Lupus and Ara figures.
- A culmination of pictorial celestial cartography shortly before scientific atlases increasingly removed mythological figures.

# People, events and works

## Claimed births

Sun-sign lists commonly identify people born approximately 23 October–22 November as Scorpios. These are modern tropical classifications and say nothing by themselves about ancient natal astrology.

Frequently cited examples include:

- Marie Curie
- Pablo Picasso
- Fyodor Dostoevsky
- Claude Monet
- Georgia O’Keeffe
- Indira Gandhi
- Charles de Gaulle
- Martin Luther
- Sylvia Plath

Exact classification depends on birth year, time, location and ingress. A person born near the cusp cannot be assigned reliably from a generic date table.

## Roman imperial and military uses

Scorpio was a Roman military emblem, including association with the Praetorian Guard in some material. The animal could also signify danger, protection, Africa or a natal sign.

**Disputed popular claim.** “Scorpio was Augustus’ personal sign” oversimplifies a complex record. Augustus publicly emphasized Capricorn despite being born under Libra by ordinary solar reckoning; ancient explanations invoke conception, Moon, ascendant or political symbolism. Scorpio imagery should not be assigned to him without object-specific evidence.

## Antares and warfare

Because Antares resembles Mars, conjunctions of Mars and Antares attracted ancient and later astrological attention. Astrologers associated the pair with war, fire and military upheaval.

**Status:** documented omen/astrological tradition; no causal astronomical effect is established.

## Temples and orientations

Numerous popular works claim temples, pyramids or cities were aligned to Antares.

Each claim requires:

- Demonstrated ancient sightline.
- Construction date.
- Horizon altitude.
- Precessional correction.
- Archaeological evidence that the alignment was intentional.

No universal “Scorpio temple network” is recognized by archaeology.

## Modern works

Scorpio/Scorpius has supplied titles and imagery to:

- Karlheinz Stockhausen’s *Tierkreis*.
- Astrological poetry and popular songs.
- Science-fiction star systems.
- Military vehicles and weapons.
- Films and television characters named “Scorpio.”
- Contemporary visual art using Antares or the scorpion glyph.

These uses inherit a mixed archive of poison, Mars, sexuality, secrecy and rebirth rather than one continuous ancient meaning.

# Controversies and disputes

## Was Scorpio originally an eagle?

**Finding:** no.

The Babylonian, Greek and Roman figure is explicitly a scorpion. Eagle symbolism comes through the fixed-sign interpretation of Ezekiel/Revelation and later esotericism. Claims that an original Egyptian eagle was “degraded” into a scorpion are modern speculative mythology.

## Did Libra originate only when Rome cut off the claws?

**Finding:** misleading.

The Claws were certainly part of an older enlarged Scorpion and remained alive in Greek and Arabic star names. But Babylonian evidence also supports a Balance constellation/sign. Greek and Roman practices reflect coexistence and transmission, not a single Roman act of invention.

## Is Antares Arabic?

**Finding:** Greek name, Arabic parallel.

*Antares* is Greek, conventionally “rival of Ares.” Arabic astronomy called it the Heart of the Scorpion. European texts sometimes confused or hybridized the traditions.

## Does GIR.TAB encode a hidden spiritual sentence?

**Finding:** unsupported.

It is the standard written name of the Scorpion. Esoteric decompositions based on isolated cuneiform sign meanings ignore scribal and lexical context.

## Were Gilgamesh’s scorpion-men the constellation?

**Finding:** possible symbolic relationship, not explicit identity.

Both belong to Mesopotamian scorpion-and-sun imagery, and the guardians stand at the Sun’s gate. The epic does not call them MUL.GÍR.TAB or state that they were placed among the zodiacal stars.

## Is Serqet the Egyptian Scorpio?

**Finding:** culturally related animal, not demonstrated identity.

Serqet is genuinely ancient; the twelve-sign zodiac entered Egypt much later. Dendera’s zodiacal Scorpio should not be treated as proof of a pharaonic twelve-sign system.

## Did NASA add Ophiuchus?

**Finding:** modern hoax/misreporting.

NASA did not create, add or recognize a new astrological sign. Ophiuchus was already one of Ptolemy’s constellations.

### 1970

Steven Schmidt proposed a fourteen-sign zodiac including Ophiuchus and Cetus. This was an alternative modern astrological scheme.

### 1995

Walter Berg promoted a thirteen-sign sidereal zodiac using IAU constellation boundaries. His *The 13 Signs of the Zodiac* gained particular attention in Japan. Berg openly describes his system as a break from the twelve-sign tradition.

### 2011

Comments by astronomer Parke Kunkle were reported as discovery of a “new sign.” Viral reports confused:

- Constellations with signs.
- Precession with recent stellar movement.
- IAU boundaries with astrological sign divisions.
- Astronomical description with astrological legislation.

### 2016 and 2020

The story resurfaced falsely as “NASA changed the zodiac.”

### The substantive issue

The Sun crosses thirteen IAU constellations conventionally counted along the ecliptic, including Ophiuchus. It also grazes Cetus under some definitions of solar disk/boundary contact.

But the historical zodiac is a system of twelve equal signs. There is no contradiction: a coordinate division need not reproduce unequal modern constellation polygons.

## “Scorpio lasts seven days”

True only for the Sun’s residence within the present IAU constellation. False for tropical or equal-sign sidereal Scorpio, both of which span thirty degrees.

## “The Babylonians deliberately threw out Ophiuchus because twelve was neater”

**Overstated popular reconstruction.** Babylonian astronomers knew more than twelve ecliptic constellations and constructed twelve equal signs in relation to twelve schematic months. But there is no known tablet narrating a meeting at which they “removed Ophiuchus.” That wording dramatizes a gradual mathematical standardization.

## “The zodiac shifted because the Moon pulled the Earth”

**Misleading.** Lunisolar torque contributes to axial precession, but zodiacal drift accumulated gradually over millennia. It was not a recent displacement discovered in 2011.

## Exact sidereal Scorpio dates

**Disputed by system.** Lahiri, Fagan–Bradley, Raman, Krishnamurti and other ayanāṃśas define different offsets. “The real Scorpio dates” is therefore not a neutral phrase.

## Tropical astrology forgot precession

**Incorrect as a blanket claim.** Hipparchus and Ptolemy knew precession. Tropical astrologers deliberately relate signs to equinoxes and solstices. Whether that system has predictive validity is a separate question from whether its practitioners understand precession.

## Characterology mistaken for ancient doctrine

Statements such as “all ancient peoples considered Scorpios jealous, magnetic and sexually intense” are unsupported. Some ingredients are ancient—Mars, genitals, poison, secrecy—but the integrated psychological personality is primarily modern.

## Pluto’s supposed ancient rulership

Pluto cannot have ruled Scorpio in surviving ancient astrology because the planet was unknown. Claims that Sumerians knew Pluto and assigned it to Scorpio are unsupported by cuneiform astronomical evidence.

## “Higher forms” as ancient initiation

The scorpion–eagle–phoenix ladder is modern esoteric astrology. Its components have older histories, but their presentation as three evolutionary levels lacks an identified ancient source.

## Maui’s fishhook as proof of global diffusion

**Unsupported.** Similar star patterns can generate similar images independently. No evidence demonstrates Babylonian transmission of Scorpio to pre-contact Polynesia.

## Star-name problems

Several popular derivations remain uncertain:

- **Sargas = ŠAR.GAZ:** attractive but disputed.
- **Graffias:** transmitted through confused Greek/Latin/Arabic nomenclature.
- **Lesath:** received through textual corruption; simple glosses may conceal uncertainty.
- **Antares from Arabic ʿAntar:** false or at best a secondary medieval association.
- **Acrab:** securely from Arabic *al-ʿaqrab*.

# Open questions

1. How early can the exact stellar membership of Mesopotamian MUL.GÍR.TAB be reconstructed, as distinct from its name?
2. Did the Scorpion-men at Māšu have a formal astronomical identification in a lost or unpublished commentary?
3. Which Babylonian Balance traditions directly influenced the Greek separation of Libra from the Claws?
4. What is the precise textual relationship between the surviving *Catasterismi* epitome, the fragmentary recension and the Orion scholia attributed to Hesiodic astronomy?
5. Which Scorpio correspondences belong to the earliest recoverable *Sefer Yetzirah* recension, and which entered through medieval commentaries?
6. Can the first printed occurrence of the fully standardized ♏ glyph be identified more narrowly than the Renaissance almanac tradition?
7. Who first formulated the scorpion–eagle–phoenix sequence explicitly as three evolutionary stages? Twentieth-century occult authors transmit it, but a secure first edition remains to be demonstrated.
8. Which astrologer was absolutely first to publish Pluto as ruler of Scorpio? Evidence clusters in 1930–1931, but periodical priority requires a complete bibliographic audit.
9. How consistently did European medical practitioners actually follow Scorpio surgical prohibitions, as opposed to copying tables?
10. Which individual Schifanoia Scorpio-decan figures derive from Abū Maʿshar, *Picatrix*, Pietro d’Abano or a lost intermediary?
11. Exact annual solar ingress and egress through IAU Scorpius should be calculated for a specified year, ephemeris and convention rather than copied as timeless dates.
12. A comprehensive corpus of Scorpio coins, cathedral cycles and manuscript images remains a worthwhile object-level project; generic symbol lists cannot substitute for catalogue records.
