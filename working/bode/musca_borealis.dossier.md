# Musca Borealis: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact** — directly attested in a surviving catalogue, globe, chart, manuscript, institutional record, or official proceeding.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern identification of historical stars, names, or intentions from coordinates and textual descriptions.
- **Tradition** — a transmitted cultural identification whose historical existence is documented, although its origin or exact stellar membership may be uncertain.
- **Disputed** — competing identifications or attributions exist.
- **Unverified claim** — often repeated, but not located in a sufficiently early or authoritative source during this research.
- **Myth/legend** — a narrative claim belonging to religious or mythographic tradition, not an astronomical historical proposition.
- **Modern invention** — a recent name, interpretation, or retrospective association.

## Basic identification

### Status

**ABOLISHED / NOT RETAINED BY THE IAU.**

More precisely, **Musca Borealis was omitted from the IAU’s canonical list of “principal constellations,” adopted at the first General Assembly in Rome in May 1922**. The IAU did not issue a separate decree saying “Musca Borealis is abolished.” The omission made it non-standard; Eugène Delporte’s boundaries, approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930, placed its entire former area inside **Aries**. Its stars are therefore now **33, 35, 39, and 41 Arietis**. [IAU history of the constellations](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [original 1922 list and historical explanation](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html); [Delporte, *Délimitation scientifique des constellations*](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ).

Because Musca Borealis was not retained:

- it has **no official IAU abbreviation**;
- it has **no official IAU genitive**;
- “Mus,” *Muscae*, belongs to the surviving southern constellation **Musca**, not to Musca Borealis;
- the grammatically regular historical genitive is **Muscae Borealis**, “of the Northern Fly,” but this is antiquarian, not official.

### Position

The figure occupied the faint quadrilateral or triangle-plus-one-star above the back or rump of Aries, near the modern Aries–Triangulum–Perseus region:

- **33 Arietis**
- **35 Arietis**
- **39 Arietis**
- **41 Arietis**

Ptolemy had not made these a separate figure. In the *Almagest* they belonged among Aries’s **ἀμόρφωτοι**, *amorphōtoi*, “unformed” or “external” stars, specifically the four over the Ram’s rump. The modern identifications are supported by coordinate comparison in modern critical reconstructions of the Ptolemaic catalogue. [Toomer’s *Almagest* translation, Aries catalogue](https://classicalliberalarts.com/resources/PTOLEMY_ALMAGEST_ENGLISH.pdf); [Peters and Knobel, *Ptolemy’s Catalogue of Stars*](https://starlinesofalmagest.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Almagest-Peters-Knobel-1.pdf).

### Principal names and etymologies

| Form | Meaning and linguistic note | Status and first secure use |
|---|---|---|
| **Apes** | Latin *apēs*, “bees,” nominative plural of *apis*; genitive *apium*. Sometimes modern writers incorrectly treat it as singular or translate it as “the Bee.” | **Documented:** Plancius globe, 1612/13; surviving 1649 reissue. |
| **Apis** | Latin singular, “bee,” genitive *apis*. | **Documented variant:** used by some derivative globes, including Habrecht. It is dangerously ambiguous because Bayer’s southern insect was also *Apis*. |
| **Vespa** | Latin “wasp,” genitive *vespae*. | **Documented:** Bartsch’s 1624 chart; his prose reportedly retains *Apes*. |
| **Lilium** | Latin “lily,” genitive *lilii*. The image is specifically the French royal fleur-de-lis. | **Documented:** Pardies, 1674; Royer, 1679. |
| **Fleur de Lis / le Lis** | French “lily” or heraldic fleur-de-lis. | **Documented:** French atlas tradition. |
| **Musca** | Latin “fly,” genitive *muscae*. | **Documented:** Hevelius, atlas prepared by 1687 and published in 1690; Bode also labels it *Musca*. |
| **Musca Borealis** | Latin “Northern Fly”; *borealis* derives from Greek Βορέας, Boreas, the north wind. | **Documented late form:** securely present in Jamieson, 1822. Earlier attribution to Hevelius or Bode is imprecise: their charts say *Musca*. |
| **Northern Fly** | English. | Nineteenth-century vernacular. |
| **la Mouche** | French, “the Fly.” | Documented in the Fortin/Flamsteed-derived French atlas tradition. |
| **die Fliege**, later **Nördliche Fliege** | German “the Fly,” “Northern Fly.” | Bode’s vernacular presentation uses *Fliege*; the qualified form is later usage. |
| **la Mosca / Mosca Boreale** | Italian “Fly / Northern Fly.” | Later vernacular. |
| **la Mosca Boreal** | Spanish “Northern Fly.” | Modern antiquarian Spanish. |
| **Abeja / Avispa / Lirio** | Spanish “bee / wasp / lily.” | Translations, not recovered as independent historical constellation names. |

No Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Hebrew, Persian, or Arabic culture is known to have called these stars a fly, bee, wasp, or lily. Names from those languages discussed below belong to **different cultural asterisms occupying the same stars**.

### Bode’s exact status

The brief’s phrase “Bode’s *Musca Borealis* (1801)” requires correction.

**Documented:** Bode’s 1801 plate labels the insect **MUSCA**, not demonstrably *Musca Borealis*, and draws it **inside the pictorial and cartographic domain of Aries**. The plate is generally catalogued as “Aries, Musca, Pisces Borealis and Pisces Australis.” [Bode plate description and image](https://www.georgeglazer.com/archives/maps/archive-celestial/bodecentaur.html); [ETH’s complete public-domain *Uranographia*](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148).

Bode’s atlas contains 20 enormous copperplate maps and a companion catalogue advertising **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters**, not “830 stars in 109 groups.” The unexplained “830 stars in 109 groups” wording in the brief could not be reconciled with Bode’s publication and may describe a particular museum, digital, or linked-data collection rather than the *Uranographia* itself. [Bode’s companion catalogue](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036).

## Stars and objects

### The four historical stars

Approximate modern data vary slightly by catalogue release.

| Historical letter | Modern designation | V magnitude | Type | Important names and notes |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| **d Muscae** | **33 Arietis** | 5.30–5.33 | A-type multiple/binary system | Flamsteed 33 Ari. No IAU proper name. |
| **a Muscae** | **35 Arietis** | 4.64–4.66 | B3 V, binary | Flamsteed 35 Ari. Chinese 胃宿一, *Wèi Xiù yī*, “First Star of Stomach.” |
| **b Muscae** | **39 Arietis** | 4.51–4.52 | K1.5 III red-clump giant | Flamsteed 39 Ari; official proper name **Lilii Borea**. Chinese 胃宿二. |
| **c Muscae** | **41 Arietis** | 3.61–3.63 | B8 Vn, multiple system | Brightest member; official proper name **Bharani**, specifically component 41 Ari Aa. Chinese 胃宿三. Suspected very-low-amplitude variability appears in some catalogues. |

The lowercase *a–d Muscae* letters are **historical constellation letters associated with Bode’s treatment**, not Bayer letters from Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria*. Bayer did not recognize the northern insect. Later tables sometimes misleadingly call these “Bayer designations.”

### 41 Arietis — Bharani

**Documented modern nomenclature:** the IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Bharani** for component **41 Arietis Aa** on 30 June 2017. It derived the name from the Indian lunar mansion **Bharaṇī**, not from the European fly. [IAU Working Group report](https://www.iau.org/static/science/scientific_bodies/working_groups/280/wg-starnames-triennial-report-2015-2018.pdf); [IAU star-name catalogue](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/naming_stars/).

Sanskrit **भरणी, Bharaṇī** is usually connected with the root *bhṛ*, “bear, carry, support”; translations include “the bearer.” The older Vedic form **Apa-bharaṇī / Apabhāraṇī**, conventionally “the bearers-away,” appears in the *Taittirīya* lists. Its presiding deity is **Yama**, lord of death and departed ancestors. Traditional iconography assigns it the **yoni**, the female generative organ. These are properties of the Indian mansion, not myths of Musca Borealis.

A nineteenth-century dispute concerned which of 35 or 41 Arietis was the mansion’s “junction star.” Whitney favored the positional testimony for 35 Ari despite 41 Ari being brighter and closer to the ecliptic. This remains a historical identification problem rather than a dispute over the three-star membership of the mansion. [Whitney’s notes accompanying the *Sūrya-Siddhānta*](https://classicalastrologer.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/surya_siddhanta_english.pdf).

### 39 Arietis — Lilii Borea

**Documented:** Lacaille’s 1757 *Astronomiae fundamenta* used **Līliī Boreā**, “on/in the northern [part] of the Lily,” for 39 Arietis and **Līliī Austrīnā**, “on/in the southern [part] of the Lily,” for 41 Arietis. The forms are compressed New Latin positional labels, not names from antiquity.

The IAU approved **Lilii Borea** as the modern proper name of 39 Arietis on 5 September 2017. Thus one surviving star name preserves the otherwise extinct *Lilium*. [IAU Working Group report](https://www.iau.org/static/science/scientific_bodies/working_groups/280/wg-starnames-triennial-report-2015-2018.pdf); [historical discussion of Lacaille’s tables](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Lilii_Borea).

The spelling **Lilli Borea**, frequently encountered online, is a modern misspelling. Latin *lilium* has one initial *l* followed by *ili-*; its genitive is *lilii*.

### Bayer and Flamsteed numbers

- None of the four has an original northern-fly Bayer Greek letter.
- All four have **Flamsteed Aries numbers: 33, 35, 39, 41 Arietis**.
- Historical Bode-style letters are normally reconstructed as **d, a, b, c Muscae**, respectively.
- Printed sources sometimes omit *d* for 33 Ari or count only the brighter triangle 35–39–41 Ari. This explains sources describing the figure as either **three** or **four** stars.

### Internal asterisms

- **European Musca/Apes/Vespa/Lilium:** four-star figure, although many renderings visually emphasize 35–39–41.
- **Bharaṇī:** 35, 39, and 41 Arietis.
- **Chinese Wèi, 胃, “Stomach”:** the same principal triangle, 35, 39, and 41 Arietis.
- **Athāfiyy:** Arabic “tripod-stones,” reported for 35, 39, and 41 Arietis in later compilations; see the cautions under Arabic tradition.
- Modern amateur maps sometimes preserve the group simply as the “Northern Fly” asterism. That usage has no official status.

### Deep-sky objects

**Finding:** no Messier, NGC, IC, Caldwell, or named nebular object can safely be assigned to Musca Borealis as a historical constellation. The four-star figure was never supplied with official boundaries, so “inside” it cannot be defined with Delporte-like precision.

Objects advertised as belonging to “Musca”—NGC 4372, NGC 4833, NGC 5189, the Engraved Hourglass Nebula, the Dark Doodad, and Nova Muscae 1991—belong to the **southern IAU constellation Musca**. Assigning them to Musca Borealis is a common search-engine and popular-reference error. [Modern southern Musca object list](https://www.constellationdirectory.org/constellations/musca.html).

Nor does any major deep-sky object appear to carry the specific name “Musca Borealis.”

### Variables, novae, and supernovae

- **41 Arietis** is listed in several compilations as a suspected variable, approximately V = 3.60–3.64. This is not a famous named variable and the variation is extremely small.
- 33 and 35 Arietis are multiple systems; multiplicity is not the same thing as photometric variability.
- No historical nova or supernova has been securely recorded **within the small Musca Borealis figure**.
- **Nova Muscae 1991** belongs to southern Musca and is unrelated.

### Meteor showers

No recognized shower is historically named **Musca Borealids**.

The **Daytime Arietids** have a radiant in the broader Aries region, often described in current observing literature as near 41 Arietis. This is an accidental modern positional proximity, not a survival of Musca Borealis. It would be anachronistic to rename them after the obsolete fly.

## Chronology of attestation

### Second century CE — Ptolemy

**Documented:** Ptolemy’s *Almagest* VII–VIII lists the relevant objects among the stars outside the formal figure of Aries. Four are described as lying over the rump. The identification with 41, 39, 35, and 33 Arietis is a **scholarly reconstruction** from the catalogue positions and descriptions.

Ptolemy therefore supplied the stellar material but neither insect nor lily. The boundary was anatomical and descriptive: stars could lie near a constellation without belonging to its pictured body.

### ca. 964 — al-Ṣūfī

**Documented:** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita* (“Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars”) transmitted and corrected the Ptolemaic constellation catalogue while also recording indigenous Arabic star lore. Its Aries chapter retained the four exterior stars over the rump.

No northern fly is present. Al-Ṣūfī therefore constitutes evidence **against** an ancient or medieval Islamic Musca Borealis. Numerous manuscripts survive, but some Aries leaves in individual copies are later replacements, so iconographic arguments must identify the manuscript and folio rather than citing “an al-Sufi manuscript” generically. [Survey of al-Ṣūfī manuscripts](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_manuscripts.htm); [Library of Congress Arabic manuscript](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%D9%83%D8%AA%D8%A7%D8%A8_%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%83%D9%88%D8%A7%D9%83%D8%A8_%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AB%D8%A7%D8%A8%D8%AA%D8%A9_%D9%84%D9%84%D8%B5%D9%88%D9%81%D9%8A_%D9%85%D8%AA%D8%A8%D9%88%D8%B9%D9%8B%D8%A7_%D8%A8%D8%A3%D9%82%D9%88%D8%A7%D9%84_%D9%85%D8%A3%D8%AB%D9%88%D8%B1%D8%A9_%D9%88%D8%B9%D8%AC%D8%A7%D8%A6%D8%A8_%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AE%D9%84%D9%82_%D9%84%D9%84%D9%82%D8%B2%D9%88%D9%8A%D9%86%D9%8A_(%D9%85%D9%83%D8%AA%D8%A8%D8%A9_%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%83%D9%86%D8%BA%D8%B1%D8%B3).pdf).

### 1515 — Dürer, Stabius, and Heinfogel

**Documented negative evidence:** Dürer’s printed celestial maps preserve the classical Ptolemaic sky. The four stars remain associated with Aries’s surroundings; no insect or lily appears. Dürer cannot be cited as an iconographic witness to Musca Borealis.

### 1603 — Bayer

**Documented negative evidence:** Bayer’s *Uranometria* does not contain Plancius’s northern bee, since Plancius introduced it later. Bayer does show a southern **Apis**, the ancestor of modern southern Musca.

This chronology is crucial: statements that Bayer mapped Musca Borealis are erroneous. Bayer’s bee is southern.

### 1612/13 — Petrus Plancius: Apes

**Earliest secure invention. Documented fact.**

Plancius placed **Apes**, “Bees,” on a small celestial globe of 1612 or 1613, published in Amsterdam by Pieter van den Keere. The surviving digital witness commonly used today is a **1649 reissue**, so “the surviving 1612 globe” is technically imprecise. [Digitized Plancius globe and provenance](https://www.meteo-maarssen.nl/lib_plancius_1612.html).

The plural may suggest several bees or a swarm, although the engraving is frequently summarized as one bee. No surviving authorial explanation establishing the symbolic reason has been found.

**Unverified:** claims that Plancius meant the bee as a specific biblical, Dutch, political, or commercial emblem. Plancius introduced several biblically flavored figures, but that does not prove a particular allegory for *Apes*.

### 1621 — Isaac Habrecht II

**Documented reception:** Habrecht’s globe/planisphere used **Apis** for the northern bee. Older astronomical historians sometimes credited Habrecht with inventing the constellation.

**Disputed attribution, now largely resolved:** Plancius’s earlier globe makes Habrecht a transmitter, not the inventor. The old attribution survives in nineteenth-century works because Plancius’s small globe was less accessible.

### 1624 — Jakob Bartsch: Vespa

**Documented:** Bartsch’s *Usus astronomicus planisphaerii stellati*, published at Strasbourg, labels the figure **Vespa**, “Wasp,” on the chart, while retaining **Apes** in the accompanying text. [Digitized Bartsch edition](https://www.meteo-maarssen.nl/lib_bartsch_1624.html); [atlas images and publication history](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/bartsch.htm).

This internal inconsistency is important. Bartsch may have been distinguishing the northern insect from Bayer’s southern *Apis*, but no explicit statement proving that motive was recovered.

Bartsch has often been credited with inventing Vespa and several other figures. Modern chart-history research instead treats him mainly as the publisher who disseminated Plancius’s inventions.

A later religious explanation links Bartsch’s bee/wasp to Samson finding honey in the lion’s carcass, Judges 14:8–9. That interpretation is associated with Bartsch’s Christianized explanations, but the precise wording should be checked in the 1624 Latin before it is quoted as a direct gloss on *Vespa*. It is **tradition or tentative interpretation**, not established motive.

### 1627 — Julius Schiller

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaced Aries with **St Peter, Prince of the Apostles**, *Sancti Petri Principis Apostolorum*. Because Schiller’s atlas follows and enlarges Bayer rather than Plancius’s 1612 globe, there is no separately developed Christian “bee” in this location. The four stars are absorbed into St Peter’s stellar field.

Schiller’s atlas was printed at Augsburg by Andreas Aperger; Matthias Kager designed the imagery, Lucas Kilian engraved it, and Johann Bayer collaborated. [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720); [Library of Congress copy](https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbc0001.2017rosen1317/?sp=1&st=gallery).

### 1660/61 — Cellarius

Cellarius’s *Harmonia Macrocosmica* helped preserve both traditional and Christianized stellar imagery. Modern asterism catalogues report **Apes** in this atlas tradition, but individual plate identification is essential because southern and northern insect names are readily confused.

### 1674 — Ignace-Gaston Pardies: Lilium

**Documented and frequently overlooked:** Pardies’s posthumously published *Globi coelestis in tabulas planas redacti descriptio* replaced the insect with **Lilium**, a heraldic fleur-de-lis, by 1674.

This predates Royer and is the earliest located printed chart of the lily. [National Széchényi Library’s historical survey](https://blog.oszk.hu/foldabrosz/csillagterkep-tortenet-4/).

### 1679 — Augustin Royer: Lilium

**Documented reception, disputed invention:** Royer’s *Cartes du ciel réduites en quatre tables*, Paris, 1679, shows **Lilium / Fleur de Lys**. Royer was *Architecte du Roi* and the emblem honored French monarchy, conventionally Louis XIV.

Royer is often named as the inventor, but Pardies’s 1674 appearance prevents a confident sole attribution. The safest formulation is: **Pardies first securely charts it; Royer independently used or popularized it five years later.** [Royer atlas description and plates](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/Royer%201679%20sud.htm).

### 1687/1690 — Johannes Hevelius: Musca

Hevelius’s atlas plates were substantially ready or dated by 1687; the complete *Prodromus Astronomiae* and *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* were published posthumously at Danzig in 1690 by Elisabeth Hevelius.

**Documented:** the chart calls the insect **Musca**, “Fly.” It is shown above/on Aries’s back.

**Crucial catalogue qualification:** Hevelius did **not** give Musca an independent catalogue section. Its four stars remain under Aries. This shows that the picture was less independent astronomically than a modern constellation entry implies. [Complete Hevelius scan](https://www.e-rara.ch/download/pdf/207924.pdf); [atlas description](https://meteo-maarssen.nl/lib_hevelius_1690.html).

### Eighteenth century — divided reception

- **Flamsteed:** the original *Atlas Coelestis* did not establish Musca Borealis as a major independent constellation.
- **Le Monnier, 1743:** returned to *la Mouche* in French mapping, following Hevelius rather than the lily.
- **Lacaille, 1757:** preserved *Lilium* in the positional names *Lilii Borea* and *Lilii Austrina*.
- **Fortin, 1776:** his reduced French edition derived from Flamsteed shows **la Mouche** beside *le Bélier*.
- **Bode, 1782:** *Vorstellung der Gestirne* includes **Fliege/Musca** in the Aries field.
- The lily continued sporadically in maps by Carel Allard and Corbinianus Thomas, but did not displace the fly permanently.

### 1801 — Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented:** Bode includes the fly on the Aries plate under **MUSCA**. The figure is a naturalistic winged fly perched above the Ram’s back, integrated into Aries’s field rather than enclosed by a modern legal boundary.

The atlas was published in Berlin “apud autorem,” by the author, with financial support named on the title page from Friedrich von Hahn. It consists of twenty large copperplates. Its companion German/French text and catalogue is the *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne / Description et connoissance générale des constellations*, listing 17,240 entries. [Marseille digital atlas](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035); [companion text](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036).

No separate Bode myth or dedication for Musca was recovered. The documented evidence suggests he was preserving Hevelian cartographic tradition, not inventing a new honorific constellation.

### 1807 — Thomas Young

Young’s *Course of Lectures on Natural Philosophy and the Mechanical Arts* lists or displays **the Fly**, evidence that the figure still appeared in educational astronomy.

### 1822 — Alexander Jamieson: Musca Borealis

Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* has a dedicated plate captioned **“Aries and Musca Borealis.”** This is the earliest secure appearance located for the full qualified name. The map was drawn for 1820 and published in London on 1 February 1822. [David Rumsey catalogue record and plate](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/view/all/what/Celestial/Atlas%2BMap/when/1822?sort=pub_list_no).

Jamieson claimed that his plan differed essentially from Bode’s, although institutional comparison finds that it followed Bode’s 1805 smaller atlas very closely. [Linda Hall Library on Jamieson](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/further-out/10-jamieson-alexander/).

### 1824/25 — *Urania’s Mirror*

Card 16 of *Urania’s Mirror, or A View of the Heavens*, engraved by Sidney Hall and published in London, is titled **“Aries and Musca Borealis.”** The fly hovers naturally over the Ram’s back. The cards derive heavily from Jamieson’s atlas while adding pinholes for illuminated viewing. [Image and catalogue description](https://www.gettyimages.dk/detail/news-photo/aries-and-musca-borealis-card-number-16-from-uranias-mirror-news-photo/1036139364).

### 1833–35 — Burritt

Elijah H. Burritt’s American educational atlas belongs to the popular pictorial-atlas tradition that continued many obsolete figures. Its 1835 edition survives in the American Museum of Natural History and David Rumsey collections. The figure’s inclusion should be verified plate-by-plate rather than assumed from its presence in Jamieson. [AMNH Burritt atlas](https://digitalcollections.amnh.org/archive/Celestial-map-from-Burritt-s-Atlas-designed-to-illustrate-the-geography-of-the-heavens-2URM1T14S658.html); [David Rumsey record](https://www.davidrumsey.com/maps5787.html).

### Mid-to-late nineteenth century — scientific abandonment

R. H. Allen reported that Musca Borealis survived in some popular works but was omitted by the more scientific atlases of **Argelander, Heis, and Klein** and was not recognized in the **British Association Catalogue**. This gradual abandonment substantially predates the IAU. [Allen, *Star-Names and Their Meanings*, p. 292](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Musca_Borealis%2A.html).

### 1922–1930 — standardization

- **1922, Rome:** the IAU adopts the principal-constellation list and abbreviations; Musca Borealis is absent.
- **1925, Cambridge:** Delporte proposes systematic boundaries.
- **1928, Leiden:** the boundary scheme is approved.
- **1930:** the tables and atlas are published; the former fly lies wholly in Aries.

The surviving southern Musca receives **Mus**, genitive **Muscae**. “Musca Australis” can consequently be shortened without ambiguity.

### 2017 — two names preserved

The IAU’s star-naming program formally approves:

- **Bharani** for 41 Arietis Aa;
- **Lilii Borea** for 39 Arietis.

Thus the European lily and Indian lunar mansion survive in official stellar nomenclature, while Musca Borealis itself does not.

## Invention and reception

### What the figure honored

#### Apes

No securely documented dedication is known. A bee could carry Christian, royal, civic, industrious, or natural-historical associations in early modern Europe, but assigning one without Plancius’s testimony would be speculative.

#### Vespa

Bartsch’s change may have differentiated the northern insect from Bayer’s southern *Apis*. This is a plausible **scholarly inference**, not an attested explanation.

The Samson-and-honey interpretation is a **Christianizing tradition** associated with Bartsch’s commentary. In Judges 14 the relevant creatures are bees in a lion’s carcass, not wasps; hence it cannot by itself explain the chart label *Vespa*.

#### Lilium

The fleur-de-lis is explicitly heraldic and French. In the Royer context it honored the French monarchy, normally identified with **Louis XIV**. Pardies’s prior use complicates any statement that Royer personally invented it as a new royal compliment.

#### Musca

No patron, voyage, instrument, monarch, or event is known to have been honored. Hevelius appears simply to have converted the bee/wasp into a fly.

### The doubled celestial insect

For a time the European sky contained two flies or two closely related insects:

1. the northern Plancius–Bartsch–Hevelius figure in Aries;
2. the southern *Apis/Musca*, derived from the Keyser–de Houtman southern star observations and mapped by Plancius/Bayer.

The northern name’s qualification as **Borealis** and the southern one’s as **Australis** were retrospective disambiguations. They were not born as a deliberately balanced pair.

Claims that Musca Borealis was “the only insect twice in the sky” are rhetorically effective but imprecise. At different dates the pair could be bee/bee, bee/wasp, fly/bee, or fly/fly, depending on the atlas. Other insect constellations were also proposed—John Hill’s *Scarabaeus*, for example—although they had even less acceptance.

### Why it failed

No IAU document gives a specific reason for rejecting Musca Borealis. The historical evidence suggests several cumulative causes:

- only three or four faint stars;
- complete overlap with an already recognized ancient constellation;
- no independent catalogue status even in Hevelius;
- unstable identity—bee, wasp, lily, fly;
- confusing duplication of southern Musca;
- declining use in serious nineteenth-century catalogues.

This is a **historical reconstruction**, not the language of an IAU verdict.

## Myths and legends

### No classical catasterism

**Documented negative finding:** Musca Borealis is early modern. There is no fly, bee, wasp, or lily catasterism for these four stars in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- the scholia to Aratus or Germanicus;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias.

Accordingly, no ancient source says who placed Musca Borealis in heaven or why. Modern websites that give it a Greek myth normally borrow the myth of **Aries**, the golden ram, or invent a story from general bee mythology.

### The golden ram beneath it

Because the stars remained Ptolemy’s external Aries stars, the ancient myths relevant to their *region* are Aries myths, not Musca myths.

**Myth — Phrixus and Helle.** Athamas’s children Phrixus and Helle escape sacrifice on a divinely supplied golden-fleeced ram. Helle falls into the Hellespont; Phrixus reaches Colchis, sacrifices the ram, and gives its fleece to Aeëtes. The ram is then catasterized in later astronomical mythography. Principal witnesses include Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 19; Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.20; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9.1; Ovid, *Fasti* 3.849–876 and *Metamorphoses* 7; and Hyginus, *Fabulae* 1–3.

**Variant — cloud or divine sender.** Nephele, the cloud-mother, commonly sends the ram; other retellings emphasize Hermes. The ram may speak, fly, or swim through the air depending on the source.

**Variant — its faintness and posture.** Astronomical mythographers explained the Ram’s backward-looking pose as gazing toward Helle, or connected its faint stars with the animal having shed its golden fleece. These explanations attach to Aries’s whole figure, not the four later fly stars.

### Samson’s bees

**Biblical story/tradition:** Judges 14:5–9 recounts Samson killing a lion and later finding a swarm of bees and honey in its carcass. His riddle—food from the eater and sweetness from the strong—follows in Judges 14:14.

This story is relevant only because Bartsch and related Christian uranographers allegorized constellations biblically. It is not a catasterism: Judges never places the bees in heaven, and no evidence was found that Plancius invented Apes specifically to represent them.

### Bharaṇī and Yama

**Indian religious-astrological tradition:** Bharaṇī is governed by Yama and associated with bearing, restraint, death, passage, and generation. Its yoni emblem and later astrological character derive from the nakṣatra tradition. This is a culturally independent interpretation of three of the same stars.

It is not evidence for a prehistoric Eurasian “fly” or “bee.” Similarity of stellar membership demonstrates reuse of a conspicuous triangle, not continuity of image.

### Chinese Stomach

**Chinese astronomical tradition:** 胃, *Wèi*, “Stomach,” is one of the twenty-eight lunar mansions and belongs to the White Tiger of the West. Its three stars are conventionally identified with 35, 39, and 41 Arietis. It was connected with granaries or the storage and digestion of grain in later celestial-administrative interpretation.

Again, this is not an insect myth and should not be collapsed into the history of Musca Borealis.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

The broader Aries region descends from the Mesopotamian **MUL.LÚ.ḪUN.GÁ**, “the Hired Man” or “Agrarian Worker,” later associated with the Ram. The shift toward a ram is sometimes reconstructed through pastoral associations and Dumuzi.

**Finding:** no secure cuneiform source was located that isolates exactly 33, 35, 39, and 41 Arietis as a named insect-like asterism. Claims of a direct Babylonian ancestor for Musca Borealis are unsupported.

### Egyptian

Popular works often associate Aries with ram-headed Amun, but the direct mapping of the classical constellation Aries to Amun is a Hellenistic or modern synthesis, not a securely demonstrated pharaonic identification of these four stars.

**Finding:** no Egyptian name or decan was securely traced to this exact group. “Coptic *Koleōn*, Belly or Scabbard,” occurs in Allen and derivative literature, but its philology and primary source were not independently established here. It should be treated as **disputed antiquarian tradition**.

### Greek

Ptolemy’s term is descriptive: the stars are **ἀμόρφωτοι**, outside the formal Aries figure. No collective Greek proper name for this quartet was located.

Greek **μυῖα, myia**, “fly,” was used in early-modern discussion of the *southern* insect. It is not an ancient Greek name for Musca Borealis.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī retained the Ptolemaic four stars above Aries’s rump; he did not name them as a fly.

The second Arabic lunar mansion **البطين, al-Buṭayn**, “the Little Belly,” traditionally belongs to a small group in Aries. But there is a significant identification dispute:

- most classical mansion authorities place al-Buṭayn among **δ, ε, and ρ/ζ Arietis**, nearer the Ram’s belly;
- some later European compilers equated it with the Musca/Bharaṇī triangle;
- al-Bīrūnī’s comparison of Bharaṇī and al-Buṭayn helped produce the conflation.

Thus the statement “the Musca Borealis stars formed al-Buṭayn” is **not safely universal**.

**Athāfiyy**, “tripod stones,” is reported by Allen for 35, 39, and 41 Arietis. It belongs to indigenous Bedouin visual lore: three stones supporting a cooking pot. The report is plausible, but Allen does not adequately expose the early Arabic textual chain on his Musca page, so exact antiquity remains open.

**Nāʾir al-Buṭayn**, “the Bright One of the Little Belly,” was attributed by al-Tīzīnī and tentatively identified by Allen with 41 Arietis. Because other placements of al-Buṭayn use a different triangle, this identification is **disputed**.

### Persian, Sogdian, and Khwarazmian

Allen, following nineteenth-century Orientalist mansion tables, gives:

- Persian **Pīš Parvīs**, approximately “Before the Pleiades”;
- Sogdian **Barv**;
- Khwarazmian **Farrankhand**, glossed “Forerunners.”

The group is said to include μ Arietis with the Musca stars. These names concern lunar-station systems and the group’s location before the Pleiades.

Their exact spellings, linguistic derivations, and star memberships vary in manuscripts and modern transliterations. They should be treated as **documented antiquarian reports requiring renewed primary-language verification**, not as settled proper names of 33–41 Arietis.

### Indian

The strongest non-Western identification is **Bharaṇī / Apabharaṇī**, normally 35, 39, 41 Arietis. It is the second nakṣatra in the common 27-mansion order, but becomes the “28th” in older tables beginning elsewhere or counting Abhijit. Thus sources calling it the “second” and the “twenty-eighth” need not describe different asterisms.

The mansion is attested in Vedic literature, including the *Taittirīya Saṃhitā* and *Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa*, long before Musca Borealis.

### Chinese

胃宿, **Wèi Xiù**, “Mansion of the Stomach”:

- 胃宿一 — 35 Arietis;
- 胃宿二 — 39 Arietis;
- 胃宿三 — 41 Arietis.

The identifications are associated with the Shi Shen star tradition and later Chinese catalogues. The group is one of the western-sector lunar mansions, under the White Tiger.

33 Arietis is not ordinarily part of the canonical three-star Wèi group.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No historical Hebrew name for the four-star insect was found.

Medieval Jewish astronomical works used Hebrew zodiac terminology for Aries, notably **טלה, Ṭaleh**, “lamb/ram,” and transmitted Arabic astronomy. Kabbalistic and later occult schemes map Aries to the Hebrew month Nisan, the tribe of Judah, the letter *he*, or other correspondences depending on the text.

**Finding:** no premodern Hebrew or Kabbalistic source was located that distinguishes the Musca quartet as bee, wasp, lily, or fly. Modern occult tables calling 39 Arietis “Musca Borealis” are retrospective Western fixed-star astrology.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No early alchemical or Hermetic commentary specifically on Musca Borealis was located.

Modern esoteric authors can associate flies with corruption, bees with the soul or industry, and lilies with purity or royalty. Those are free symbolic readings, not documented constellation tradition. No evidence links Bode’s fly to an alchemical operation, substance, or emblem.

### Astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* gives planetary natures for parts of Aries but does not single out these four external stars under later insect names.

None of the Musca Borealis stars belongs to the canonical list of fifteen **Behenian fixed stars**.

Modern fixed-star astrology assigns individual interpretations to “Apis” (33 Ari), “Lilium” (35 Ari), “Musca Borealis” (39 Ari), and “Bharani” (41 Ari). These labels are **modern inventions or retrospective catalogue names**, often propagated from Vivian Robson and online compilations. They should not be projected into Ptolemy, Arabic magic, or medieval Latin astrology.

The historically defensible astrological structures crossing the group are:

- Indian **Bharaṇī**;
- Chinese **Wèi**;
- possibly, but disputed in membership, Arabic **al-Buṭayn**.

## Boundary history

### Ptolemy

No closed boundaries. The four stars were outside the pictured Aries body but catalogued immediately after Aries as unformed stars.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer’s Aries plate retained the classical arrangement. The northern insect did not yet exist. Bayer letters assigned to Aries proper therefore do not constitute a Musca boundary.

### Plancius, 1612/13

The globe isolated the four exterior stars pictorially as Apes. Globe figures were not legal territorial divisions; overlap and interstitial placement remained possible.

### Bartsch, 1624

Vespa occupies the same approximate group. No rigorous boundary change occurred.

### Pardies and Royer, 1674–79

The same stars became the fleur-de-lis. Again the transformation was iconographic, not a coordinate annexation.

### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius drew Musca but catalogued the stars under Aries. In practical catalogue ownership, Aries never lost them.

### Lacaille and Bode

Lacaille’s names memorialized the Lily, while Bode’s plate drew Musca. Bode’s own cartography placed the fly inside Aries. The stars could therefore have simultaneous pictorial and catalogue identities.

### Delporte, 1930

Delporte’s official Aries polygon incorporates all four. Musca Borealis took no stars from Perseus or Triangulum under the modern system and lost none to them: its complete conventional membership became Aries.

The phrase “the stars went back to Aries” is historically apt but slightly simplified. Since Ptolemy, Hevelius, and Bode continued to catalogue them with Aries, in a strict catalogue sense they had never fully left.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas

The Farnese Atlas, a Roman marble generally dated to the second century CE and preserving an earlier Hellenistic celestial scheme, depicts Aries but no northern insect. Because individual faint stars are not mapped reliably enough to isolate this quartet, it is contextual evidence only.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

Aries appears in paired globe-view and sky-view illustrations. The four external rump stars may be plotted, but there is no fly. Manuscripts differ, and some Aries sections are later replacements; exact manuscript, date, repository, and folio must be specified.

### Dürer, 1515

Dürer’s northern and southern maps show the Ptolemaic sky. No insect appears over Aries.

### Bayer, 1603

No northern bee. Bayer’s **southern Apis** is the major source of later confusion.

### Plancius globe, 1612/13

The first known depiction, labeled **Apes**, occupies the small space above Aries. The accessible surviving witness is the 1649 reissue.

### Bartsch, 1624

The chart labels it **Vespa (Apes)**. Its small insect form is schematic because of the planisphere’s scale.

### Pardies, 1674

A stylized French fleur-de-lis replaces the insect.

### Royer, 1679

The fleur-de-lis is shown as a heraldic, not botanical, lily. Its political meaning is visually reinforced by the French royal context.

### Hevelius, 1687/1690

A naturalistic fly, **Musca**, appears over the Ram. Hevelius’s atlas uses the globe convention, so left and right are reversed relative to a direct sky view.

### Bode, 1801

Bode’s Musca is a delicately engraved naturalistic insect with translucent wings, apparently resting or hovering immediately above Aries’s woolly back. The visual nesting expresses what the catalogue already implies: Musca lies within Aries.

### Jamieson, 1822

Plate XIII, **“Aries and Musca Borealis,”** isolates both figures in a hand-colored, concave sky view. The qualified name is explicit.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, 1824/25

Card 16 reproduces the Ram and northern fly in a popular domestic format. Pinholes at star positions allowed the card to be held before a light. This is probably the most widely reproduced image of Musca Borealis today.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

**Finding:** no securely identified ancient coin, medieval cathedral portal, ceiling, or calendar depicts Musca Borealis, which did not yet exist. Images of bees, wasps, lilies, flies, or Aries in those media cannot be identified as this constellation without an accompanying stellar pattern or label.

Likewise, French coins and architecture bearing fleurs-de-lis are relevant to the emblem adopted for *Lilium*, not direct representations of the constellation.

## People, events, and works

### Petrus Plancius

A Dutch Reformed minister, cartographer, and promoter of overseas navigation. He introduced the northern Apes on his 1612/13 globe, but it was not derived from new exploration: the stars were already in Ptolemy.

### Jakob Bartsch

German astronomer, physician, and later Johannes Kepler’s son-in-law. His 1624 *Usus astronomicus* disseminated several Plancian constellations. He should not be credited uncritically as their inventor.

### Ignace-Gaston Pardies

French Jesuit scholar. His posthumous 1674 celestial charts provide the earliest located Lilium.

### Augustin Royer

Architect to the French king. His 1679 atlas repeated or popularized Lilium and introduced other royal imagery, including *Sceptrum et Manus Iustitiae*.

### Johannes and Elisabeth Hevelius

Johannes devised the Musca plate; Elisabeth completed and published the *Prodromus* complex after his death. Attribution simply to “Hevelius, 1690” can obscure her essential role as editor and publisher.

### Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille

His 1757 catalogue preserved the lily in *Lilii Borea* and *Lilii Austrina*. The surviving official name Lilii Borea therefore owes more directly to Lacaille’s stellar nomenclature than to Bode.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode preserved an exceptionally inclusive historical sky. His *Uranographia* contains more than one hundred figures, including many already marginal. Musca’s presence reflects this encyclopedic policy.

### Alexander Jamieson and Sidney Hall

Jamieson supplied the first secure full label *Musca Borealis*. Sidney Hall’s *Urania’s Mirror* carried it into popular visual culture.

### Navigation

No explorer or navigator is known to have steered by “Musca Borealis.” The stars are relatively faint and the figure was not internationally stable. 41 Arietis could serve incidentally as an Aries-region reference, but claims of Musca-based navigation are unverified.

### Literature, music, buildings, ships, and missions

No major poem, painting, composition, building, historical ship, or space mission was found that is securely named for **Musca Borealis** rather than southern Musca, the biological genus *Musca*, or the common fly.

Modern art prints and constellation merchandise reproduce Bode or *Urania’s Mirror*, but these are reception objects, not independent historical traditions.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Who invented it?

**Resolved in favor of Plancius, with qualification.** Older writers credited Bartsch or Habrecht because their printed works were more accessible. Plancius’s 1612/13 globe predates both.

### 2. Was Plancius’s name singular or plural?

The chart reads **Apes**, properly “bees.” “The Bee” is a convenient modern singularization. Some images may look like one insect, but the Latin form remains plural.

### 3. Did Bartsch call it Apes or Vespa?

Both. The chart uses **Vespa**; the text retains **Apes**. Statements giving only one erase an actual inconsistency in the primary work.

### 4. Who invented Lilium?

Frequently Royer, 1679; better evidence points to **Pardies, 1674**, with Royer as an early adopter or popularizer. Independent conception remains possible but unproved.

### 5. Did Hevelius call it Musca Borealis?

His chart says **Musca**. The full qualified name is securely found later. Saying Hevelius “invented Musca Borealis” compresses two historical stages.

### 6. Did Bode call it Musca Borealis?

The 1801 plate says **Musca** and places it with Aries. “Bode’s Musca Borealis” is acceptable as a retrospective identifier, not an exact quotation of his label.

### 7. Was it abolished in 1922 or 1930?

Both dates describe different stages, but neither should be dramatized as a special execution:

- **1922:** omitted from the standardized IAU list;
- **1928:** boundaries approved;
- **1930:** boundary tables published, assigning the area to Aries.

### 8. Three stars or four?

The full early-modern figure is normally reconstructed as 33, 35, 39, 41 Arietis. The brighter triangle 35–39–41 is the Indian Bharaṇī and Chinese Wèi. Later sources sometimes substitute the conspicuous triangle for the complete fly.

### 9. Al-Buṭayn or Bharaṇī?

They are not automatically the same. Bharaṇī’s identification with 35–39–41 is strong. Most Arabic authorities place al-Buṭayn farther south among different Aries stars. European tables that equate it with Musca probably reflect cross-system harmonization.

### 10. “Bayer letters”

The *a–d Muscae* labels are not original Bayer designations. Calling 35 Arietis “Alpha Muscae Borealis” can also be confused with **Alpha Muscae**, the modern southern star.

### 11. Lilii Borea spelling and meaning

- Correct: **Lilii Borea**.
- Common error: **Lilli Borea**.
- It means the northern star/part of Lilium, not “Northern Lily.”
- It is now officially attached to 39 Arietis.

### 12. Confusion with southern Musca

This is the largest continuing error source. Nova Muscae 1991, NGC 5189, NGC 4833, the Dark Doodad, and Alpha Muscae are all southern. Database searches that omit *Borealis* routinely merge the two.

### 13. “Only insect twice in the sky”

This is a modern epigram, not a historical rule. At some moments two similarly named insects did coexist, but their exact names changed and other proposed insect constellations existed.

### 14. Hoaxes and fabricated mythology

The dedicated contested-material searches located no famous historical hoax concerning Musca Borealis. The more common problem is **synthetic mythology**: modern pages attach the golden-ram story, generic bee symbolism, Amun, or alchemical fly lore directly to the northern fly without an early source.

### 15. “830 stars in 109 groups”

This claim could not be traced to Bode’s *Uranographia*. Bode’s documented companion catalogue claims 17,240 objects, and the atlas depicts more than one hundred constellations. “830 stars” more closely resembles the scale of ancient naked-eye catalogues and may be metadata from a modern collection. It should not be quoted as a property of Bode’s 1801 work without a separate source.

## Open questions

1. What exact wording does Bartsch use when connecting *Apes/Vespa* to biblical symbolism, and does he explicitly cite Samson?
2. Is Pardies’s 1674 Lilium demonstrably the direct source of Royer’s 1679 figure, or did both draw on a third French cartographic design?
3. What is the earliest occurrence of the exact binomial **Musca Borealis** before Jamieson? Lacaille’s use of *Musca Australis* creates the conceptual distinction, but a secure earlier northern label remains to be found.
4. Which globe maker first assigned the complete sequence *a–d Muscae* to 35, 39, 41, and 33 Arietis?
5. What primary Persian, Sogdian, Khwarazmian, and Coptic texts underlie Allen’s forms *Pish Parvis*, *Barv*, *Farrankhand*, and *Koleōn*?
6. Which early Arabic authority first equated al-Buṭayn with the Bharaṇī/Musca triangle, contrary to the more usual δ–ε–ρ/ζ Arietis placement?
7. Did every edition of Burritt’s atlas retain the fly, or only particular derived plates?
8. What external collection or project is meant by “the Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)”?
