# Pegasus: Research Dossier

> **Status — documented fact.** Pegasus was retained when the International Astronomical Union standardized 88 constellation names and abbreviations in 1922. Its official boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published by Eugène Delporte in 1930. Its modern abbreviation is **Peg** and its Latin genitive **Pegasi**. It was not abolished. Under Delporte’s boundaries, the former shared star δ Pegasi/α Andromedae—Alpheratz—belongs wholly to **Andromeda**.  
> [IAU constellation list and boundary history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

## Basic identification

### Name and meaning

**Pegasus** is Latin *Pegasus*, from Greek **Πήγασος, Pḗgasos**, the name of the mythic horse.

- **Ancient etymology/tradition:** Hesiod, *Theogony* 282–286, derives the name from Greek **πηγή / πηγαί**, “spring(s),” because Pegasus was born beside the springs of Ocean. This is the first surviving explicit etymology.
- **Modern philological judgment—disputed:** the Hesiodic derivation is often considered folk etymology. The suffix and the myth’s Anatolian setting have prompted proposals of a pre-Greek or Anatolian origin.
- **Scholarly hypothesis:** comparison with Luwian **Pihaššašši**, a storm deity, has been proposed because Pegasus eventually carries Zeus’s lightning. This is suggestive, not a demonstrated linguistic derivation.
- **Obsolete speculation:** Samuel Bochart’s Phoenician/Hebrew compound *pag/pega* “bridle” + *sūs* “horse” (“bridled horse”), repeated in nineteenth-century star lore, has no standing in current historical linguistics.
- **Unverified claim:** an Egyptian derivation from alleged *pgw*, “washing vessel” or “pitcher,” cannot be traced to a secure Egyptian lexical or astronomical source.

Relevant texts: [Hesiod, *Theogony*](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodTheogony.html); [Etymology discussion](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Pegasus); [French Academy, *Pégase*](https://www.dictionnaire-academie.fr/article/A9P1210).

### Bode’s labels and place in the atlas

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode’s Berlin *Uranographia* (1801) labels the figure **PEGASUS** in Latin. In German accompanying works it is *Pegasus* or *das geflügelte Pferd*. The horse appears with wings and only its forepart, in the inherited pose, head toward Enif and body emerging from the eastern edge of the Great Square. It shares its plate with **Delphinus** and **Equuleus**.

Bode’s atlas consists of large copper-engraved maps. The atlas proper is overwhelmingly graphical, not a sequence of mythographical essays. Its explanatory and numerical companion is the *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne …* (1801), cataloguing 17,240 stars, doubles, nebulae and clusters. Thus claims that *Uranographia* contains a long prose “description of Pegasus” confuse the atlas with its companion catalogue.

The cited “830 stars in 109 groups” appears to be collection-level or exhibition metadata, not Bode’s count for Pegasus. It could not be verified in Bode’s title, index, or companion catalogue and should not be quoted as a Pegasus statistic without the unidentified collection record.

Digital witnesses: [ETH Zürich facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [NYPL Bode collection](https://old-digitalcollections.nypl.org/collections/joannis-elerti-bode-uranographia-sive-astrorum-descriptio-viginti-tabulis-aeneis); [Marseille catalogue and description](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035).

### Figure and boundaries

The recognizable figure is a horse’s head, neck, forelegs, chest and wings. It has no hindquarters. Aratus, *Phaenomena* 205–224, already says that only the head and forepart appear, as though the remainder rises from the ocean. Ptolemy similarly catalogues the **Horse**, not an anatomically complete animal.

**Important correction:** Ptolemy’s catalogue name and star descriptions do not make the figure wingless in the sense of proving that he rejected wings. His catalogue describes stars anatomically, including stars associated with the forebody, head and legs, but his surviving mathematical text is not an illustrated atlas. Later manuscript pictures vary. The assertion “Ptolemy drew Pegasus without wings” is therefore misleading: Ptolemy supplied no securely surviving authorial drawing. The safer statement is that he called it **Hippos**, catalogued only the front half, and did not expressly make wings necessary to its definition.

#### Boundary sequence

- **Eudoxus/Aratus, fourth–third centuries BCE:** no boundary polygons; a stellar outline called the Horse.
- **Ptolemy, c. 150 CE:** twenty principal stars assigned to the Horse plus “unformed” stars around it. Alpheratz is anatomically “the star on the navel” of the Horse but also participates in Andromeda.
- **Bayer, 1603:** twenty-three lettered positions, α through ψ. Alpheratz received the dual designation **δ Pegasi** and **α Andromedae**. Bayer probably treated π as one unresolved position.
- **Flamsteed, catalogue 1725/atlas 1729:** numbered 89 Pegasi stars. His 6 and 11 Pegasi lie in modern Aquarius. He resolved or separately numbered stars which Bayer’s chart had combined.
- **Hevelius, 1690:** preserved the broad traditional image rather than imposing exclusive territorial boundaries.
- **Lacaille:** did not materially redraw northern Pegasus; his principal innovations concerned the southern sky.
- **Bode, 1782 and 1801:** expanded the plotted stellar population, accepted π¹/π² as separate, retained δ Pegasi/α Andromedae as shared, and depicted the classical fore-half with elaborate wings.
- **Argelander and Heis, nineteenth century:** increasingly schematic star mapping reduced the authority of pictures, but still lacked the modern exclusive polygons.
- **IAU/Delporte:** the 1922 list kept Pegasus; Delporte’s B1875 right-ascension/declination polygon was approved in 1928 and printed in 1930. Precession makes these originally straight coordinate segments appear slightly tilted on J2000 charts.

Modern neighbors are **Andromeda, Lacerta, Cygnus, Vulpecula, Delphinus, Equuleus, Aquarius**, and **Pisces**. Pegasus covers about **1,121 square degrees**, the seventh-largest official constellation, with a 35-segment boundary.

The chief loss was **Alpheratz** to Andromeda. Stars formerly numbered **6 and 11 Pegasi** fall in Aquarius. Conversely, the territorial constellation now includes many telescopic objects that were never components of the old pictured horse.

## Stars and objects

### The Great Square

The **Great Square of Pegasus** consists of:

- **α Pegasi, Markab**
- **β Pegasi, Scheat**
- **γ Pegasi, Algenib**
- **α Andromedae, Alpheratz**

It remains a valid asterism even though its northeastern corner is officially outside Pegasus.

The Square was not named “the Great Square” in classical antiquity. That descriptive English name became standard in practical modern astronomy. It overlaps, but is not necessarily identical in every reconstruction, with the Babylonian Field and the Arab bucket.

### Alpheratz: the borrowed corner

**α Andromedae**, Flamsteed **21 Andromedae**, is **Alpheratz** or **Sirrah**:

- **Alpheratz** derives from Arabic **سرة الفرس, surrat al-faras**, “the horse’s navel.”
- **Sirrah** is a shortened form of *surra*, “navel.”
- It was Bayer’s **δ Pegasi** and **α Andromedae**.
- Ptolemy’s anatomical descriptions made it relevant to both figures.
- Delporte placed it in Andromeda; δ Pegasi is now obsolete.

Calling Alpheratz “stolen” by Andromeda is popular rhetoric. Exclusive boundary assignment was a practical requirement of modern cataloguing, not an adjudication of mythic ownership.

### Other notable stars and systems

- **51 Pegasi**, Flamsteed 51, is a Sun-like G-type star. In 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz announced **51 Pegasi b**, the first planet discovered around a normal main-sequence, Sun-like star. Earlier pulsar planets were already known; therefore “first exoplanet ever” is false. The planet is now officially named **Dimidium**.
- **HD 209458** hosts the transiting hot Jupiter Osiris; its atmosphere became a foundational target in exoplanet spectroscopy.
- **HR 8799** hosts the first multi-planet system directly imaged.
- **IK Pegasi** is an A star–white dwarf binary. It is often called a “future Type Ia supernova candidate,” but whether and how it can reach explosion conditions remain evolutionary-model questions; it is not “about to explode.”
- **γ Pegasi/Algenib** is a β Cephei/SPB-type pulsator.
- **β Pegasi/Scheat** is a semiregular red giant.
- **AG Pegasi** is a symbiotic binary whose nineteenth–twentieth-century outburst was extraordinarily slow; later behavior has complicated the label “slowest nova.”
- **R Pegasi** is a Mira variable.
- **RU Pegasi** is a dwarf nova.
- **TX Pegasi** is a red carbon star.
- **V376 Pegasi and LQ Pegasi** are cataclysmic/nova-like variables.
- No securely identified naked-eye historical supernova is recorded as having occurred in the modern Pegasus polygon. Modern extragalactic supernovae have repeatedly been found in its numerous galaxies.

### Deep-sky objects

#### Messier 15 / NGC 7078

**M15**, near Enif, is a dense Galactic globular cluster. Jean-Dominique Maraldi discovered it in 1746; Charles Messier catalogued it in 1764. It has a core-collapse structure and contains **Pease 1**, the first planetary nebula identified in a globular cluster.

#### Galaxies and groups

- **NGC 7331:** bright, nearly edge-on unbarred spiral, discovered by William Herschel in 1784. Its visual companions form the “Deer Lick Group,” mostly background galaxies rather than a physical compact group.
- **Stephan’s Quintet:** **HCG 92**, containing NGC 7317, 7318A, 7318B, 7319, and the foreground interloper NGC 7320. Édouard Stephan discovered the grouping in 1877. Four principal galaxies form an interacting physical group; NGC 7320 is much nearer.
- **NGC 7479:** barred spiral, sometimes called the “Propeller Galaxy.”
- **NGC 7814:** edge-on spiral nicknamed the “Little Sombrero.”
- **NGC 7742:** face-on ring galaxy.
- **Pegasus Dwarf Irregular Galaxy / DDO 216:** Local Group dwarf, discovered photographically by Albert G. Wilson in the mid-twentieth century.
- **Pegasus I cluster:** galaxy cluster centered broadly around NGC 7619/7626.
- **Pegasus III and Pegasus IV:** modern ultra-faint Milky Way satellites found in wide-field survey data; their names refer to sky position, not mythological membership.
- **Pegasus II/Sextans C:** historical survey naming is inconsistent; caution is necessary because “Pegasus dwarf” can denote different systems in popular summaries.

#### Nebulae and remnants

Pegasus is galaxy-rich but poor in bright emission nebulae. Objects carrying “Pegasus” are generally named by location rather than physical connection. **SNR G70.0−21.5** is an exceptionally high-latitude Galactic supernova remnant projected in Pegasus. Popular captions assigning it an exact age such as “90,000 years” should be treated as model-dependent, not a historical observation.

### Meteor showers

- **July Pegasids, IAU shower 175 JPE:** a weak, fast northern shower, usually active in early to mid-July, with a radiant in southern Pegasus. Recent calendars give a peak near July 10–12, speed about 63–64 km/s and low ZHR.
- Its parent has been proposed as comet **C/1979 Y1 (Bradfield)**, but association remains less secure than those of major showers.
- Historical catalogues also contain minor or provisional **Pegasid** radiants. A radiant’s name can persist while precession or revised clustering changes its formal constellation position.

[International Meteor Organization calendar](https://www.imo.net/files/meteor-shower/cal2026.pdf); [JPL meteor-stream table](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sb/meteor_streams.html).

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the Greek Horse

**Middle second millennium BCE—documented text, reconstructed stars.** The Boghazköy star list includes **AŠ.IKU/IKU**, “Field.” Later Babylonian material, especially *MUL.APIN* in its first-millennium recension, locates the Field in this sector. Older books routinely equated IKU with the four modern Square stars. More recent philological reconstruction warns that the original Field may have used **α, ψ, 72 and 78 Pegasi**, with Markab at an eastern corner, rather than the modern Square exactly.

**Babylonian Horse—documented name, reconstructed identification.** **MUL.ANŠE.KUR.RA**, “Horse,” occupied stars now assigned to the arms/upper body of Pegasus, probably including β Pegasi and η–μ Pegasi. **γ Pegasi** also belonged to Anunitu or the “Tails” connected with the ancestor of Pisces. Therefore no single Mesopotamian Pegasus existed; several native figures partitioned the area.

There is no demonstrated Egyptian antecedent for the entire Horse. Claims that Pegasus encoded the Nile’s seasonal closing or the end of navigation originate in early-modern comparative speculation, not Egyptian star catalogues.

### Archaic Greece: the mythic animal

**c. 700 BCE—Hesiod, documented myth.** *Theogony* 270–294 gives the earliest surviving literary genealogy. Medusa lay with Poseidon; when Perseus severed her head, Chrysaor and Pegasus emerged. Pegasus is named from Ocean’s springs. He flew away and thereafter dwelt with Zeus, carrying thunder and lightning. Hesiod does not mention Bellerophon here.

Whether Hesiod imagined visible wings is debated. His verb describes Pegasus flying away, but explicit wing terminology becomes clearer in later art and poetry.

**Sixth century BCE—material evidence.** Pegasus appears on Corinthian silver staters from approximately 550–540 BCE. The winged horse and Corinthian koppa establish his civic association with Corinth independently of surviving literary accounts. The Corfu Artemis temple pediment, early sixth century BCE, shows Medusa with Pegasus and Chrysaor.

**476 BCE—Pindar, first full surviving taming narrative.** *Olympian* 13.63–92 tells how Bellerophon struggled to capture Pegasus at Peirene. Athena appeared in a dream, gave him a golden bridle, and received an altar as Athena Hippia/Chalinitis. With Pegasus he defeated the Chimera, Amazons and Solymi. This is the earliest surviving detailed account tying Athena, the bridle and Peirene to the capture.

### The stellar Horse

**Fourth century BCE—Eudoxus, reconstructed.** Eudoxus’s lost *Phaenomena* is the principal source behind Aratus. A fore-half Horse was probably already present, but the surviving evidence does not establish that Eudoxus named it Pegasus.

**c. 275 BCE—Aratus, documented astronomical figure.** *Phaenomena* 205–224 calls it simply **Hippos**, “Horse.” It lies beneath Andromeda; its navel is marked by a star shared with her head, and only the upper half rises from the sea. A spring is said to flow from its hoof. Aratus does not clearly identify it with Bellerophon’s winged Pegasus.

This silence is critical. The stellar Horse and mythological Pegasus were not necessarily identical at the earliest recoverable astronomical stage.

### Catasterism tradition

**Third century BCE original, later epitome—Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 18.** The surviving work is an epitome and derivative witnesses, not Eratosthenes’ autograph.

Its principal versions say:

1. The Horse is Pegasus, born from Medusa and Poseidon.
2. He opened Hippocrene with his hoof.
3. He flew to the stars or was placed there.
4. A competing account identifies the figure with **Melanippe/Hippe**, daughter of Chiron, transformed into a mare and set among the stars.
5. A counterargument noted in the tradition says the stellar animal cannot be Pegasus because it has no represented wings.
6. The figure’s hindquarters are absent.

The text’s internal contradiction—identifying Pegasus while preserving objections—is evidence of compilation. Textual critics have suspected that an explicit concluding Pegasus identification entered one branch later.

### Roman reception

**First century BCE—Cicero.** In his Latin *Aratea*, Cicero translates the constellation as **Equus**, Horse, not necessarily Pegasus.

**First century BCE—Vitruvius, *De architectura* 9.** Uses **Equus** in his survey of constellations.

**First century BCE/CE—Manilius, *Astronomica* 1.355–360.** Uses **Equus**. Manilius does not clearly call it Pegasus, a useful warning against retrojecting the mythological name into every astronomical source.

**Early first century CE—Germanicus, *Aratea*.** Uses poetic names including **Sonipes** and, in transmitted passages, Pegasus. His poem and its scholia helped join the astronomical Horse to Pegasus in Latin manuscript culture.

**Ovid, c. 8 CE.** In *Metamorphoses* 4.786–5.7, Pegasus and Chrysaor spring from Medusa’s blood; Perseus reaches the Muses, and Urania explains that Hippocrene was made by Pegasus’s hoof. *Fasti* 3.449–458 refers to the Horse constellation and Hippocrene. Ovid does **not** make Pegasus Perseus’s mount during Andromeda’s rescue: Perseus flies with winged sandals.

**Pseudo-Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.18, probably first–second century CE.** Gives the fullest ancient catalogue of variants:

- “Aratus and many others” call the sign Pegasus, child of Neptune and Medusa.
- Pegasus opened Hippocrene on Helicon.
- Bellerophon rode him against the Chimera after Proetus sent the hero to Iobates.
- After victory Bellerophon tried to fly to heaven, looked down, became terrified, fell and died or wandered.
- Pegasus continued upward and was placed among the constellations by Jupiter.
- Others say the figure is Melanippe/Hippe, daughter of Chiron, concealed in horse form after pregnancy by Aeolus.
- Euripides’ lost *Melanippe* tradition is invoked.
- The missing hindquarters preserve modesty or conceal her sex.
- The figure’s wings create a difficulty for the Hippe interpretation in some versions.

**Pseudo-Hyginus, *Fabulae* 57, 157, 250–251 and related entries.** Scatters the same genealogy, Chimera and Bellerophon material across mythographical lists. The *Fabulae* have a difficult transmission and should not automatically be assigned to Augustus’s librarian Hyginus.

**Ptolemy, c. 150 CE, *Almagest* 7–8.** Catalogues **Hippos** among the 48 figures, with twenty principal stars. The work canonized its astronomical geometry but does not supply a catasterism.

### Late antiquity

**Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 2.3.1–2.3.2, first or second century CE.** Bellerophon, falsely accused by Stheneboea/Anteia, is sent to Iobates and ordered against the Chimera. He mounts Pegasus, offspring of Medusa and Poseidon. The account does not make Perseus Pegasus’s rider.

**Pausanias, second century CE.**

- *Description of Greece* 2.4.1: Corinthian Bellerophon and Pegasus traditions.
- 2.31.9: at Troezen, water was said to have sprung when Pegasus struck the ground.
- 5.19.5: the Chest of Cypselus depicted Bellerophon and Pegasus fighting the Chimera.
- 9.31.3: Hippocrene on Helicon was created by Pegasus’s hoof.
- 2.1.9: an image of Bellerophon on Pegasus at the fountain of Peirene.

Pausanias documents local cult, monuments and etiologies rather than one harmonized biography.

**Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*, fifth century CE.** Repeatedly uses Pegasus as a poetic emblem of flight, Medusan birth and the Muses. Nonnus 24.270–end elaborates Hippocrene and poetic inspiration. The often-repeated statement that Pegasus served Zeus as literal thunderbolt courier rests most securely on Hesiod’s “house of Zeus” and thunder/lightning passage; later writers amplify his Olympian service, but not every thunderbolt scene in Nonnus names Pegasus.

### Islamic and medieval transmission

**c. 964—ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sufi, *Book of the Fixed Stars*.** Al-Sufi calls it **al-Faras al-Aʿẓam**, the Greater Horse, translates and corrects Ptolemy, and supplies the standard pair of mirror images: as seen on a globe and as seen in the sky. Manuscripts usually show the fore-half winged horse. The star names in Islamic catalogues combine translated Greek anatomy with indigenous Arab asterisms.

**Twelfth century—Gerard of Cremona.** His Latin *Almagest*, translated from Arabic, transmitted the Horse’s catalogue to western Europe. Forms derived from *al-faras* appear alongside Latin *Equus* and *Pegasus*.

**Carolingian through later medieval Aratea.** Manuscripts of Germanicus, Cicero and Avienus show Pegasus/Horse in varying poses. Wings are common but not universal. Text and picture could disagree because illustrations inherited separate exemplars.

### Printed atlases

**1515—Albrecht Dürer.** Dürer’s northern celestial map, made with Conrad Heinfogel and Johannes Stabius at Nuremberg, shows the classical Horse from the globe-maker’s external viewpoint. It helped stabilize Renaissance printed iconography.

**1603—Johann Bayer, *Uranometria*.** Plate XIX is headed **PEGASUS** and lists Greek **Ἵππος**, Latin **Equus**, and Arabic-derived **Alpheras** among synonyms. Bayer assigned Greek letters, including δ to Alpheratz while also calling it α Andromedae.

**1627—Julius Schiller.** In *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Augsburg, Pegasus becomes **Sanctus Gabriel Archangelus**, the Archangel Gabriel. The constellation’s stars were retained while pagan imagery was replaced. Schiller’s engravers were Lucas Kilian and collaborators; Bayer contributed astronomical work. The system never displaced conventional nomenclature.  
[Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/); [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720).

**1660—Andreas Cellarius.** *Harmonia Macrocosmica* reproduced both pagan and Schillerian Christian skies, giving Gabriel a secondary afterlife.

**1690—Johannes Hevelius.** The posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*, published at Gdańsk by Elisabeth Hevelius, depicts Pegasus externally as on a celestial globe. The anatomical orientation therefore reverses compared with sky-view maps.

**1729—Flamsteed.** *Atlas Coelestis* retained the full allegorical horse and greatly enlarged the stellar catalogue.

**1763/1776—Fortin’s French reduction of Flamsteed.** Made the imagery affordable and portable; Bode’s 1782 atlas grew directly from this tradition.

**1782—Bode, *Vorstellung der Gestirne*.** A German, corrected and expanded adaptation of the Fortin/Flamsteed atlas.

**1801—Bode, *Uranographia*.** The high point and near endpoint of monumental pictorial uranography: **PEGASUS**, winged, half-emergent, densely surrounded by catalogue stars.

**1822—Alexander Jamieson, *Celestial Atlas*.** Simplified but retained the winged fore-half. His images were a major source for *Urania’s Mirror*.

**c. 1824—*Urania’s Mirror*.** The London educational card for Pegasus combines pierced star holes with Pegasus, Equuleus and Delphinus. The cards often borrow Jamieson while altering pose and decoration.

**1833 onward—Elijah Burritt.** *Geography of the Heavens* and its atlas popularized the Great Square for American students.

**Mid-nineteenth century—Argelander and Heis.** Scientific atlases increasingly omitted or faintly subordinated the mythic figure, preparing the transition from picture to coordinate-defined area.

**1922–1930—IAU.** Pegasus was kept; its territory was made exclusive and Alpheratz assigned to Andromeda.

## Classical myths and legends

### Birth from Medusa

**Myth; first surviving attestation c. 700 BCE.** Hesiod, *Theogony* 270–286, says Poseidon coupled with Medusa in a meadow; when Perseus severed her head, Pegasus and Chrysaor sprang out. Pegasus’s name is linked to Ocean’s springs. Later writers differ on whether the children emerged from Medusa’s neck, blood, or severed body: Pindar, *Olympian* 13; Euripidean fragments; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4.784–786; Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.2; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 151.

### Pegasus and Bellerophon

**Myth; securely attested by Pindar in 476 BCE.** Bellerophon desires Pegasus but cannot master him. Athena appears in a dream and supplies a golden bridle; he captures the horse at Peirene. Pindar emphasizes divine favor and Corinthian ritual. Later prose sometimes has the seer Polyidus advise Bellerophon to sleep at Athena’s altar.

### Anteia/Stheneboea and the false accusation

**Myth; archaic core in Homer, fuller versions later.** Homer, *Iliad* 6.155–205, tells Bellerophon’s story but does not name Pegasus: Proetus’s wife Anteia desires him, accuses him when rejected, and causes him to be sent to Lycia carrying fatal signs. Pindar and later mythographers graft Pegasus onto this older heroic cycle. Hyginus uses Antia or Stheneboea; Pseudo-Apollodorus uses Stheneboea.

### The Chimera

**Myth; Bellerophon’s feat first attested in Homer, Pegasus added explicitly by later tradition.** The Chimera is lion, goat and snake, breathing fire. Homer has Bellerophon kill it “trusting in signs of the gods.” Pindar and later art make Pegasus essential: flight allows attack from above. Hesiod, *Theogony* 319–325, says Pegasus and Bellerophon killed her.

### Solymi, Amazons and Lycian ambush

**Myth; Homeric.** After the Chimera, Iobates sends Bellerophon against the Solymi and Amazons and places Lycian warriors in ambush. Homer does not mention Pegasus in these actions; Pindaric and later retellings often presume him.

### Stheneboea’s punishment

**Variant myth, chiefly later and fragmentary.** In Euripides’ lost *Stheneboea*, reconstructed from fragments and summaries, Bellerophon may take Stheneboea aloft on Pegasus and cast her into the sea near Melos. This episode must not be presented as part of Homer’s account.

### Attempted ascent to Olympus

**Myth; classical poetic tradition, fully narrated in Pindaric and Hyginian reception.** Bellerophon becomes overambitious and tries to ride Pegasus to Zeus’s dwelling. He is thrown and thereafter wanders alone. Pindar, *Isthmian* 7.44–48 and *Olympian* 13, supplies the warning that winged Pegasus cast off Bellerophon when he wished to enter heaven. Hyginus *Astronomica* 2.18 gives a rationalizing variant: looking down terrifies him and he falls.

### The gadfly

**Later tradition, weakly attested in extant classical sources.** The familiar tale that Zeus sent a gadfly to sting Pegasus, causing Bellerophon’s fall, is ubiquitous in modern mythographies but difficult to trace to the major surviving Greek and Roman accounts requested here. Pindar says Pegasus threw him; Hyginus uses vertigo. The gadfly should therefore be labeled a later elaboration unless a specific ancient scholion is cited.

### Hippocrene

**Myth; stellar hint in Aratus, explicit in Eratosthenic/Hyginian and Ovidian tradition.** Pegasus strikes Helicon and produces **Hippocrene**, “Horse’s Spring.” Aratus 216–223 calls the water Hippos’s spring; Pseudo-Eratosthenes 18 and Hyginus 2.18 name Pegasus; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 5.250–268, has Urania show it to Minerva. Pausanias 9.31.3 records the Boeotian local tradition.

### Why Pegasus struck Helicon

**Variant tradition.** Some accounts merely say his hoof opened the spring. A later version says Helicon swelled upward in delight at the Muses’ song until Poseidon ordered Pegasus to strike it, checking the mountain. This developed etiological tale is more fully preserved in late mythographical and poetic sources than in the early catasterisms.

### Peirene and Troezen springs

**Local tradition.** Pegasus drinks at Peirene in Corinth when captured. Pausanias 2.31.9 separately reports that the spring Hippocrene at Troezen—distinct from the famous Boeotian one—was made by Pegasus’s hoof. Later retellings sometimes collapse Peirene, Troezen and Helicon into one spring.

### The Muses and poetic inspiration

**Later literary symbolism.** Because Hippocrene belonged to the Muses, Pegasus became the horse of poetry. Ancient sources establish the spring connection, but the stock emblem “mount Pegasus = write poetry” flourished particularly in Roman, Renaissance and early-modern literature. The French word is documented in literary usage from the sixteenth century; Ronsard helped spread it.

### Servant of Zeus and bearer of thunder

**Myth; first surviving attestation in Hesiod.** *Theogony* 284–286 says Pegasus left earth for Zeus’s house and “brings” thunder and lightning to Zeus. This makes the thunderbolt role genuinely archaic, not a Renaissance invention. Later mythographers transform a compact genealogical statement into long faithful service.

### Catasterism by Zeus

**Catasterism tradition; earliest surviving in the Eratosthenic complex.** After reaching heaven—either after Bellerophon’s fall or independently—Pegasus becomes the constellation. Hyginus says Jupiter placed him among the stars. Aratus’s Horse does not explicitly narrate this placement, so the identification is a mythographical overlay upon an older astronomical figure.

### Hippe/Melanippe as competing identity

**Ancient competing catasterism.** In Pseudo-Eratosthenes and Hyginus, the Horse can be **Hippe or Melanippe**, daughter of Chiron. Seduced or made pregnant by Aeolus, she flees to avoid her father, is changed into a mare, and is put in the sky. Her hindquarters are omitted so that Chiron, the neighboring Centaur in some celestial logic, cannot recognize her, or to preserve modesty. The narrative is related to Euripides’ lost *Melanippe the Wise* but survives in divergent forms.

### Pegasus and Perseus rescuing Andromeda

**Popular error/late artistic tradition.** No major early Greek account makes Pegasus Perseus’s mount during Andromeda’s rescue. Ovid explicitly equips Perseus with winged sandals. The nearby constellations encouraged artists to merge the cycles. Medieval and Renaissance pictures, Rubens, later academic painting, Shakespeare’s “Perseus’ horse,” modern films, and popular constellation guides spread the error. It is mythic reception, not an early classical version.

### Tarsus and the fallen feather

**Late legend/unverified classical attribution.** A story that a feather fell from Pegasus during his catasterism and gave Tarsus its name circulates in modern compilations. It is sometimes attached loosely to Aratus, but it is not in *Phaenomena* 205–224. No secure early text was located during this review.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

The region contains at least three distinguishable constructions:

1. **IKU, the Field**, an agricultural or cadastral rectangle.
2. **The Horse**, in stars of the northern/eastern modern Pegasus figure.
3. **Anunitu and the Tails**, involving γ Pegasi and stars that led toward Pisces.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** because Babylonian catalogues describe risings, paths and relative positions rather than giving modern coordinates, exact identifications depend on models. The common equation “IKU = the modern Great Square” is plausible in broad visual terms but too confident in exact membership.

### Egyptian

No securely documented pharaonic constellation matching Pegasus has been demonstrated. Dendera and diagonal-star traditions divide this sky differently. Claims that *Pegasus* is Egyptian for a vessel, that the half-horse marks cessation of Nile navigation, or that the Square was an Egyptian ship are early-modern or occult speculation.

### Arabic and Bedouin skies

Two systems coexisted:

- **Greco-Arabic scientific astronomy:** Ptolemy’s Horse became **al-Faras al-Aʿẓam**, Greater Horse.
- **Indigenous Arab astronomy:** the Square became **al-Dalw**, the leather well-bucket. Lines of stars represented its ropes and associated apparatus. The region also contained the final **suʿūd**, “lucky” asterisms: *Saʿd al-Humām, Saʿd al-Maṭar, Saʿd al-Bahāʾim,* and *Saʿd al-Bāriʿ*.

Arabic lunar mansions crossing this area include:

- **al-Fargh al-Muqaddam**, “the Fore Spout/Mouth of the Bucket,” associated with α and β Pegasi.
- **al-Fargh al-Muʾakhkhar**, “the Rear Spout/Mouth,” associated with γ Pegasi and α Andromedae.

The modern names Homam, Matar, Biham, Sadalbari, Salm and Alkarab preserve this indigenous layer rather than Pegasus’s anatomy.

### Persian

Al-Sufi was Persian by origin, but his *Book of the Fixed Stars* is Arabic and participates in the Ptolemaic Arabic system. Persian translations use ordinary horse vocabulary and Persianized Arabic star names. No independent ancient Iranian Pegasus constellation was verified. Comparisons with the **Simurgh** or divine Iranian horses are comparative mythology, not identity.

### Chinese

Modern Pegasus is divided among the **Black Tortoise of the North**:

- **室, Shì**, “Encampment” or “House,” the thirteenth lunar mansion, principally α and β Pegasi.
- **壁, Bì**, “Wall,” the fourteenth mansion, principally γ Pegasi and α Andromedae.
- Additional stars belonged to **Lìshì**, “Thunder and Lightning,” **Téngshé**, “Flying Serpent,” **Tǔgōng**, and related asterisms depending on catalogue and epoch.

Translations such as “Palace Encampment” and “Eastern Wall” reflect later cosmological interpretation. Shì and Bì are independent Chinese constructs, not Chinese names for Pegasus.

### Indian

The Square overlaps two lunar mansions:

- **Pūrva Bhādrapadā**, the “former fortunate/auspicious foot,” conventionally tied to α and β Pegasi; the Indian government’s modern principal-star table uses **β Pegasi**.
- **Uttara Bhādrapadā**, the “latter fortunate foot,” conventionally associated with γ Pegasi and α Andromedae; its principal star is often α Andromedae.

Their deities, symbols and astrological meanings belong to Indian lunar astronomy, not to a winged-horse myth. Exact stellar membership varies among siddhāntic and modern lists.  
[Government of India nakshatra table](https://packolkata.imd.gov.in/panchang/en/nakshatra_division).

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No independent ancient Hebrew Pegasus figure is securely attested. Medieval Jewish astronomers translated Arabic and Ptolemaic astronomy, using *sus*, “horse,” or transliterations.

Later Christian “Hebrew star-name” literature manufactured moralized meanings—e.g. Markab as “returning from afar,” Scheat as “he who goes and returns,” and Enif as “the branch” or “water.” These are not credible derivations of the Arabic names.

Kabbalistic assignments of Pegasus to tribes, letters, Sephiroth or messianic figures are early-modern or modern correspondences, not recoverable biblical astronomy.

### Christianized sky

**Documented modern invention.** Julius Schiller’s 1627 *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaced Pegasus with **the Archangel Gabriel**, plate XIX. It was part of a systematic reform: northern pagan figures became New Testament or Christian figures, zodiacal constellations Apostles, and southern figures Old Testament subjects. Cellarius reproduced this scheme, but astronomers retained Pegasus.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

Pegasus acquired allegorical meanings—volatile spirit, ascent of intellect, sublimation, poetic frenzy and the mercurial imagination—in Renaissance emblem books and later occultism. These are analogical readings, not a single ancient Hermetic doctrine.

No canonical *Corpus Hermeticum* passage identifies the constellation Pegasus with an alchemical operation. Claims that the Great Square is a primordial “Hermetic square,” portal or encoded philosopher’s stone are modern esoteric inventions.

### Astrology

Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* 1.9, assigns stars in the Horse a planetary nature broadly resembling **Mars and Mercury**. Later astrologers individualized Markab, Scheat and Algenib, often treating Scheat as dangerous through drowning or falls and Markab as conferring honor with peril.

These delineations are historical astrological tradition, not astronomical effects.

Pegasus contains **no member of the standard fifteen Behenian fixed stars**. Modern magical-astrology websites sometimes call Markab “Behenian”; that is incorrect relative to the canonical fifteen-star list.

The Arabic and Indian lunar mansions genuinely cross its stars, but Pegasus itself is not a zodiac sign. Claims of a present “Age of Pegasus” caused by Scheat near the equinoctial point are modern invention without standing in conventional precessional-age systems.

## Iconography

### Antiquity

- **c. 590–580 BCE, Corfu:** west pediment of the Temple of Artemis shows Medusa with Pegasus and Chrysaor; Archaeological Museum of Corfu.
- **c. 540 BCE onward, Corinth:** silver staters show the winged Pegasus with koppa; Athena later appears on the reverse. [Canellopoulos Museum example](https://camu.gr/en/item/argyros-korinthiakos-statiras/).
- **Fifth–fourth centuries BCE:** Greek vases show Bellerophon mounted on Pegasus against the Chimera.
- **Chest of Cypselus, reported by Pausanias 5.19.5:** Bellerophon, Pegasus and Chimera.
- **Roman mosaics and sarcophagi:** Pegasus appears as Bellerophon’s mount, a fountain-maker and a symbol of apotheosis.

### The Farnese Atlas

**Documented artifact, interpretation disputed.** The second-century CE Farnese Atlas in Naples bears a sculpted celestial globe whose constellations reflect Hellenistic traditions. A fore-half Horse appears. Claims that the globe is a direct copy of Hipparchus’s lost catalogue remain debated; iconography alone cannot establish exact authorship.

### Islamic manuscripts

Al-Sufi manuscripts depict each constellation twice: globe view and sky view. Pegasus is usually a winged fore-half horse with red or gold star marks. Surviving manuscripts are later copies; captions that call a fifteenth-century manuscript “al-Sufi’s 964 drawing” should distinguish the original work’s date from the copy’s date.  
[IAU image of al-Sufi Pegasus](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/images/detail/iau1603b/).

### Latin manuscripts

Carolingian and Romanesque *Aratea* manuscripts show a half-horse, often winged, sometimes emerging from a wavy band. Their images can preserve antique models while their accompanying constellation text derives from Germanicus, scholia, Hyginus or the *Aratus Latinus*.

No particular Pegasus can be assigned confidently to every cathedral zodiac. Cathedral portals normally privilege the twelve zodiac signs and labors of the months; Pegasus is exceptional rather than standard.

### Printed sequence

- **1515, Nuremberg:** Dürer–Stabius–Heinfogel northern map.
- **1603, Augsburg:** Bayer, *Uranometria*, plate XIX.
- **1627, Augsburg:** Schiller’s Gabriel.
- **1690, Gdańsk:** Hevelius, globe-view Pegasus.
- **1729, London:** Flamsteed, elaborate horse.
- **1782, Berlin/Stralsund:** Bode’s smaller popular atlas.
- **1801, Berlin:** Bode’s monumental plate.
- **1822, London:** Jamieson.
- **c. 1824, London:** *Urania’s Mirror* pierced educational card.
- **1830s–1850s:** Burritt and other school atlases.
- **Late nineteenth century:** constellation art increasingly yields to schematic lines and printed boundaries.

## People, events and works

### Astronomers and discoveries

- **Aratus** made the Horse one of the most memorable non-zodiacal figures in surviving Greek astronomical poetry.
- **Ptolemy** fixed its ancient stellar catalogue.
- **Al-Sufi** corrected magnitudes and positions and preserved both Greek and Arab star lore.
- **Bayer** assigned its Greek letters.
- **Flamsteed** assigned the numbered stars.
- **Bode** gave it its most densely plotted monumental artistic representation.
- **Maraldi** discovered M15 in 1746; **Messier** catalogued it in 1764.
- **William Herschel** discovered NGC 7331 in 1784.
- **Édouard Stephan** identified Stephan’s Quintet in 1877.
- **Mayor and Queloz** discovered 51 Pegasi b in 1995, work recognized with half of the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics.

### Navigation

The Great Square is an excellent seasonal and directional signpost, but no well-documented ancient or early-modern voyage was found that demonstrably steered by “Pegasus” as a primary navigational constellation. Mariners relied much more explicitly on pole stars, Ursa Major/Minor, circumpolar stars, solar altitude and later astronomical tables.

Claims that Phoenicians named Pegasus as a ship’s figurehead or that the half-horse marked cessation of the sailing season come from speculative etymology, not navigational logs.

### Literature

- Homer’s Bellerophon narrative provides the heroic core without naming Pegasus.
- Pindar’s *Olympian* 13 is the central early Pegasus poem.
- Euripides wrote lost tragedies *Bellerophon*, *Stheneboea* and *Melanippe*; fragments shaped later narratives.
- Ovid made Pegasus and Hippocrene central to the Muses’ landscape.
- In Ariosto and later chivalric literature, winged mounts owe much to Pegasus, although the hippogriff is distinct.
- Shakespeare, *Troilus and Cressida* 1.3, calls Pegasus “Perseus’ horse,” illustrating the influential Perseus conflation.
- Renaissance poets used “mounting Pegasus” for poetic composition; “Pegasus” consequently became shorthand for inspiration and sometimes for bad or overambitious verse.

### Visual art

Major postclassical subjects include:

- Mantegna, **Parnassus** (1497): Pegasus and the Muses.
- Cellini’s and related Medicean emblems: Pegasus as fame and poetic patronage.
- Rubens, **Perseus Freeing Andromeda**: the late conflation places Pegasus in Perseus’s rescue.
- Odilon Redon and Symbolist artists: Pegasus as imagination.
- Numerous fountains and civic sculptures exploit the hoof-created spring.

### Music

Pegasus appears in operas and cantatas on Bellerophon, including seventeenth-century treatments of the Bellerophon cycle. Modern bands and compositions titled *Pegasus* generally invoke flight or inspiration rather than the constellation specifically.

### Ships, aircraft and spaceflight

“Pegasus” has named numerous vessels and technologies:

- **HMS Pegasus**, used repeatedly by the Royal Navy.
- **USS Pegasus** and the Pegasus-class hydrofoils.
- **Operation Deadstick/Pegasus Bridge:** the Normandy bridge was renamed for the British airborne formation’s Pegasus emblem.
- **Fairey, Bristol and Rolls-Royce Pegasus** aviation associations; the Rolls-Royce Pegasus powers the Harrier.
- **Pegasus launch vehicle**, an air-launched orbital rocket developed by Orbital Sciences.
- **Project/constellation Pegasus** names in satellites, observatories and fictional spacecraft.
- **Pegasus satellites** of the 1960s measured micrometeoroid impacts for NASA’s Apollo planning.

These names honor the mythic flying horse rather than a scientific relationship with the constellation.

## Controversies and disputes

### Was the original stellar Horse Pegasus?

**Disputed.** Aratus calls it merely the Horse and gives no wings or Bellerophon narrative. The Catasterism tradition both identifies it as Pegasus and preserves an objection that the stellar figure lacks wings. The alternative Hippe/Melanippe story may be an attempt to explain a pre-existing half-horse. The safest conclusion is that **the astronomical Horse predates the fully stabilized Pegasus identification**.

### Was Pegasus inherited directly from Babylon?

**Disputed reconstruction.** Mesopotamia had a Field, a Horse and other figures in these stars. Similarity supports possible influence, especially given Greek borrowing elsewhere, but no text documents a direct “Babylonian Horse became Greek Pegasus” transfer. Exact IKU membership is itself debated.

### Was IKU exactly the Great Square?

**Overstatement.** Older references commonly say yes. More recent star-list analysis places the Field differently, with α, ψ, 72 and 78 Pegasi. At minimum, “IKU occupied the Square’s region” is safer than asserting exact identity.

### Did Ptolemy draw a wingless Pegasus?

**Misstatement.** The *Almagest* calls it the Horse and catalogues a fore-half. No authorial Ptolemaic drawing survives. Wingless or winged manuscript images belong to later copying traditions.

### Who rode Pegasus?

**Contested by reception, not early evidence.** Bellerophon is the canonical rider. Perseus’s ride during Andromeda’s rescue is late artistic/literary conflation. Ancient vase art and Pindar support Bellerophon; Ovid gives Perseus winged sandals.

### Was Pegasus born from blood or Medusa’s neck?

These are compatible narrative formulations, not necessarily competing traditions. Hesiod says the children sprang forth when her head was cut; Ovid emphasizes blood. Later summaries concretize the neck wound.

### Did Zeus send a gadfly?

**Popular but poorly sourced.** The major requested ancient texts say Pegasus threw Bellerophon, or Bellerophon fell through terror/vertigo. The gadfly may derive from later scholia or modern harmonization with other divine-punishment myths. It should not be cited simply as “according to Pindar.”

### Why is Alpheratz in Andromeda?

Not because the IAU “renamed” it or discovered an error. It had long belonged to both figures. Exclusive twentieth-century boundaries required one assignment; its α Andromedae designation and Andromedan anatomical role prevailed. The old **δ Pegasi** became obsolete, while the asterism remained the Great Square.

### Markab’s false Hebrew etymology

**Modern pseudo-etymology.** “Returning from afar” is repeatedly advertised as ancient Hebrew. The documented word is Arabic **markab**, a mount or vehicle, intertwined with *mankib*, shoulder, in the manuscript tradition. The moralized Hebrew gloss belongs to nineteenth- and twentieth-century prophetic-constellation literature.

### Algenib: flank or wing?

The sound and medieval forms favor Arabic **al-janb**, “side/flank.” Derivation from **al-janāḥ**, “wing,” is visually tempting but philologically weaker. Confusion is increased because **Algenib** was also used for α Persei before standardization.

### Scheat’s etymology

Forms in Bayer and Riccioli, including *Seat Alpheras* and *Scheat Alpheraz*, show textual corruption. “Shin,” “foreleg” and “upper arm” compete because Arabic anatomical labels were copied without diacritics and resegmented. Absolute confidence in one English gloss is unwarranted.

### Bode and the “Great Square”

Bode did not invent the four-star pattern, the Pegasus constellation, or the Arabic bucket. His importance lies in synthesis: exceptionally dense star plotting combined with classical artwork. Describing Pegasus as a “Bode constellation” would be false.

### “First exoplanet”

51 Pegasi b was:

- not the first proposed exoplanet;
- not the first confirmed planet outside the Solar System, because pulsar planets around PSR B1257+12 were confirmed earlier;
- but the first discovered planet orbiting a **normal Sun-like main-sequence star**, announced in 1995.

### IK Pegasi as an imminent danger

Popular articles call it the “nearest future supernova” or imply an impending explosion. The white dwarf would first require a suitable mass-transfer history and perhaps a long delay; the system’s proper motion also changes its eventual distance. Candidate progenitor is accurate; imminent threat is not.

### Al-Sufi manuscript dates

Images are frequently captioned “al-Sufi, 964” although the photographed manuscript may be fourteenth- or fifteenth-century. The work was composed around 964; each surviving codex must be dated separately.

### Hoaxes and modern occult claims

No historical evidence supports:

- a “Pegasus starseed” population;
- a precessional “Age of Pegasus”;
- Pegasus as an encoded Atlantean sky portal;
- the Square predicting modern disasters;
- Egyptian priests naming it from a sacred pitcher;
- a universal prehistoric winged-horse constellation.

These are documented modern beliefs or inventions when a dated publication can be identified; otherwise they remain unverified internet claims.

## Open questions

1. **Exact formation of Catasterismi 18:** which sentences descend from Eratosthenes and which entered the epitome or scholia remains a textual-critical problem.
2. **Eudoxus’s identity for the Horse:** his lost text prevents certainty about whether it was already mythic Pegasus.
3. **Exact Babylonian mappings:** IKU and the Horse depend on philological reconstruction of positional phrases; differing modern star identifications should be published with their tablets and reasoning.
4. **Earliest use of *Pegasus* as the constellation name:** Germanicus and the Eratosthenic tradition compete depending on how the epitome is dated and reconstructed.
5. **The gadfly:** a precise ancient textual locus remains to be established.
6. **Tarsus’s fallen feather:** no secure passage in Aratus or the principal catasterism texts was found.
7. **Egyptian and Phoenician etymologies:** they lack primary linguistic evidence and survive chiefly as historiographical curiosities.
8. **Hebrew/Kabbalistic maps:** a full manuscript study could distinguish Jewish reception of Arabic astronomy from later Christian “Gospel in the Stars” claims.
9. **Bode collection wording:** the figure “830 stars in 109 groups” needs the specific museum or catalogue record before its meaning can be assessed.
10. **Historical nova census:** modern databases list many extragalactic supernovae and cataclysmic variables in Pegasus, but no comprehensive source yet joins premodern guest-star records to Delporte’s exact polygon.
