# Musca: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **Kept by the International Astronomical Union.** Musca is one of the 88 official constellations. Its abbreviation is **Mus**, genitive **Muscae**, and its official boundaries are those prepared by Eugène Delporte, approved by the IAU in 1928 and published in 1930. None of its principal stars was reassigned to another constellation.
>
> **Historical status:** A **modern European constellation**, not an ancient Greek one. It was formed in the 1590s from observations by Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman, organized and first published graphically by Petrus Plancius, and canonized in atlases by Johann Bayer.
>
> **Principal historical names:** Dutch **De Vlieghe** (“the Fly”); Latin **Musca** (“fly”), **Apis** (“bee”), **Musca Apis**, and **Musca Australis** (“Southern Fly”); French **La Mouche**; German **die Fliege** or **die südliche Fliege**; Spanish **la Mosca**.
>
> **Classical mythology:** **None is documented.** Musca lay below the effective horizon of the classical Mediterranean catalogue tradition and was neither one of Ptolemy’s 48 figures nor a catasterism in Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Aratus, Germanicus, Manilius, Ovid, or the surviving ancient scholia.

## Method and evidentiary labels

The labels used below mean:

- **Documented fact:** explicitly present in a primary object, text, catalogue, institutional record, or modern scientific database.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion argued from surviving objects and records where the original act or document is lost.
- **Tradition:** a culturally transmitted identification recorded from its practitioners or in an ethnographic source.
- **Disputed:** competing attributions or interpretations exist.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in secondary literature but not located in a controlling primary source.
- **Legend/myth:** a narrative understood as traditional rather than historical reportage.
- **Modern invention:** a recently created identification, name, or story rather than an ancient inheritance.

## Basic identification

### Official identity

**Documented fact.** The IAU recognizes:

| Field | Official form |
|---|---|
| Nominative | **Musca** |
| Genitive | **Muscae** |
| Abbreviation | **Mus** |
| English gloss | **the Fly** |
| Area | approximately **138.4 square degrees** |
| Rank by area | **77th of 88** |
| Hemisphere | Far southern celestial hemisphere |
| Present neighbors | Apus, Carina, Centaurus, Chamaeleon, Circinus, Crux |
| Brightest star | α Muscae, visual magnitude about 2.7 |
| Messier objects | None |
| Official proper names among its principal Bayer stars | None presently established in the IAU catalogue |

The IAU’s 1922 Rome assembly adopted the constellation list and three-letter abbreviations. The modern boundaries were then drawn along right-ascension and declination lines referred to equinox B1875.0, approved in 1928, and published by Delporte in 1930. The underlying practical concern included keeping previously designated variable stars in the constellations under whose names they had been catalogued. [IAU constellation history and chart service](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [IAU Style Manual, table 11](https://iauarchive.eso.org/static/publications/stylemanual1989.pdf); [Delporte bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ).

**Qualification.** The often-repeated statement that the IAU “created” the 88 constellations in 1930 compresses two actions: names and abbreviations were settled at Rome in 1922; Delporte’s boundaries were accepted at the Leiden assembly in 1928 and printed in 1930.

### Position and visibility

**Documented fact.** Musca occupies the sky immediately south of Crux and the Coalsack region. It is wholly visible from latitudes south of roughly 14° north and is circumpolar through much of the inhabited Southern Hemisphere. Its present boundary is a compact six-sided polygon. Precession means that modern-coordinate numerical limits are not identical to Delporte’s B1875 grid, although they describe the same fixed great-circle region.

### Names and etymologies

#### Musca

**Documented fact.** Latin *musca* means “fly,” especially the common housefly or a comparable small flying insect. The genitive is *muscae*. It descends from the inherited Indo-European word-family represented by Greek **μυῖα** (*muîa*, “fly”) and related Indo-European forms.

The constellation name is therefore ordinary zoological Latin, not the name of a mythological person. It was already used on Willem Janszoon Blaeu’s early-seventeenth-century globes and was later stabilized by Lacaille and the IAU.

#### De Vlieghe

**Documented fact.** Frederick de Houtman’s 1603 catalogue calls the group **De Vlieghe**, early-modern Dutch for “the Fly.” Modern Dutch is **de Vlieg**. De Houtman’s heading is crucial evidence that the intended insect in the observing/cataloguing tradition was a fly, whatever Bayer’s engraving called it.

#### Apis

**Documented fact.** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* of 1603 labels the figure **APIS**, Latin “bee.” Bayer’s plate also contains **Apis Indica**, the “Indian bee,” as an alternative zoological designation for what became Apus, the Bird of Paradise. The proximity of *Apis* and *Apis Indica* created ample scope for later confusion.

**Disputed interpretation.** It is frequently asserted that Bayer “mistook” the intended fly for a bee. The mismatch between De Houtman’s *De Vlieghe* and Bayer’s *Apis* is real, but Bayer left no statement saying that he misunderstood the animal. “Mistake” is therefore a plausible reconstruction, not documented testimony.

#### Musca Apis and Apis seu Musca

**Documented fact.** Seventeenth-century writers used compound or alternative forms rather than treating “bee” and “fly” as sharply exclusive official identities. Riccioli used **Apis seu Musca** (“bee or fly”); Edward Sherburne listed *Musca*, *Apis*, and variant learned forms; Edmond Halley’s 1679 catalogue used **Musca Apis**. The hybrid wording records uncertainty or synonymy rather than a separate constellation.

#### Musca Australis

**Documented fact.** “Southern Fly” distinguished this group from the early-modern northern insect between Aries and Perseus, variously called *Apes*, *Apis*, *Vespa*, *Musca*, and ultimately **Musca Borealis**. When the northern figure became obsolete, *Australis* was no longer necessary and the IAU retained simply **Musca**.

This dossier concerns only southern Musca. Bode’s northern fly belongs to a separate historical figure.

#### Vernacular names

These are translations, not evidence for independent ancient constellations:

- French: **La Mouche**, historically also **La Mouche Australe**.
- German: **die Fliege**, historically **die südliche Fliege**.
- Spanish: **la Mosca**.
- Italian: **la Mosca**.
- Portuguese: **a Mosca**.
- Modern Dutch: **de Vlieg**.
- Modern Greek: **Μυία** or a descriptive equivalent based on “fly.”
- Modern Hebrew: **זבוב** or **קבוצת הכוכבים זבוב**, a modern translation (“Fly constellation”), not an ancient Hebrew asterism.
- Modern Chinese: **苍蝇座 / 蒼蠅座** (*Cāngyíng-zuò*, “Fly Constellation”).

### The “only insect in the sky”

**Documented fact with qualification.** Musca is the only **official IAU constellation explicitly representing an insect**. Scorpius represents an arachnid, not an insect. Historical atlases, however, also contained bees, wasps, and the abolished Musca Borealis, so “the only insect ever placed in the sky” is false.

## Stars and objects

### The original four-star fly

**Documented fact plus modern identification.** De Houtman catalogued four stars under *De Vlieghe*. Comparison of his positions with modern catalogues identifies them with:

| Historical body part | Modern star |
|---|---|
| Head | β Muscae |
| Body | γ Muscae |
| One wing | α Muscae |
| Other wing | δ Muscae |

The identifications are secure within the positional accuracy of the catalogue. De Houtman’s catalogue typically has errors on the scale of tens of arcminutes, but the bright quadrilateral is unmistakable. Verbunt and van Gent provide modern, machine-readable identifications and estimate an error-distribution width of about 40 arcminutes for De Houtman’s positions. [Verbunt and van Gent, “Early star catalogues of the southern sky”](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2011/06/aa16795-11.pdf).

### Principal Bayer-designated stars

Musca has no Flamsteed-number series. Flamsteed’s numbering system was developed from observations accessible from England and was not systematically extended to these far-southern stars.

| Designation | Approx. visual magnitude | Nature and historical role |
|---|---:|---|
| **α Muscae** | 2.69 | Brightest star; blue-white B-type star; β Cephei-type pulsator; one wing in the original fly |
| **β Muscae** | 3.04 | Hot multiple/binary system; head of the original fly |
| **δ Muscae** | 3.61 | Orange K-type giant binary; other wing; nearest conspicuous principal star |
| **λ Muscae** | 3.6–3.7 | A-type star; later admitted from Lacaille’s *informes* |
| **γ Muscae** | 3.8 | B-type slowly pulsating/53 Persei-type variable; body or tail axis of the early figure |
| **ε Muscae** | 4.0 | Red giant and semiregular variable |
| **μ Muscae** | 4.7–4.8 | K-type giant and irregular variable |
| **η Muscae** | 4.8 | Multiple/eclipsing system, commonly classified with Algol-type variability |
| **ζ¹, ζ² Muscae** | about 5–6 | The doubled zeta designation arose from nineteenth-century editorial treatment of Lacaille |
| **θ, ι Muscae** | fainter | Later Bayer-style designations; ι Muscae is a massive multiple system with a Wolf–Rayet component |
| **κ Muscae** | faint | Lacaille’s designation, subsequently omitted by Francis Baily as too faint for his retained sequence |

**Documented fact.** Lacaille mapped and lettered ten stars from α through κ. He also catalogued two stars now called λ and μ but treated them as *informes*—outside the drawn figure. Francis Baily brought them into Musca; Benjamin Gould supplied λ and μ. Baily’s handling of Lacaille’s zeta produced **ζ¹** and **ζ²**: he believed Lacaille’s intended zeta might have been the brighter neighboring star but preserved the older assignment by applying zeta to both. This is an actual nomenclatural dispute, not folklore.

### Proper names

**Documented fact.** None of Musca’s prominent stars has a traditional Greek, Latin, Persian, or Arabic proper name standardized by the IAU. Names such as “Alpha Muscae” are designations, not proper names.

**Tradition, not IAU standardization.** In Wardaman knowledge recorded by Bill Yidumduma Harney and Hugh Cairns, α and β Muscae participate in the ceremonial headband **Burangalul**, within the larger boomerang figure **Buran**. “Burangalul” appears in some modern star-name compilations, but it should not be represented as an ancient Arabic or official IAU proper name.

### Asterisms and naked-eye pattern

**Modern descriptive usage.** The main α–β–γ–δ quadrilateral is the historical fly. Modern line drawings often add ε, λ, μ, or η and consequently do not all depict the same insect. The bright-star arrangement has sometimes been compared to a small version of Ursa Minor, but that resemblance is a modern mnemonic, not a named historical asterism.

### Clusters, nebulae, galaxies, and dark clouds

Musca contains no Messier object. Its principal catalogue objects include:

#### NGC 4372

**Documented fact.** A Milky Way globular cluster projected near the southern end of the Dark Doodad. It lies roughly 20,000 light-years away; its apparent juxtaposition with the much nearer dark cloud is a line-of-sight coincidence. The cluster was discovered by James Dunlop during his southern observations in the 1820s.

#### NGC 4833

**Documented fact.** Another bright southern globular cluster, also discovered by Dunlop. It lies close in projection to the Galactic plane and is affected by foreground extinction.

#### NGC 5189

**Documented fact.** A structurally complex planetary nebula, often nicknamed the **Spiral Planetary Nebula**. The “spiral” appearance is morphological and does not make it a galaxy. John Herschel catalogued it from the Cape in the 1830s, although earlier observational priority is sometimes attributed to James Dunlop’s entry D252; the correspondence between Dunlop’s position and the modern nebula has been discussed rather than universally assumed.

#### MyCn 18

**Documented fact.** The young bipolar planetary nebula commonly called the **Engraved Hourglass Nebula**. The name comes from its Hubble-imaged nested hourglass structure. It is not the better-known Hourglass region within the Lagoon Nebula.

#### NGC 4071

**Documented fact.** A faint galaxy within the modern Delporte boundary. It illustrates the modern meaning of a constellation: a bounded area containing unrelated foreground and background objects, not a physical stellar group.

#### Other NGC/IC objects

Catalogue searches place several faint open clusters, planetary nebulae, and galaxies within Musca, including NGC 4463 and IC objects. Lists differ because some popular pages select only showpieces and because coordinates near the modern borders must be tested against the exact polygon. Claims of a “complete” short list should therefore be treated cautiously.

#### The Musca molecular cloud and Dark Doodad

**Documented fact.** The long, narrow **Musca molecular cloud** is a nearby star-forming dark cloud. Its visually striking lane is widely called the **Dark Doodad Nebula**.

**Documented modern coinage.** NASA’s APOD account attributes “Dark Doodad” to astrophotographer and writer Dennis di Cicco, who coined it in 1986 while in Australia to observe Comet Halley. This is not an indigenous name or nineteenth-century catalogue term. [NASA APOD, “NGC 4372 and the Dark Doodad”](https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap150910.html).

#### The Coalsack

**Documented fact with boundary qualification.** The Coalsack is immediately north of Musca beside Crux; the larger obscuring complex extends across the Crux–Centaurus–Musca region. Popular captions saying that the Coalsack is simply “in Musca” are imprecise. The named visual core is principally associated with Crux, while dark-cloud structure continues southward toward Musca.

Recent molecular-cloud research has proposed a larger connected Chamaeleon–Musca–Coalsack structure, sometimes called the large **“C”**. That is an astrophysical reconstruction from gas and dust, not a historical constellation figure.

### Variable stars and eruptive objects

Important variables include:

- **α Muscae:** low-amplitude β Cephei-type pulsator.
- **γ Muscae:** slowly pulsating B/53 Persei-type variable.
- **ε Muscae:** semiregular red giant.
- **μ Muscae:** irregular or slowly irregular red/orange giant.
- **η Muscae:** eclipsing multiple system.
- **θ Muscae:** massive interacting or colliding-wind stellar system involving a Wolf–Rayet star.
- **SY Muscae:** symbiotic binary containing an evolved cool star and hot compact component.

This is not a complete GCVS inventory; the modern boundary contains numerous survey variables.

### Novae and apparent “novae”

#### GQ Muscae / Nova Muscae 1983

**Documented fact.** William Liller discovered the 1983 eruption at about magnitude 7.2. GQ Mus became the first nova from which X-rays were detected and remained an important supersoft X-ray source.

#### Nova Muscae 1991 / GRS 1124−683

**Documented fact and nomenclatural caution.** Detected first in X-rays by instruments aboard the Soviet GRANAT satellite, then identified optically at ESO in January 1991, it was provisionally called **Nova Muscae 1991**. It is not a classical white-dwarf nova in the ordinary sense: it is an **X-ray nova**, a transient low-mass X-ray binary whose compact component is dynamically identified as a black-hole candidate. The historical name survives even though the physical classification changed. [ESO, “Strange New Star Appears in the Southern Sky”](https://www.hq.eso.org/public/news/eso9101/).

This is one of Musca’s most consequential terminological traps: “nova” describes its sudden appearance and early classification, not a demonstrated classical-nova thermonuclear eruption.

#### V357 Muscae / Nova Muscae 2018

**Documented fact.** The bright Galactic nova PNV J11261220−6531086 reached approximately naked-eye magnitude 6.5 and was followed through AAVSO Alert Notice 609.

#### V415 Muscae / Nova Muscae 2022

**Documented fact.** ASASSN-22hw, also AT 2022mmp, was recognized as a Galactic nova and assigned **V415 Mus**. [AAVSO nova index and Alert Notice 781](https://www.aavso.org/index.php/category/tags/nova).

#### Reported Nova Muscae 2026 / V419 Mus

**Provisional or incompletely verified.** A 2026 object has circulated under **V419 Mus** and “Nova Muscae 2026,” associated with an ASAS-SN detection. I did not locate, in the sources consulted, a controlling published GCVS record or AAVSO alert page comparable to the 2018 and 2022 notices. It should therefore be cited as a reported/current designation rather than folded uncritically into the settled historical list.

### Supernovae

**Finding.** No naked-eye historical supernova is securely recorded in the modern Musca boundary. Extragalactic supernovae can occur in its faint galaxies, but none has shaped the constellation’s historical tradition. The black hole in Nova Muscae 1991 was presumably created by an ancient stellar-collapse event, but that is an inferred physical history, not an observed “Supernova Muscae.”

### Meteor showers

**Finding.** No major established annual meteor shower is conventionally associated with Musca. Searches of general lists sometimes generate “Muscids” from automated radiant naming or preliminary shower reports, but no prominent established Muscid stream comparable to the Perseids or Leonids was verified in the IAU Meteor Data Center material consulted. The MDC itself cautions that database inclusion records published claims and does not by itself guarantee the physical reality or independence of a shower. [IAU Meteor Data Center](https://iaumeteordatacenter.org/).

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the sixteenth century: no Musca constellation

**Documented negative finding.** At declinations around −65° to −76°, Musca was largely or wholly inaccessible to Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, classical Persian, and medieval European systematic observers. Its stars do not form a named figure in:

- the Babylonian *MUL.APIN* constellation scheme;
- surviving Egyptian decanal lists or the Dendera zodiac;
- Aratus’ *Phaenomena*;
- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hipparchus as transmitted;
- Ptolemy’s *Almagest*;
- al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars*;
- Ulugh Beg’s revision of the Ptolemaic catalogue.

Consequently, claims of a Babylonian bee, Egyptian fly, Hebrew insect, or classical Greek Musca located in these exact stars are unsupported.

### 1595–1597: the first Dutch voyage

**Documented core; disputed division of credit.** Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser sailed as chief pilot aboard the *Hollandia* in the first Dutch expedition to the East Indies. Petrus Plancius had trained or instructed him to observe southern stars. Frederick de Houtman participated and assisted. Keyser died at Bantam in September 1596. The surviving expedition returned in August 1597, bringing southern observations to Plancius.

**Disputed attribution.** Older histories alternately credit:

1. Keyser alone with observing and inventing the figures;
2. Keyser and de Houtman jointly;
3. de Houtman with an independent or substantially independent catalogue;
4. Plancius with organizing the observations into the twelve pictured constellations.

Richard de Grijs’s recent reconstruction concludes that Keyser probably led the initial observations, de Houtman assisted and later carried out further work, while Plancius probably took the leading role in delineating and naming the twelve figures. The documentary evidence does not permit a clean modern notion of sole “inventorship.” [de Grijs, “Early Star Charts of the Dutch East India Company”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12992).

### 1597/1598: Plancius–Hondius globe

**Documented through surviving descriptions and copies; original-object problem.** A 35-cm celestial globe designed by Plancius and produced with Jodocus Hondius showed the twelve new southern constellations. The original 1598 specimen generally cited in the literature is lost or no longer securely extant; later Hondius and Blaeu globes preserve its arrangement.

The insect is drawn over Chamaeleon. Sources differ over whether it was explicitly named on the earliest globe. The safest formulation is: **the figure appears, but its label is absent or not securely recoverable from the surviving evidence**.

### 1600–1603: Hondius and Blaeu

**Documented fact.** Jodocus Hondius’s 1600 globe transmitted the new southern figures. Willem Janszoon Blaeu’s 1602/1603 globes used **Musca**, providing an early explicit Latin “Fly.”

Globe genealogy is complicated by copying among Plancius, Hondius, and Blaeu. Similarity does not prove direct access to any one now-lost object; nevertheless, detailed comparison strongly supports dependence on the Plancius/Keyser material.

### 1603: de Houtman’s *Spraeck ende woord-boeck*

**Documented fact.** De Houtman appended a catalogue of southern stars to his Malay–Dutch linguistic work and used the heading **De Vlieghe**. Four stars constitute the fly.

**Disputed point.** De Houtman did not acknowledge Keyser. This omission has been explained as evidence of independent observation, appropriation, rivalry, or strained relations during the voyage. None of those explanations is proved by the silence alone. Modern positional comparison suggests both inherited and newly observed material.

### 1603: Bayer’s *Uranometria*

**Documented fact.** Bayer’s plate 49, the southern planisphere, gives the first printed celestial-atlas depiction and labels the insect **APIS**, the Bee. Bayer’s work gave the twelve southern constellations extraordinary longevity.

Bayer’s map is geocentric in the atlas convention and surrounds the pole with figures including Chamaeleon, Apis, Apis Indica, Pavo, Tucana, and others. The fly/bee remains a four-star figure.

### 1624–1627: Bartsch, Kepler, and Schiller

**Documented fact.** Jakob Bartsch used *Vespa* for the separate northern insect, partly helping later authors distinguish northern and southern forms. Kepler’s 1627 *Rudolphine Tables* included early southern-catalogue material.

In Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* of 1627, the pagan and zoological southern sky was reorganized into Old Testament figures. Musca’s place became part of **Eva, mater cunctorum viventium**—“Eve, mother of all living [people].” It was not assigned a saint: Schiller used New Testament figures in the north, apostles for the zodiac, and Old Testament figures in the south. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/); [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720).

### Mid-seventeenth century

**Documented fact.** Riccioli’s **Apis seu Musca**, Sherburne’s alternative learned forms, and globes by de Wit, Allard, and later Coronelli show that “bee” and “fly” coexisted. No central authority fixed constellation names.

Plancius also introduced a northern bee, **Apes**, on his 1612 globe. This is the principal historical reason for later “southern” and “northern” modifiers.

### 1679: Halley

**Documented fact.** Edmond Halley’s *Catalogus Stellarum Australium*, based on observations from St Helena, records **Musca Apis**. Halley catalogued 341 southern stars with telescopic instruments, greatly improving positional accuracy. He did not invent Musca. [Powerhouse Museum catalogue record](https://collection.powerhouse.com.au/object/38286); [digitized catalogue record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL23428504M/Catalogus_stellarum_australium_sive_Supplementum_catalogi_Tychenici_...).

### 1690: Hevelius

**Documented fact.** Johannes Hevelius’s posthumous *Prodromus Astronomiae/Firmamentum Sobiescianum* depicts the southern insect as a winged bee-like fly and uses **Musca Australis** in distinction from his northern *Musca*. The stellar skeleton remains α–β–γ–δ.

### 1752 observations; 1756 publication: Lacaille

**Documented fact.** Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751–1752. His southern planisphere, printed in the Academy volume for 1752 but published in 1756, calls the constellation **La Mouche**. In the Latin *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* issued posthumously in 1763, the form is **Musca**.

Lacaille did not invent the figure; he standardized the fly interpretation and greatly expanded its stellar catalogue. His French map is the important bridge from the seventeenth-century *Apis/Musca* ambiguity to the modern Fly. [Lacaille planisphere record](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/planisphere-containing-celestial-constellations.html).

### 1782: Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne*

**Documented fact.** Bode’s smaller atlas transmitted the southern figure in German astronomical culture, using the southern-fly distinction while Musca Borealis still circulated. The relevant German gloss is **die südliche Fliege**.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact.** Bode’s monumental Berlin atlas presents the figure in the Crux–Centaurus–Chamaeleon region, with the label **MUSCA AUSTRALIS**. It is shown as a naturalistic winged insect immediately in front of Chamaeleon.

Bode’s engraved plate, rather than a mythographic prose entry, is the controlling evidence. The *Uranographia* contains twenty enormous copperplates and roughly 17,000 plotted stars plus thousands of nebular entries. Its title describes the work as a delineation of the stars from the newest and most complete observations. The atlas has only a small amount of prefatory/index text; there is no extensive Musca origin narrative comparable to a classical mythographer’s account. Claims that “Bode says the chameleon is hunting the fly” should therefore distinguish what the plate shows from what its text states. [ETH complete facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [NYPL object record](https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/c168b3c0-c6b6-012f-a0fc-58d385a7bc34?canvasIndex=0).

**Correction to a secondary error.** One modern textbook says that Bode’s atlas sometimes labels this constellation *Apis*. Inspection and Bode-specific reproductions support **Musca Australis** and a bee-like visual treatment, not a simple controlling label *Apis*. The error probably conflates Bayer’s name with Bode’s insect iconography.

### Nineteenth-century reception

- **Francis Baily:** rationalized Lacaille’s stellar lettering, dropped κ as insufficiently bright, preserved the zeta ambiguity as ζ¹/ζ², and admitted stars later lettered λ and μ.
- **Benjamin Gould:** supplied λ and μ in the southern Bayer-style sequence.
- **John and James Herschel:** observed southern nebulae and double stars from the Cape; their cataloguing helped populate Musca with telescopic objects.
- **Jamieson, 1822:** Musca appears on the southern-hemisphere planisphere rather than receiving a dedicated British-latitude plate. Jamieson’s atlas could not show every far-southern constellation on its regional charts.
- **Urania’s Mirror, 1824/1825:** contrary to broad advertising about constellations visible in the British Empire, the card set omitted several far-southern figures. Its “Musca” card entry is **Musca Borealis**, not southern Musca. Treating it as an illustration of modern Musca is a common error.
- **Burritt:** American geographical-astronomy manuals mention or map Musca on southern planispheres, generally inheriting Bode/Jamieson-era nomenclature.
- **Argelander and Heis:** their northern visual catalogues did not redefine the remote southern figure.
- **Uranometria Argentina, 1877:** Benjamin Gould’s systematic southern work helped establish the stellar membership later used in formal boundary making.

### 1922–1930: IAU settlement

**Documented fact.** Musca was retained at Rome in 1922 as one of the 88 principal constellations with abbreviation **Mus**. The first printed list contained 89 headings only because obsolete Argo appeared alongside Carina, Puppis, and Vela; this did not make Musca provisional.

Delporte’s boundaries were designed after Gould’s southern work and encompass Musca’s established variables and principal stars. The final figure is an area of sky, not merely Bode’s illustrated insect.

### Present

**Documented fact.** Modern astronomical usage is simply **Musca**. “Musca Australis” survives historically and descriptively but is not the official nominative. The northern Musca was not retained; its stars now lie in Aries. Southern Musca lost no comparable identity.

## Invention, purpose, and reception

### What did the figure honor?

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Unlike Robur Carolinum, Scutum, Gloria Frederici, Taurus Poniatovii, or Bode’s apparatus constellations, Musca honored no documented monarch, patron, instrument, machine, or individual. It belongs to the natural-history vocabulary of Dutch voyages: bird of paradise, toucan, flying fish, chameleon, peacock, crane, and other exotic or maritime creatures.

**Unverified hypothesis.** Some popular accounts suggest the southern figures were borrowed from Indonesian or other indigenous constellations. No direct voyage record demonstrating an indigenous source for the fly has been found. The names’ European natural-history character and Plancius’s apparent editorial role make European invention more likely, but the possibility of unrecorded local influence cannot be disproved.

### The chameleon’s prey

**Iconographic fact.** From the earliest graphic tradition, the insect lies immediately above or before Chamaeleon. Later artists often orient the lizard toward it.

**Tradition/modern reading.** “The fly is the chameleon’s prey” is a natural visual interpretation and is now standard popular lore.

**Negative finding.** No surviving statement by Keyser, de Houtman, Plancius, Bayer, Hevelius, Lacaille, or Bode has been located that tells a story in which the chameleon was placed in heaven to hunt this fly. It is therefore iconographic wit, not a documented creation myth.

### Why it survived

**Scholarly interpretation.** Musca survived because:

1. it occupied a genuinely uncatalogued southern region;
2. the four bright stars make a compact, recognizable group;
3. Bayer’s widely reprinted *Uranometria* transmitted it;
4. Halley and Lacaille reused it in scientific catalogues;
5. it acquired variable-star and object nomenclature before the IAU settlement.

By contrast, the northern Musca competed for stars already securely assigned to Aries and Perseus and had little scientific necessity.

## Classical myths and legends

### No Musca catasterism

**Documented negative finding.** There is no Musca entry in:

- Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* or the Epitome;
- Hyginus, *De Astronomia/Poetica Astronomica*;
- Hyginus, *Fabulae*;
- Aratus, *Phaenomena*;
- the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus’s *Aratea* or its scholia;
- Ovid, *Fasti* or *Metamorphoses*;
- Manilius, *Astronomica*;
- Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*;
- Pausanias, *Description of Greece*.

The fly as an animal occurs in classical literature—for example, in tales of Io’s torment the pursuing creature is normally a gadfly—but no ancient author identifies that insect with these stars. Attaching Io’s gadfly, Myiagros/Myiodes cults, or generic Greek fly symbolism to Musca is a **modern associative invention**.

### False classical identifications

- **Io’s gadfly:** mythic, ancient, but never catasterized as Musca.
- **Zeus Apomyios/Myiagros, “averter of flies”:** real cult epithets and rituals, but unrelated to the southern constellation.
- **The bee of Melissa or the infant Zeus:** ancient bee traditions, but not Bayer’s Apis.
- **Samson’s bees:** applied by Bartsch to the separate northern insect, not securely to Musca Australis.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian and Egyptian

**Negative finding.** No accepted Babylonian or pharaonic Egyptian asterism has been securely matched to Musca’s principal stars. The far-southern declination makes direct identification improbable. Dendera’s Greco-Egyptian zodiac is not evidence for Musca.

### Greek and Roman

**Negative finding.** The region fell outside the Ptolemaic catalogue. No Greek or Roman star names survive for α, β, γ, or δ Muscae.

### Arabic and Persian astronomy

**Documented negative finding.** Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* follows Ptolemy’s 48 constellations. It therefore contains neither Musca nor indigenous Arabic names for its four principal stars. Al-Ṣūfī worked within a tradition that compared Ptolemaic figures with Bedouin asterisms, but no Bedouin Musca-group is documented there.

Similarly, Ulugh Beg’s Persianate catalogue revised Ptolemaic positions and does not introduce this far-southern fly. Consequently, purported “Arabic proper names” for Musca’s bright stars should be treated as spurious unless a later navigational source is specified.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mapping

**Negative finding.** Musca does not belong to ancient Jewish mazalot, biblical constellation lists, the twelve tribal/zodiacal mappings, or standard medieval Kabbalistic star schemes. Modern Hebrew simply translates the IAU constellation. No defensible premodern Kabbalistic fly mapping to these stars was found.

### Indian astronomy

**Negative finding.** None of the 27/28 nakshatras reaches Musca; the lunar mansions follow the ecliptic, whereas Musca is far south of it. No Sanskrit nakshatra name or classical Indian fixed-star designation is securely associated with α–δ Muscae.

Southern Indian and navigational communities may have possessed local knowledge of this region, but no specific Musca-equivalent was verified in the consulted historical sources. Absence from Sanskrit elite astronomy is not proof that no local oral asterism ever existed.

### Chinese astronomy

**Documented fact.** Ancient Chinese observers did not include these far-southern stars in the Three Enclosures and Twenty-Eight Mansions. After Jesuit transmission of European southern charts in the late Ming and early Qing periods, the region was incorporated into the **Twenty-three Southern Asterisms**.

Later Chinese mappings include:

- **蜜蜂 Mìfēng**, “Bee”: β and α Muscae and additional stars, preserving the European *Apis* concept.
- **海山 Hǎishān**, “Sea and Mountain”: a large southern asterism extending through Carina, Centaurus, Musca, and Vela; λ Muscae is conventionally numbered within it.
- Modern constellation: **苍蝇座 / 蒼蠅座**, “Fly Constellation.”

These are post-contact Chinese adaptations, not proof of an independent ancient Chinese fly. [Summary of Musca in Chinese uranography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musca_in_Chinese_astronomy).

### Wardaman

**Recorded tradition.** In Wardaman knowledge from northern Australia, the Musca stars participate in **Buran**, a ceremonial boomerang in the “Central Arena” around Crux. α and β form or mark the ceremonial forehead band **Burangalul**; γ and δ are associated with armbands.

**Evidentiary caution.** The published documentation is modern ethnography, especially Harney and Cairns’s *Dark Sparklers*. It records indigenous knowledge but does not supply an archaeological date for its origin. Calling it “thousands of years old” without a separately dated chain of evidence would exceed the source.

### Arrernte and Luritja

**Recorded tradition.** Ethnographic literature reports a division of the sky between Arrernte and Luritja camps around the Milky Way; Musca’s stars were grouped among those claimed on the Arrernte side along with Fomalhaut and bright stars in Pavo and Grus. This is not an insect identification.

### Christianized sky

**Documented fact.** Schiller’s 1627 replacement was **Eve, Mother of All Living**, not a saint and not an isolated Christian fly. The stars were absorbed into a larger biblical human figure. Schiller’s reform failed to replace ordinary astronomical nomenclature, though Cellarius later reproduced the Christianized system in *Harmonia Macrocosmica*.

### Astrology, Hermeticism, and alchemy

**Negative finding.**

- Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* assigns planetary natures to stars of ancient constellations but says nothing about Musca.
- No principal Musca star belongs to the standard list of fifteen Behenian fixed stars.
- No Western or Indian lunar mansion crosses Musca.
- No established medieval Hermetic or alchemical correspondence for these stars predates the constellation’s invention.

**Modern invention.** Contemporary astrological websites assign traits such as persistence, annoyance, industry, or predation to Musca. These are retrospective symbolic readings, usually derived from the insect rather than a transmitted premodern doctrine. They should not be presented as Ptolemaic astrology.

## Iconography

### Ancient monuments and manuscripts

**Negative finding.** Musca cannot appear as a constellation on:

- the Farnese Atlas;
- classical zodiacal coins;
- medieval Aratus manuscripts;
- al-Ṣūfī manuscripts;
- Dürer’s 1515 northern and southern celestial maps;
- Romanesque cathedral zodiac portals;
- medieval calendars based on the zodiac.

A fly-like mark on such an object would not, without an inscription and matching stars, constitute Musca.

### Dated iconographic sequence

1. **1597/1598, Amsterdam — Plancius/Hondius globe:** earliest known figure; small insect above Chamaeleon; original globe lost or insecurely extant.
2. **1600, Amsterdam — Hondius globe:** transmitted the Plancian southern figures.
3. **1602/1603, Amsterdam — Blaeu globe:** labels the insect *Musca*.
4. **1603, Augsburg — Bayer, *Uranometria*, plate 49:** engraved insect labeled **APIS**; first atlas depiction.
5. **1627, Augsburg — Schiller:** the stars absorbed into Eve; engravings associated with Johann Matthias Kager and Lucas Kilian.
6. **1650s–1670s, Dutch globes and planispheres:** Musca/fly imagery becomes conventional.
7. **1688, Venice/Paris — Coronelli globes:** the southern insect appears among accepted southern figures.
8. **1690, Gdańsk — Hevelius:** **Musca Australis**, rendered bee-like beside Chamaeleon.
9. **1742, Nuremberg — Doppelmayr:** fly imagery on southern planispheres.
10. **1756, Paris — Lacaille:** schematic **La Mouche**, with scientific stars increasingly dominant over naturalistic figure-work.
11. **1782, Berlin/Stralsund — Bode, *Vorstellung der Gestirne*:** southern fly in a compact popular atlas.
12. **1801, Berlin — Bode, *Uranographia*:** large naturalistic insect labeled **Musca Australis**, directly before Chamaeleon amid the Milky Way and dark Coalsack region.
13. **1822, London — Jamieson:** included on the southern planisphere; no separate Musca plate.
14. **1824/1825, London — *Urania’s Mirror*:** southern Musca omitted; the depicted “Musca” is the obsolete northern fly.
15. **Late nineteenth century:** scientific atlases increasingly suppress pictorial figures in favor of lines, lettering, and catalogue boundaries.
16. **1930 onward:** Delporte/IAU charts show a bounded polygon, not an insect body.

### Coins, ceilings, churches, and calendars

**Finding.** No securely identified premodern coin, cathedral portal, monumental zodiac ceiling, or calendar depicts this southern Musca as such. Modern observatory ceilings and educational planispheres may include it as one of all 88 constellations, but that is generic modern inclusion rather than a distinctive Musca cult or iconographic tradition.

## People, events, and works

### Navigators

Keyser and de Houtman observed the stars during Dutch voyages intended partly to improve southern navigation and globe-making. Plancius had a practical interest in navigational astronomy. However, no evidence was found that Musca itself served as a standard latitude or direction finder comparable to Crux, Canopus, or the Magellanic Clouds.

**Popular error.** “Explorers steered by Musca” overstates the evidence. Southern-star surveying assisted navigation generally; Musca’s faint compact quadrilateral was not documented as a principal navigational pointer.

### Observers

- **Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser:** initial observational leader; died before publication.
- **Frederick de Houtman:** assistant and later independent observer/catalogue publisher.
- **Petrus Plancius:** cartographic organizer and probable delineator.
- **Johann Bayer:** made *Apis* internationally visible through *Uranometria*.
- **Edmond Halley:** remeasured southern stars from St Helena.
- **Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille:** expanded the star catalogue and fixed the fly name in French/Latin usage.
- **James Dunlop:** discovered or catalogued major southern clusters, including NGC 4372 and NGC 4833.
- **John Herschel:** observed and catalogued southern nebulae from the Cape.
- **Dennis di Cicco:** coined “Dark Doodad” in 1986.
- **William Liller:** discovered Nova Muscae 1983.
- **Massimo Della Valle and Brian Jarvis:** identified the optical counterpart of Nova Muscae 1991 at ESO.

### Ships and missions

- The Dutch ship **Hollandia** carried Keyser and de Houtman on the first voyage.
- **GRANAT**, a Soviet–French high-energy observatory, detected the 1991 X-ray transient with international instrumentation.
- Later X-ray observatories studied GQ Mus and Nova Muscae 1991.

No historically important vessel or space mission named simply *Musca* was securely identified in the consulted institutional records. Biological ships or works titled *Musca* often refer to the fly genus rather than the constellation.

### Literature, music, painting, and architecture

**Finding.** Musca has a relatively slight independent cultural afterlife compared with Orion or Crux. Its strongest artistic corpus is celestial cartography itself—Bayer, Hevelius, Lacaille, Bode, and southern planispheres. Searches produced no canonical early-modern poem, major musical work, or monumental building demonstrably named for this constellation rather than for the Latin insect/genus.

Modern astrophotography of the Dark Doodad, NGC 5189, and MyCn 18 constitutes the most substantial current visual culture associated with Musca.

## Controversies and disputes

### Who invented Musca?

**Disputed.** Keyser, de Houtman, and Plancius all have defensible roles:

- Keyser: principal first-voyage observer.
- De Houtman: assistant, later observer, first catalogue publisher using *De Vlieghe*.
- Plancius: recipient/editor and probable constellation designer.
- Bayer: not the inventor, but first atlas publisher.

“Musca was invented by Bayer” is false if “invented” means first delineated; “Musca was invented solely by Keyser” is stronger than the surviving documents justify.

### 1597 or 1598?

**Documentary uncertainty.** Sources alternate between “late 1597” and “early 1598” for Plancius’s globe. Both refer to the same production episode. An exact year should be given as **1597/1598** unless the individual physical edition is specified.

### Fly or bee?

**Documented ambiguity.** De Houtman says fly; Bayer says bee; Blaeu says fly; Riccioli and Halley combine the terms; Lacaille settles on fly. It was not continuously “known as the Bee for two centuries” in any exclusive sense: both traditions coexisted.

### Did Lacaille rename it?

**Qualified fact.** Lacaille decisively promoted *La Mouche/Musca*, but he did not originate *Musca*: Blaeu and other seventeenth-century sources already used it. “Renamed by Lacaille” is acceptable only as shorthand for standardization.

### Bode’s name

**Contested secondary reporting.** Bode is sometimes said to show *Apis*. His naturalistic figure may look bee-like, but the southern designation associated with his atlas is **Musca Australis**. Confusion probably comes from Bayer or from reproductions whose labels are cropped.

### The chameleon-prey story

**Modern interpretive tradition.** The visual relationship is real; an originating myth or authorial explanation has not been traced. It should not be described as a Dutch, Greek, or Aboriginal legend without a source.

### “Only insect”

**Qualified.** True among current IAU constellations; false across the entire history of Western constellations.

### Musca versus Musca Borealis

**Documented source of errors.** Nineteenth-century descriptions placing Musca “between Aries and Perseus” refer to **Musca Borealis**, not modern Musca. Likewise, the insect in *Urania’s Mirror* is the northern one.

### Bayer versus Flamsteed numbering

**Popular error.** Musca’s Greek letters are Bayer-style designations, but later letters were regularized by Lacaille, Baily, and Gould. There are no ordinary Flamsteed numbers for its principal stars.

### Proper-name commerce

**Modern invention.** Commercial star registries attach purchased names to Musca stars, but these have no IAU standing. α Muscae remains without an IAU-approved conventional proper name.

### Nova Muscae 1991

**Scientific reclassification.** The object’s name invites the false inference that it was a classical nova. It is a black-hole X-ray transient. This is a historical naming survival, not a hoax.

### “Ancient Musca”

**Unsupported claim.** Websites sometimes supply generalized fly or bee myths and imply that they explain the constellation. No chain from an ancient text to these specific stars exists.

## Open questions

1. **Exact authorship of the twelve southern figures.** No surviving memorandum divides observation, constellation design, and naming among Keyser, de Houtman, and Plancius.
2. **The lost Plancius globe.** Better documentation or rediscovery of an original 1597/1598 specimen could settle whether the insect was labeled and whether it was intended as fly or bee.
3. **Sources behind the animal program.** Travel literature and European natural-history collections are likely influences, but a precise visual prototype for the fly has not been established.
4. **Indigenous contact.** No documented borrowing has been demonstrated, but voyage records are incomplete and do not exhaust knowledge exchanged orally in the Indian Ocean world.
5. **Dunlop’s NGC 5189 priority.** Matching some of Dunlop’s imprecise catalogue entries to modern objects remains a historiographical problem.
6. **The extent of the Musca–Coalsack complex.** Modern molecular-cloud studies may revise the physical relationship among clouds historically treated as separate dark markings.
7. **Muscid meteor claims.** Preliminary radiant groups require continued orbital confirmation before they can be treated as stable showers.
8. **Nova Muscae 2026/V419 Mus.** Its settled catalogue identity and final classification should be checked against a future GCVS name list or formal AAVSO/CBAT notice.
9. **Wardaman chronology.** The tradition is well recorded in modern ethnography, but its historical depth cannot be assigned a numerical age from that evidence alone.
10. **Bode collection metadata.** The characterization “830 stars in 109 groups” does not describe the complete *Uranographia*, which plots vastly more stars and approximately 100-plus constellation figures. It may refer to a particular institutional subset, teaching collection, card index, or exhibition chain; no matching Bode catalogue description was located.
