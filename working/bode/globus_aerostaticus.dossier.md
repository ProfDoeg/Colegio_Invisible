# Globus Aerostaticus: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact** — stated or depicted in a contemporary primary source, official catalogue, or institutional record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern identification inferred by comparing historical charts/catalogues with modern coordinates.
- **Tradition** — a culturally transmitted identification not necessarily historical in the modern evidentiary sense.
- **Disputed** — competing identifications or attributions exist.
- **Unverified claim** — repeated in secondary literature but not located in a contemporary source during this investigation.
- **Legend/myth** — a narrative presented as such by its transmitting culture.
- **Modern invention** — a deliberately created modern constellation, name, or interpretation.

## Basic identification

### Status

**ABOLISHED / NOT ACCEPTED AS AN IAU CONSTELLATION.**

More precisely, **Globus Aerostaticus was omitted from the standardized list of 88 constellations adopted at the IAU’s first General Assembly in Rome in May 1922**. No located resolution separately declared “Globus Aerostaticus is abolished.” The language “abolished by the IAU in 1922,” though convenient, is therefore shorthand for non-selection.

In 1928 the IAU approved Eugène Delporte’s comprehensive boundaries, published in 1930 for equinox B1875.0. Those boundaries assigned every point formerly occupied by the balloon principally to:

- **Microscopium** — abbreviation **Mic**, genitive **Microscopii**
- **Piscis Austrinus** — **PsA**, genitive **Piscis Austrini**
- **Capricornus** — **Cap**, genitive **Capricorni**

This sequence is documented by the [IAU’s history of the constellations](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), the original Rome-list discussion summarized from *Transactions of the IAU* I, p. 158, and Delporte’s [Délimitation scientifique des constellations](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ).

Globus Aerostaticus consequently has **no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, area, boundary, or membership list**. The forms sometimes printed today—**Glo**, **GlA**, and **Globi Aerostatici**—are modern or nineteenth-/twentieth-century editorial conveniences, not IAU standards.

A formally regular Latin genitive would indeed be **Globi Aerostatici**, but its appearance in modern constellation tables does not make it historically official.

There are **no Babylonian, Egyptian, classical Greek, ancient Latin, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, or Sanskrit names for “Globus Aerostaticus.”** It was an eighteenth-century European technological image. Ancient names attached to some of the same stars belong to other figures and are treated below.

### Position and form on Bode’s sky

**Documented fact:** In Bode’s *Uranographia* plate XVI the balloon floats south of Capricornus, east of Microscopium, and beside the shortened northwestern or tail region of Piscis Notius/Piscis Austrinus. The plate contains Aquarius, Capricornus, Piscis Notius, Microscopium, and Globus Aerostaticus.

Bode’s accompanying German description, as transcribed in Barentine, reads:

> *Wurde von de la Lande im Jahr 1798 zum Andenken des von Montgolfier erfundenen Aerostaten als ein neues Sternbild vorgeschlagen. Er steht unterhalb dem Steinbock westwärts beym südlichen Fisch, und ist aus einigen ehemals zum Theil zu diesen Bildern gehörigen kleinen Sternen formirt.*

Gloss: “It was proposed by de la Lande in 1798 as a new constellation in memory of the aerostat invented by Montgolfier. It stands below Capricorn, westward by the Southern Fish, and is formed from several small stars formerly belonging in part to these figures.”

This passage is important because it establishes:

1. Lalande as proposer;
2. 1798 as the proposal date;
3. Montgolfier’s aerostat as the commemoration;
4. the borrowing of faint stars from pre-existing neighboring figures.

The original atlas is preserved in the [ETH Zürich digitization](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); the plate is also identified and reproduced by the [Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/36-bode-s-uranographia-and-its-aftermath-1801-1851/).

## Stars and objects

### Bode’s lettered stars

Bode used **lower-case Roman letters**, not Bayer Greek letters, for the Balloon. In the engraving the *a* can resemble Greek alpha. Later sources occasionally call these “Bayer designations,” but that is misleading: Bayer died in 1625, 173 years before the figure was proposed.

The following reconstruction is the currently circulated concordance. The letters **j** and **o** were skipped in accordance with common alphabetic/cataloguing practice.

| Bode | Historical/modern identifier | Approx. visual magnitude | Modern disposition |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **a** | **4 Piscis Austrini = ε Microscopii** | 4.71 | Microscopium |
| **b** | **8 Piscis Austrini** | 5.73 | Piscis Austrinus |
| **c** | **5 Piscis Austrini** | about 6 | Piscis Austrinus |
| **d** | **3 Piscis Austrini** | about 5.4 | Assigned in modern treatments to the Mic/PsA border region; also associated with Chinese *Tiāntián* |
| **e** | **6 Piscis Austrini** | about 5.97 | Piscis Austrinus |
| **f** | **7 Piscis Austrini**, HD 205529 | 6.10 | Piscis Austrinus |
| **g** | **HD 202287**, HR 8124 | 6.12 | Microscopium |
| **h** | **41 Capricorni**, HD 206356 | 5.24 | Capricornus |
| **i** | **HD 202940**, HR 8148 | 6.7 | Capricornus |
| **k** | **HD 202941** | faint | Capricornus/border region |
| **l** | **HD 206291**, HR 8282 | 6.49 | Capricornus |
| **m** | **HD 205905** | faint | Capricornus/border region |
| **n** | **HD 202773** | faint | Capricornus/Microscopium border region |

**Caution:** Bode’s accompanying description speaks generally of “several” faint stars, while Richard Hinckley Allen says Bode’s *Die Gestirne* gave the Balloon **twenty-two stars**. The familiar modern concordance lists only thirteen lettered stars. These claims are not necessarily contradictory: an engraved figure could contain unlettered plotted stars as well as lettered catalogue members. A complete critical collation of the plate with Bode’s star catalogue remains desirable.

### The brightest star: ε Microscopii / Aerostaticus

The brightest member was Bode’s **a**, formerly **4 Piscis Austrini**, now **ε Microscopii**:

- J2000 position: approximately RA 21h 17m 56.3s, Dec −32° 10′ 21″
- apparent magnitude: about 4.71
- spectral type: approximately A1 V
- distance: about 166 light-years
- traditional proper name before 2026: none

A nomenclatural complication arose because Bode had already used **ε Microscopii** for the star now usually called **2 Piscis Austrini/HD 200763**. After the Balloon disappeared, later practice transferred ε Mic to the brighter 4 PsA/Bode-*a*, leaving Bode’s own ε Microscopii without that letter. This is a genuine historical designation shift, not merely a typographical error.

### The name Aerostaticus, 2026

**Documented modern invention:** On **16 July 2026**, the IAU Working Group on Star Names adopted **Aerostaticus** as the proper name of ε Microscopii, deliberately preserving the memory of the obsolete constellation. The proposal had been under discussion since 2023; WGSN documents considered *Globus* and/or *Aerostaticus*, with the latter fitting the working length convention.

Thus the constellation remains abolished, but its history has now been formally preserved in an IAU-approved **star name**. The modern star is:

- **Aerostaticus**
- **ε Microscopii**
- HR/HIP identification as listed in the IAU catalogue
- IAU constellation: **Microscopium**

This adoption is reported by the [IAU’s 2026 announcement](https://www.iau.org/IAU/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx) and the historical [Aerostaticus record](https://ase.exopla.net/index.php/Aerostaticus).

### Proper names and derivations among the other members

No other Bode Balloon member is known to have possessed an established ancient Greek, Arabic, Latin, or Persian proper name. Their familiar identifiers are catalogue designations:

- **Flamsteed numbers** such as 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 Piscis Austrini order stars primarily by right ascension within Flamsteed’s assigned constellation.
- **HD numbers** derive from the *Henry Draper Catalogue*.
- **HR numbers** derive from the *Harvard Revised Photometry*, subsequently known as the Yale *Bright Star Catalogue*.
- **41 Capricorni** is a Flamsteed designation.

The absence of traditional names is unsurprising: the group’s brightest star is only magnitude 4.7, and most members are near sixth magnitude.

### Internal asterism

The only documented internal figure is the balloon itself:

- a large decorated envelope or globe;
- a narrow lower neck;
- a suspended gondola or basket;
- lettered stars distributed through the envelope, rigging, and vicinity.

No distinct historically named “basket,” “burner,” or “ropes” asterism has been located in Bode’s text.

### Deep-sky objects

**Finding: none can securely be called a Globus Aerostaticus object.**

The reasons are methodological:

1. the figure had no agreed polygonal boundaries;
2. Bode’s pictorial outline is not equivalent to an IAU sky area;
3. nineteenth-century observers normally indexed nebulae by larger accepted constellations or coordinates;
4. no Messier, NGC, IC, Caldwell, or other major deep-sky object is named for Globus Aerostaticus.

Bode plotted large numbers of Herschel nebulae across the *Uranographia*, but no located source establishes a deep-sky object as a member of the Balloon specifically. Assigning a modern Microscopium, Capricornus, or Piscis Austrinus galaxy retrospectively would therefore be a **modern reconstruction**, not historical membership.

### Variables, novae, and supernovae

- **HD 202287** is catalogued as a suspected or low-amplitude pulsating variable in some databases.
- Several modern variables exist in the surrounding IAU constellations, including ZZ and other Piscis Austrini systems, but no source found calls them variables “of Globus Aerostaticus.”
- No historical nova or supernova is documented under the extinct constellation’s name.
- No supernova remnant carries its name.

### Meteor showers

No recognized meteor shower is named the **Globus Aerostaticids**, **Aerostaticids**, or equivalent. Unlike Quadrans Muralis, whose memory survives in the Quadrantids, the Balloon left no established meteor-radiant name.

### Boundary history

#### Ptolemy, second century CE

**Documented ancient catalogue tradition:** Ptolemy’s *Almagest* listed the Southern Fish and then six “unformed” stars outside its figure, described broadly as lying behind the fish’s tail. Modern historians associate part of this region with later Microscopium and the territory subsequently used for the Balloon.

These were not a balloon and carried no aeronautical meaning.

#### Al-Ṣūfī, c. 964

Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* called the Southern Fish **Ṣūrat al-Ḥūt al-Janūbī**, “the figure of the Southern Fish.” Modern historical summaries report that he did not reproduce Ptolemy’s six far-southern unformed stars, probably because he could not observe or identify them securely from his latitude. This is a **scholarly reconstruction**, not an explicit confession by al-Ṣūfī.

#### Bayer, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* retained Piscis Austrinus and assigned Greek letters to its principal stars. No Balloon existed. Some later Balloon stars were unlettered or associated with the Southern Fish.

#### Schiller, 1627

Schiller reused Bayer’s stellar framework. He could not depict Globus Aerostaticus, which would not be invented for another 171 years.

#### Flamsteed, catalogue 1725 / atlas 1729

Flamsteed numbered stars in this zone as members of Piscis Austrinus, including 1–8 PsA. At least 1 PsA became γ Mic and 4 PsA became ε Mic. His allocations explain why Bode’s Balloon appears to have “taken” several stars from the Southern Fish.

#### Lacaille, 1751–56 and 1763

Lacaille erected **Microscopium** in the faint southern region below Capricornus. He redistributed stars formerly regarded as unformed or attached to Piscis Austrinus. This created the immediate cartographic setting into which Lalande inserted the Balloon.

#### Bode, 1801

Bode shortened the pictorial tail or western reach of Piscis Austrinus and occupied the opening between it, Microscopium, and Capricornus. His prose says the faint stars had formerly belonged “in part” to the neighboring figures.

#### Nineteenth century

Different atlases copied the image without imposing uniform legal boundaries. Statements such as “east of Microscopium,” “west of the Southern Fish,” or “under Capricornus” describe the picture rather than a common boundary.

#### Delporte, 1930

Delporte’s B1875 lines placed the sky into the 88 accepted constellations. The historical Balloon’s stars consequently reside in **Microscopium, Piscis Austrinus, and Capricornus**. Precession makes those originally horizontal/vertical B1875 boundaries appear slightly tilted when plotted in J2000 coordinates.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1783: no Balloon constellation

No credible evidence was found for an ancient or medieval balloon figure in these stars. Claims that Chinese sky lanterns, biblical “chariots,” or alchemical vessels anticipated Globus Aerostaticus are retrospective analogies, not genealogical antecedents.

### 4 June 1783 — Annonay

**Documented fact:** Joseph-Michel and Jacques-Étienne Montgolfier publicly launched an unmanned hot-air balloon at Annonay. The Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum describes this as the event that “launched the air age.”

### 27 August 1783 — Paris

**Documented fact:** Jacques Alexandre César Charles launched a small unmanned **hydrogen** balloon from the Champ de Mars. This is a separate technological line from the Montgolfiers’ hot-air machine.

### 19 September 1783 — Versailles

**Documented fact:** A Montgolfier balloon carried a sheep, duck, and rooster before Louis XVI and the court. This was the first famous aerostatic flight with living passengers, but not yet humans.

### 21 November 1783 — Paris

**Documented fact:** Jean-François Pilâtre de Rozier and François Laurent, marquis d’Arlandes, made the first untethered human flight in a Montgolfier hot-air balloon, from La Muette across Paris, lasting about 25 minutes.

### 1 December 1783 — Tuileries

**Documented fact:** Jacques Charles and Nicolas-Louis Robert made the first crewed free flight in a hydrogen balloon. Popular accounts sometimes collapse the hot-air and hydrogen achievements into one “first balloon.” They should be distinguished.

These events are documented together by the [Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/ascension-montgolfier-balloon/nasm_A19370051000), the [Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co29133/first-public-demonstration-of-a-montgolfier-balloon-4th-june-1783), and [ICAO’s aviation-history account](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/postalhistory/aviation_history_human_flights_with_balloons.htm).

### August 1798 — Gotha astronomical congress

**Documented through Lalande’s retrospective account and Bode’s adoption:** Lalande and Bode attended the international astronomical meeting organized by Franz Xaver von Zach at Gotha. Lalande later wrote:

> *Nous sommes convenus d’y ajouter deux nouvelles constellations, la Presse de Goutemberg et le Globe de Montgolfier… J’ai cru que la plus belle découverte des Français méritait bien d’y occuper une place.*

Gloss: “We agreed to add two new constellations there, Gutenberg’s Press and Montgolfier’s Globe… I thought that the finest discovery of the French well deserved to occupy a place there.”

The passage occurs under the year 1798 in Lalande’s *Bibliographie astronomique avec l’histoire de l’astronomie depuis 1781 jusqu’à 1802* (Paris, 1803), p. 798. The [ETH record and digitization](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/923579) supplies the primary work.

“Greatest discovery” is a widespread secondary paraphrase; the reproduced French reads **“la plus belle découverte”**, more literally “the finest/most beautiful discovery.” This is a small but real quotation issue.

The mutual exchange was:

- a French honor: Montgolfier’s balloon;
- a German honor: Gutenberg’s printing press, **Officina Typographica**.

Bode was already engraving the later plates of the *Uranographia*. The Balloon appeared on XVI; the Printing Office on XVIII.

### 1801 — Bode’s publication

**First surviving cartographic appearance located:** Johann Elert Bode, *Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio*, Berlin, plate XVI.

Bode also described it in *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*. Consequently:

- **Lalande invented/proposed it**;
- **Bode first published and graphically realized it**.

Calling Bode the inventor without qualification is therefore misleading; calling Lalande the engraver or atlas author is equally wrong.

### 1807 — Thomas Young

John Barentine records Young’s English name **“Balloon”** and a nonstandard abbreviation/genitive tradition. The precise primary atlas passage was not independently recovered here; this remains documented through Barentine’s collation.

### 1822 — Alexander Jamieson

**Documented fact:** Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas*, London, plate 21, includes **Le Ballon Aerostatique** with Aquarius, Capricornus, Piscis Austrinus, Microscopium, and Corona Australis. A bibliographic description and plate citation survive through the [Linda Hall-derived record](https://journals.ala.org/index.php/lrts/article/viewFile/5782/7243).

Jamieson’s attractive human-scale atlas was important for the figure’s passage from Bode’s monumental scientific atlas into popular education.

### 1824 — Green

Barentine quotes Green’s positional description: “a little to the west of Fomalhaut and south of Capricornus.” This wording illustrates the loose boundaries used by nineteenth-century handbooks.

### c. 1825 — Sidney Hall, *Urania’s Mirror*

**Documented fact:** Card 26 shows Aquarius and Piscis Australis with **Ballon Aerostatique** partly at the lower right. The cards were pierced so their stars appeared luminous when held to light. The surviving image is catalogued in the [Wikimedia historical collection](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Globus_Aerostaticus).

Attribution of *Urania’s Mirror* has itself been historically confused; the set is now generally associated with Richard Rouse Bloxam’s authorship and Sidney Hall’s engraving rather than “Aspin” as sole author. That dispute concerns the card set, not invention of the Balloon.

### 1825 — Hall atlas/chart tradition

A separate “Hall (1825)” chart is cited in modern surveys. Care is needed not to count every reproduction of the *Urania’s Mirror* engraving as an independent atlas reception.

### 1839 — Joseph Johann Littrow

**Documented fact:** Littrow’s *Atlas des gestirnten Himmels*, plate XXII, retained the Balloon along with other Bodean figures. The [Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/joseph-johann-littrow/) identifies it beside Capricornus and Microscopium.

### 1845 — Kendall

Kendall described it as “under Capricornus and west of the Southern Fish,” another non-boundary positional definition.

### 1858 — Bouvier

Bouvier placed it “east of Microscopium and south of Capricornus.” This agrees broadly with Bode but again shows that it functioned as a picture, not a standardized territory.

### Nineteenth century — Secchi

Allen reports that Angelo Secchi still retained it as **Aerostáto** in Italian charts. The exact edition/page was not recovered in the accessible searches, so the claim is best labeled **secondary but plausible**.

### Argelander and Heis

No firm evidence was located that Friedrich Wilhelm Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* or Eduard Heis’s atlas adopted Globus Aerostaticus as a working constellation. Their atlases participated in the nineteenth-century movement toward restrained, observational constellation schemes. Assertions that “nearly all nineteenth-century atlases” carried the Balloon are therefore too broad.

### Burritt

No secure occurrence was located in Elijah H. Burritt’s principal atlas. It should not be silently inferred from Jamieson or *Urania’s Mirror*.

### Flamsteed, Bayer, Hevelius, Lacaille

None could have received Globus Aerostaticus by name: all their relevant works predate 1798. They belong to the region’s prehistory, not its reception.

### 1899 — Richard Hinckley Allen

Allen’s *Star Names and Their Meanings* summarized the constellation as:

> *Globus Aerostaticus, vel Aetherius, the Balloon*

and credited Lalande, Bode, Ideler, Secchi, and the French vernacular. His page became highly influential in modern obsolete-constellation literature. It also appears to have originated or at least uniquely propagated **Aetherius**.

### 1911 — *Encyclopaedia Britannica*

The eleventh edition listed Globus Aerostaticus among Lalande’s innovations but said many such idiosyncratic rearrangements had fallen into oblivion. This shows that it was already treated historically before the IAU standardization.

### 1922–30 — exclusion and reassignment

The IAU standardized the principal list in 1922; Delporte’s boundaries were accepted in 1928 and published in 1930. Globus Aerostaticus received neither an abbreviation nor territory.

### 1988 onward — modern historical recovery

Ian Ridpath’s *Star Tales* and later John C. Barentine’s *The Lost Constellations* reconstructed the figure for modern readers. Online reproductions of Bode and *Urania’s Mirror* made it a familiar example of a lost technological constellation.

### 2023–26 — WGSN preservation

The IAU Working Group on Star Names considered reviving part of the title as a stellar proper name. On 16 July 2026 it adopted **Aerostaticus** for ε Mic, returning a controlled remnant of Lalande and Bode’s balloon to official nomenclature without restoring the constellation.

## Invention and reception

### What the constellation honored

**Documented fact:** Lalande called it the **Globe de Montgolfier**, and Bode said it commemorated the aerostat invented by Montgolfier. The primary evidence therefore favors the Montgolfiers’ hot-air balloon specifically.

The picture nevertheless belonged to the broader “balloon mania” generated by all the French aerostatic experiments of 1783, including Pilâtre de Rozier’s flights and Charles and Robert’s hydrogen balloon. Saying that it honored “the invention of ballooning” is reasonable context; saying that it specifically honored Jacques Charles is unsupported.

### Lalande’s interest in aeronautics

Lalande’s recorded enthusiasm is demonstrated by his choice of the balloon as “la plus belle découverte des Français” and his effort to place it permanently in the sky. I found no evidence that Lalande was himself an aeronaut or balloon designer. “Enthusiast for aeronautics” is supportable; “pioneer balloonist” is not.

### The technological sky

The Balloon was part of an Enlightenment-era migration from mythic beings toward instruments, machines, and national achievements.

Nearby or contemporary examples include:

- Lacaille’s **Microscopium**, **Telescopium**, **Antlia Pneumatica**, **Fornax**, **Horologium**, **Octans**, **Circinus**, **Norma**, and **Reticulum**;
- Bode’s **Machina Electrica**, representing an electrostatic apparatus;
- **Officina Typographica**, honoring Gutenberg’s printing press;
- Lalande’s **Quadrans Muralis**, honoring the astronomical mural quadrant.

The “French balloon/German press” pairing was explicitly negotiated by Lalande and Bode. It is stronger evidence than the later notion that Bode independently invented a whole technological family.

### Why it failed

No contemporary IAU memorandum explaining the rejection of this individual figure was found. The likely causes are a **scholarly reconstruction**:

- its stars were faint;
- it intruded into already established constellations;
- its membership and limits were not standardized;
- relatively few authoritative working catalogues adopted it;
- by 1900 it was mainly a historical curiosity;
- scientific nomenclature required a stable, non-overlapping set.

This is a case of limited adoption followed by standardization—not evidence that astronomers objected to balloons as such.

## Myths and legends

### Classical myth

**Finding: none.** Globus Aerostaticus does not appear in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’ *De astronomia* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus or the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias;
- any classical catasteristic corpus.

No god placed it in the heavens; no person, beast, or object was metamorphosed into it. Its “catasterism” is a conscious 1798 act by two astronomers.

### Modern foundation narrative

The closest thing to a myth is the Enlightenment foundation story: humanity conquered the air in 1783, and Lalande rewarded that triumph with celestial immortality in 1798. Unlike an ancient myth, its creators, motive, date, and publication can be documented.

Later popular accounts sometimes embellish this into “the Montgolfier brothers’ actual balloon was placed among the stars.” The chart is symbolic; it does not reproduce a uniquely identified surviving craft with demonstrable heraldic fidelity.

### Balloon precursors

Stories that Zhuge Liang invented a hot-air balloon or that ancient sky lanterns anticipated the Montgolfiers are traditions about terrestrial aeronautics. No evidence connects them to this constellation or its stars.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Mesopotamian and Babylonian

No Mesopotamian asterism can securely be equated with the entire Balloon. Claims that the whole region was a Babylonian Southern Fish conflate a much larger ancient figure with Bode’s small borrowed patch.

Piscis Austrinus itself has been compared with Mesopotamian fish traditions, but the Balloon stars are peripheral and faint. No cuneiform text naming a “balloon,” airborne globe, or equivalent in this exact group exists.

### Egyptian

No Egyptian decan or Dendera figure has been securely identified with Bode’s Balloon stars. General claims that all later constellations possess Egyptian antecedents are unsupported here.

### Greek and Roman

The stars were unformed stars behind Piscis Austrinus or simply faint background stars. They were not a distinct classical constellation.

### Arabic and Persian

Al-Ṣūfī’s name for Piscis Austrinus was:

- **Ṣūrat al-Ḥūt al-Janūbī** — “the figure/image of the Southern Fish.”

His work was composed in Arabic at Shiraz in a Persian courtly environment, but this does not create a separate Persian balloon name. The six very southerly Ptolemaic unformed stars were reportedly omitted or not identified by him.

No securely documented Bedouin asterism was found for the exact Bode Balloon group. Given the stars’ faintness and low altitude from northern Arabia, absence is plausible, but it should not be converted into proof that none ever existed.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No premodern Hebrew name for Globus Aerostaticus was located. Hebrew translations of “balloon” or mappings of the Southern Fish are modern linguistic equivalents.

No primary Kabbalistic text was found assigning these particular stars to a sefirah, Hebrew letter, tribe, angel, or ritual object. Popular “biblical zodiac” systems that map every constellation to scripture postdate and reinterpret the Western atlas tradition. They are not evidence for an ancient Hebrew Balloon.

### Chinese astronomy

The relevant stars did not form one Chinese Balloon. They were distributed among traditional Chinese asterisms in the **Black Tortoise of the North**:

- **Líyú** (璃瑜), “Jade Ornament on Ladies’ Wear”:
  - α Mic — Líyú 1
  - **ε Mic / Bode a — Líyú 2**
  - γ Mic — an added star
  - HD 200763 — an added star
  - 5 PsA and 6 PsA are also placed in Líyú in some expanded catalogues
- **Tiāntián** (天田), “Celestial Farmland”:
  - **3 PsA** is identified as Tiāntián 1 in modern concordances
- **Tiānqián** (天錢), “Celestial Money”:
  - includes several Piscis Austrinus stars; 7 and 8 PsA appear as later/additional members in expanded lists
- **Jiǔkǎn** (九坎), “Nine Water Wells”:
  - formed mainly from other Microscopium stars.

These identifications are concordances between traditional Chinese star catalogues and modern Western designations, not proof that every listed “additional” star belonged to the earliest recension. The [Chinese Microscopium concordance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopium_in_Chinese_astronomy) and [Piscis Austrinus concordance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piscis_Austrinus_in_Chinese_astronomy) preserve their source trail to Chinese educational and Hong Kong Space Museum tables.

### Indian astronomy

The Balloon lies well south of the ecliptic. Its celestial longitude falls broadly beneath the Aquarius sector crossed by the later lunar mansions **Dhaniṣṭhā**, **Śatabhiṣaj/Śatabhiṣā**, and **Pūrvabhādrapadā**, depending on coordinate epoch and the star considered. This does **not** establish membership: Indian nakṣatras are anchored primarily by ecliptic stars or divisions, not by every remote southern star sharing their longitude.

No Sanskrit asterism encompassing Bode’s thirteen lettered Balloon stars was located.

### Julius Schiller’s Christianized sky, 1627

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* predates Globus Aerostaticus. It therefore supplies no Christian replacement for the Balloon itself.

The surrounding Bayer stars then assigned to **Piscis Austrinus** were Christianized as:

- **Hydriae Farinae Sareptanae Viduae** — the flour jar(s) of the widow of Zarephath, from the Elijah narrative in 1 Kings 17.

Whether every later Balloon star fell within Schiller’s flour-vessel outline requires star-by-star comparison of the engraving. It is incorrect to say that Schiller replaced “the Balloon” with the widow’s jar; he replaced the pre-existing Southern Fish, before the Balloon existed. The primary atlas is digitized by the [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/) and [ETH Zürich](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720).

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No pre-1798 alchemical or Hermetic interpretation of Globus Aerostaticus exists. Later writers could assimilate a floating sphere to air, sublimation, ascent, or the alchemical element, but no source-driven continuous tradition was found.

The nearby **Fornax Chemica** is genuinely related to the imagery of chemistry. That does not make the Balloon an alchemical vessel.

### Astrology

Because the constellation was invented after the main corpus of traditional Western astrology had formed:

- Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* gives it no planetary nature;
- it is not a Behenian fixed star or image;
- it has no traditional rulership, dignity, or mundane signification;
- it does not constitute a lunar mansion.

Modern astrological websites sometimes assign the group zodiacal longitudes in Aquarius and invent interpretive meanings from flight, elevation, instability, technology, or speculation. These are **modern astrological inventions**, not inherited doctrine. Modern calculations placing ε Mic near tropical Aquarius describe longitude, not IAU constellation membership.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas, second century CE Roman copy

No Balloon appears. The Farnese globe predates its invention by roughly sixteen centuries and represents classical constellations. Any attempt to identify a round classical object on it as Globus Aerostaticus is anachronistic.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, tenth century onward

They depict the Southern Fish as **al-Ḥūt al-Janūbī**, usually in paired “as seen in the sky/as seen on the globe” orientations. No balloon appears. The exact later Balloon stars may be absent where al-Ṣūfī could not verify Ptolemy’s unformed southern stars.

### Dürer, Stabius, and Heinfogel, 1515

Dürer’s northern and southern celestial maps antedate the Balloon. The southern sheet displays the inherited classical sky, not an aerostat.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* supplies the early-modern pictorial Southern Fish and nearby unformed space. No Balloon.

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Hevelius’ *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* also predates balloon flight and Lalande’s proposal. No Balloon.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

As noted, the relevant older figure became the widow of Zarephath’s flour container. This is not a Christian balloon.

### Lacaille, Paris/Cape tradition, 1756 and 1763

Lacaille introduced the **Microscope** beside the future site. His restrained scientific-instrument imagery created the iconographic precedent Lalande explicitly invoked.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

This is the decisive image. Plate XVI presents a richly engraved spherical aerostat with suspended car. It is embedded among the figures rather than drawn as a modern line diagram. Bode’s plate remains the canonical portrait.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Plate 21 adapted the Balloon in a smaller popular atlas as **Le Ballon Aerostatique**. Jamieson simplified Bode’s vast cartography but retained pictorial elegance.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, c. 1825

The constellation appears partly at the lower right of the Aquarius/Piscis Australis card. The piercings mark stars; the colored lithographic/engraved figure makes the aerostat part of domestic astronomical education.

### Littrow, Vienna, 1839

Littrow retained it in a chart lineage heavily indebted to Bode. Its presence demonstrates survival beyond British popular cards into Central European atlas making.

### Secchi, Rome, nineteenth century

Allen reports an Italian **Aerostáto**. Exact plate and edition remain an open bibliographical question.

### Argelander and Heis

No Balloon image was verified. Their omission, if confirmed from complete atlases, would chart its transition from decorative atlas figure to historical relic.

### Coins, ceilings, cathedral portals, and calendars

No coin, ancient ceiling, cathedral portal, or medieval calendar can depict this constellation under its proper identity. Balloon imagery became widespread in medals, prints, ceramics, clothing, and calendars after 1783, but a round balloon alone is not evidence of the constellation.

No securely catalogued architectural ceiling explicitly labels the figure *Globus Aerostaticus*.

## People, events, and works

### Jérôme Lalande

Proposer of the constellation; astronomer, calculator, popularizer, and participant in the Gotha congress. His own retrospective text supplies the strongest evidence for motive and attribution.

### Johann Elert Bode

First publisher and visual designer of the figure. His atlas fixed its Latin and German forms and selected its stars.

### The Montgolfier brothers

Joseph-Michel and Jacques-Étienne Montgolfier developed and publicly demonstrated the hot-air balloon. Their surname produced French *montgolfière*, now the ordinary word for a hot-air balloon.

### Pilâtre de Rozier and d’Arlandes

First humans to make a free hot-air-balloon flight, 21 November 1783. They were part of the achievement commemorated culturally, although Lalande named Montgolfier rather than the aeronauts.

### Jacques Charles and Nicolas-Louis Robert

Made the first crewed free hydrogen-balloon flight, 1 December 1783. Their machine belongs to the same balloon age but is not the documented namesake.

### Franz Xaver von Zach and the Gotha meeting

The 1798 Gotha congress provided the institutional encounter at which Lalande and Bode agreed on the Balloon and Printing Press.

### Alexander Jamieson, Sidney Hall, Littrow, Secchi

These cartographers and educators prolonged the figure’s life after Bode.

### Explorers and navigation

No expedition or mariner is documented as having “steered by Globus Aerostaticus.” Its stars are too faint to be leading navigational markers, and the figure was never standard in nautical astronomy. Fomalhaut and brighter Capricornus stars, not the Balloon, were the useful regional landmarks.

### Discoverers of objects

No major comet, planet, nebula, cluster, nova, or galaxy discovery was officially reported under the constellation’s name in the sources consulted.

### Literature, painting, and music

The 1783 balloon craze generated poems, novels, engravings, fashions, operatic entertainments, and music. Examples include late-eighteenth-century German poems and theatrical works titled *Der Luftballon*. These belong to the same cultural moment but have no demonstrated dependence on Bode’s constellation.

No canonical poem, painting, symphony, building, ship, spacecraft, or scientific mission was found to be explicitly named **Globus Aerostaticus** after the constellation.

A modern Polish student stratospheric-balloon project has used *Globus Aerostaticus* as a title, but that is a modern homage, not continuity of astronomical usage.

### Paris 2024 Olympic cauldron

A balloon-like cauldron rose during the 2024 Paris Olympic opening ceremony. Some modern constellation-history pages compare it with the Montgolfière. There is no evidence that it was designed as a representation of Bode’s constellation.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Lalande or Bode: who invented it?

**Resolution:** Lalande proposed it; Bode first drew and published it.

Evidence:

- Lalande: “we agreed to add” the Montgolfier Globe, 1798;
- Bode: explicitly says it “was proposed by de la Lande in 1798”;
- Bode’s *Uranographia*: first located chart, 1801.

Italian and popular summaries that call it simply “introduced by Bode” describe publication, not intellectual origin.

### 2. Was it created in 1798 or 1801?

Both dates apply to different acts:

- **1798:** proposal/agreement at Gotha;
- **1801:** first surviving publication in Bode.

Calling 1801 the “invention date” obscures Lalande’s testimony.

### 3. Did it honor hot-air ballooning or all balloons?

Primary evidence says **Montgolfier**, hence hot-air balloon. Jacques Charles’s hydrogen flight supplies historical context but is not named in the dedication.

### 4. “La plus grande” or “la plus belle découverte”?

Modern English accounts frequently say Lalande called ballooning “the greatest discovery of the French.” The accessible French transcription reads **la plus belle découverte**, “the finest/most beautiful discovery.” Unless another edition reads *grande*, quotation marks around “greatest” should be avoided.

### 5. “Globus Aerostaticus” means “hot-air balloon”

Not exactly word-for-word. It means “aerostatic globe/ball.” In eighteenth-century usage an *aérostat* could be a lighter-than-air craft generally. Its dedication supplies the specific Montgolfier hot-air meaning.

### 6. Aetherius

**Disputed/late:** No evidence was found that Lalande or Bode called it *Aetherius*. Allen’s 1899 wording appears to be the source. It may be an elegant Latin synonym or editorial invention.

### 7. Official abbreviation and genitive

Claims that **Glo** or **GlA** and **Globi Aerostatici** are “the” abbreviation and genitive are misleading. They occur in modern historical catalogues. The IAU never standardized them.

### 8. “Abolished by a 1922 IAU vote”

The IAU selected the 88 principal constellations and their abbreviations. Globus Aerostaticus was absent. No separate anti-Balloon resolution was located. “Excluded” or “not retained” is more exact than “abolished,” although the latter is conventional shorthand.

### 9. Its stars all went to Piscis Austrinus

False. The recovered membership spans **Microscopium, Piscis Austrinus, and Capricornus**. The brightest, ε Mic, is now in Microscopium; 41 Cap and several HD stars lie in Capricornus.

### 10. Its stars all went to Microscopium

Also false. This error likely arises because the brightest and historically most discussed member is ε Mic.

### 11. Thirteen stars or twenty-two?

Bode lettered thirteen stars *a–n* with *j* omitted, according to the standard concordance. Allen says Bode’s German treatment counted twenty-two. Until plate and catalogue are collated star by star, both numbers should be reported with their differing meanings.

### 12. Bode’s *a* as “Alpha Globus Aerostatici”

It is a lower-case Roman **a**, though visually alpha-like. Calling it “α Globus Aerostatici” retroactively turns Bode’s non-Bayer lettering into a Greek Bayer designation.

### 13. The ε Microscopii switch

Bode’s ε Mic was not the star now called ε Mic. Modern ε Mic was Bode’s Balloon-*a* and Flamsteed 4 PsA. This reassignment is a major source of mistaken star lists.

### 14. The image’s orientation

Historical celestial maps may be drawn as seen from Earth or from outside a celestial globe. Mirrored web reproductions can reverse east and west. Positional prose and plate orientation must therefore be distinguished.

### 15. Antiquity, astrology, and “lost lore”

No ancient myth, Babylonian balloon, Egyptian aerostat, Behenian star, or classical astrological nature was recovered. Such material, where asserted, is modern esoteric invention.

### 16. Hoaxes

The dedicated searches found no famous hoax specifically centered on Globus Aerostaticus. The recurrent problems are misattribution, retrospective antiquity, invented official abbreviations, and unmarked modern astrology rather than a single organized hoax.

## Open questions

1. **Complete Bode membership:** Which nine unlettered stars account for Allen’s total of twenty-two, and are they explicitly catalogued under the Balloon in Bode’s *Allgemeine Beschreibung*?
2. **Lalande–Bode correspondence:** Does surviving correspondence from 1798 describe the exchange in more detail than Lalande’s 1803 retrospective?
3. **First printed occurrence:** Did an ephemeral Gotha circular, globe, manuscript chart, or periodical display the Balloon between August 1798 and Bode’s 1801 atlas?
4. **Aetherius:** Did Allen invent the synonym, inherit it from an unidentified source, or misunderstand an adjective?
5. **Ideler:** Which exact work and edition contains *Luft Ball*, and does it indeed count twenty-two stars?
6. **Secchi:** Which atlas, plate, and edition contains *Aerostáto*?
7. **Argelander and Heis:** Complete page-by-page verification is needed to turn apparent omission into a firm bibliographical finding.
8. **Burritt:** Was the Balloon present in any edition or derivative chart, or is its association with Burritt an inference from other popular atlases?
9. **Chinese concordances:** Which Balloon members belong to the oldest Chinese catalogue layers, and which were later *zēng* (“added”) stars?
10. **The “830 stars in 109 groups” formulation:** No primary Bode passage connecting that exact total with Globus Aerostaticus or a named “Bode collection on chain” was located. It may refer to an external collection schema rather than the 1801 atlas itself.
11. **WGSN dossier:** Publication of the full etymological decision record for *Aerostaticus* would clarify why the star was selected and how the sixteen-character issue was applied.
