# Cassiopeia: Research Dossier

> **Status: KEPT.** Cassiopeia is one of the 88 constellations recognized by the International Astronomical Union. Its official abbreviation is **Cas** and its Latin genitive is **Cassiopeiae**. The constellation name was standardized in 1922; Eugène Delporte’s rectilinear boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in 1930. No part of Cassiopeia was abolished, although the modern boundary—not the old painted queen—now determines which stars and deep-sky objects belong to it. [IAU constellation list and history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

## Editorial method and evidentiary labels

The following labels distinguish kinds of claim:

- **Documented fact**: supported by a surviving text, object, catalogue, observation, or official record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction**: a modern historical or astronomical inference from incomplete evidence.
- **Tradition**: a culturally transmitted identification demonstrably present in sources but not necessarily factual.
- **Disputed**: a claim for which qualified scholars or observations materially disagree.
- **Unverified claim**: repeated in modern literature but not traced to adequate primary evidence.
- **Myth**: a narrative presented by ancient or traditional sources as sacred, heroic, or cosmological history.
- **Legend**: a later narrative attached to a historical person, place, or object.
- **Modern invention**: a recent name, identification, or interpretation sometimes presented as ancient.

“Cassiopeia” below may mean three different things that must not be confused:

1. the five-star **W/M asterism**;
2. the ancient and early-modern **figural constellation**, whose membership varied by catalogue;
3. the precisely bounded **IAU territory** established by Delporte.

## Basic identification

### Official astronomical identity

| Field | Official or current value |
|---|---|
| Latin nominative | Cassiopeia |
| Latin genitive | Cassiopeiae |
| IAU abbreviation | Cas |
| Conventional English figure | the Seated Queen; the Queen |
| Status | One of the 88 recognized constellations |
| Ptolemaic status | One of Ptolemy’s 48 constellations |
| Modern area | approximately 598 square degrees, 25th largest |
| Principal asterism | the W, becoming an M when rotated |
| Brightest star by standard visual magnitude | α Cassiopeiae, Schedar, about magnitude 2.24; γ Cas can vary enough to rival or exceed it |
| Modern neighbors | Camelopardalis, Cepheus, Lacerta, Andromeda, Perseus |
| General location | northern Milky Way, circumpolar from much of the north temperate zone |

These official forms are given by the [IAU](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/). “Seated Queen” is descriptive, not a separate official Latin name.

### The name

**Documented fact.** Greek sources use forms including Κασσιέπεια, *Kassiepeia*, and Κασσιόπεια, *Kassiόpeia*. Latin writers and manuscripts offer *Cassiopeia*, *Cassiepeia*, *Cassiepia*, *Cassiopea*, and *Cassiope*. Hyginus commonly has *Cassiepia* or *Cassiepeia*. Medieval spellings are exceptionally unstable because the name passed through Greek, Arabic, and Latin manuscript traditions.

**Disputed/unknown etymology.** No secure Greek derivation is known. Attempts to analyze the name through Greek words for “cassia,” “voice,” “eye,” “beauty,” or “shining” are folk or speculative etymologies. The responsible conclusion remains “a pre-Greek or otherwise unexplained proper name.” [Etymonline cautiously lists the origin as unknown](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Cassiopeia).

Common vernacular descendants include:

- English: **Cassiopeia**, formerly also *Cassiopea* and *Cassiope’s Chair*.
- Spanish: **Casiopea**.
- Portuguese: **Cassiopeia** or **Cassiopeia/Cassiopeia** depending on orthography.
- French: **Cassiopée**.
- Italian: **Cassiopea**.
- German: **Kassiopeia**, traditionally also **die Himmels-W** and **das Himmels-W** for the asterism.
- Dutch: **Cassiopeia**.
- Russian: **Кассиопея**, *Kassiopeya*.
- Medieval Latin descriptive names: *Mulier sedens*, “the seated woman”; *Regina*, “queen”; *Inthronata*, “the enthroned woman”; and forms of “lady in the chair” translated from Arabic.

### Greek, Latin, Arabic, and Persian descriptive names

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy’s catalogue title is essentially “the constellation of Cassiepeia.” Aratus describes the wife of Cepheus but does not need to retell her entire story because the “hapless family of Cepheus” was already familiar.

**Documented fact.** In Arabic Ptolemaic astronomy the standard figure became:

- **ذات الكرسي — Dhāt al-Kursī**, “the Lady/Possessor of the Chair” or “She of the Chair.”
- Al-Ṣūfī lists it as **Ṣūrat Dhāt al-Kursī**, “the figure of the Lady in the Chair.” [Al-Ṣūfī constellation index](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_constellations.htm)

The title is a translation and descriptive recasting of the Greek seated woman, not evidence that a queen identical to Cassiopeia existed independently in pre-Islamic Arabia.

**Documented tradition.** A second, indigenous Arabic sky figure overlapped the same region:

- **الكف الخضيب — al-Kaff al-Khaḍīb**, “the Henna-Dyed/Stained Hand.”
- It was part of the gigantic outstretched hand or arm of **al-Thurayyā**, the Pleiades.
- Ibn Qutayba describes five bright stars in the Milky Way as the extended hand.
- Al-Ṣūfī warns that the group-name was sometimes narrowed to a single astrolabe star, β Cas.

Recent work identifies the five historical “hand” stars more probably as α, β, γ, δ, and ζ Cas, not automatically the modern W’s ε Cas. This follows their recorded medieval brightnesses and is important because γ Cas is variable. [Fischer et al. on the Arabic records and star identifications](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/asna.70024)

**Documented tradition.** β Cas was also associated with **al-Sanām al-Nāqa**, “the she-camel’s hump,” within an overlapping Arabian/Persian camel figure. “Persian Cassiopeia was a camel” is too simple: the sources preserve intersecting Ptolemaic, Bedouin, Arabic, and Persianate mappings, not a single national constellation.

### Hebrew names

**Documented fact.** Modern Hebrew ordinarily transliterates the international name as **קסיופאה**, *Qasyopēʾah/Kasiopia*. “The Queen” may be rendered descriptively as *ha-Malkah*, but it is not an established ancient biblical constellation name.

**Absence of evidence.** No securely ancient Hebrew constellation corresponding uniquely to Cassiopeia has been demonstrated. Biblical *mazzalot*, *kesil*, *kimah*, and *ash/ayish* cannot responsibly be assigned to Cassiopeia merely because later writers wished to Christianize or Hebraize the classical sky.

## Stars, asterisms, and boundaries

### The W and the Chair

**Documented fact.** The familiar W is usually drawn:

**β Cas (Caph) — α Cas (Schedar) — γ Cas — δ Cas (Ruchbah) — ε Cas (Segin).**

Turned through the sky’s daily and seasonal rotation it becomes an M, a flattened E, or a zigzag. Aratus, however, compared its alternately placed stars to the keys or crossbar mechanism of a double door, not to a Latin W, a letter unavailable to him. [Aratus, *Phaenomena* 179–204](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AratusPhaenomena.html)

The “Chair” may be drawn with four or six stars depending on the atlas. The W is therefore a modern pedagogical line figure laid across an older anatomical and furniture-based image.

#### Schedar

**Documented fact.** From Arabic **صدر**, *ṣadr*, “breast/chest,” an anatomical label derived from the Ptolemaic figure. Forms include Schedar, Shedar, Shedir, Shadar, and Seder. Hevelius used *Schedir*. The name belongs officially to α Cas.

#### Caph

**Documented fact, with frequent popular distortion.** Caph is ultimately Arabic **kaff**, “palm/hand,” shortened from the indigenous asterism *al-Kaff al-Khaḍīb*. It did not originally mean “the hand of Cassiopeia.” Medieval and modern atlas-makers reattached the inherited word to the Greek queen. β Cas was also the camel’s hump in another regional figure.

#### Gamma Cassiopeiae: Tiansi, Tsih, Cih, and Navi

**Documented fact.** γ Cas is the prototype of the γ Cassiopeiae class: rapidly rotating Be stars with emission lines, circumstellar disks, irregular optical changes, and unusually hard X-ray emission.

**Documented fact.** The IAU-approved name **Tiansi** derives from the Chinese asterism 天駟, “Celestial Four-Horse Chariot.”

**Modern invention/tradition.** “Tsih” or “Cih” derives from a historical romanization associated with the Chinese whip/coachman complex, but its mapping has varied.

**Modern invention.** “Navi” was coined during the Apollo era from astronaut Virgil Ivan “Gus” Grissom’s middle name spelled backward. It belongs informally to γ Cas, just as “Dnoces” and “Regor” reverse the names of Edward White II and Roger Chaffee. It is not Arabic, classical, or IAU-official.

**Popular error.** Some lists attach Navi to ε Cas; that is incorrect. [Discussion of the confusion on the ε Cas page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epsilon_Cassiopeiae)

#### Ruchbah

**Documented fact.** From Arabic **ركبة**, *rukbah*, “knee.” It is a contraction of a phrase such as “the knee of the Lady in the Chair.” δ Cas is an eclipsing binary of the Algol class.

#### Segin

**Disputed etymology.** Segin is not securely derived from a Cassiopeian Arabic body-part name. The best-supported account makes it an erroneous transfer or transcription of **Seginus**, historically attached to γ Boötis, itself the product of a difficult manuscript history. It became official for ε Cas after long modern usage. Claims that it is ancient “Arabic for foot” are unsupported.

#### Achird

**Modern documented name, etymology unresolved.** Achird appears to have entered star atlases with Antonín Bečvář’s *Atlas Coeli Skalnaté Pleso* in 1950. Proposed Arabic senses such as “girdle” have not been substantiated. η Cas is a nearby yellow-dwarf binary, approximately 19 light-years away.

### Bayer and Flamsteed systems

**Documented fact.** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* (Augsburg, 1603) assigned Greek letters within Cassiopeia; these were not a simple descending brightness list. Position, catalogue order, and graphic convenience mattered. γ Cas’s variability also makes retrospective brightness rankings misleading.

Flamsteed numbers in Cassiopeia run through a larger set than the five W stars. The conspicuous correspondences include:

- 11 Cas = β
- 15 Cas = κ
- 17 Cas = ζ
- 18 Cas = α
- 24 Cas = η
- 27 Cas = γ
- 30 Cas = μ
- 34 Cas = φ
- 37 Cas = δ
- 45 Cas = ε

Historical Flamsteed designations near constellation edges must be treated carefully: numbers arose from catalogue/atlas organization before Delporte’s borders. Some “Flamsteed stars of Cassiopeia” in old lists lie in neighboring modern constellations, while modern Cassiopeia contains stars never assigned a Cassiopeian Flamsteed number.

### Ptolemy’s figure

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy, *Almagest* VII–VIII, catalogued **13 stars in the figure of Cassiopeia**. Descriptions anatomically placed stars in the head, breast, shoulders, arms, knees, and chair. A modern concordance of the entries and proposed identifications is available in [Peters and Knobel’s revision](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ptolemy%27s_catalogue_of_stars-_a_revision_of_the_Almagest_%28IA_cu31924012300491%29.pdf) and this [Cassiopeia extraction](https://pbarbier.com/catalogs/ptolemy/CAS.pdf).

**Scholarly caution.** Identifying every ancient catalogue entry with a modern Bayer star is not always certain. Precession can be calculated, but copy errors, rounded coordinates, magnitude changes, and ambiguous anatomical descriptions remain.

### Boundary history

#### Ptolemy, c. 150 CE

There was no polygonal territory. A constellation was a named figure plus catalogued stars “in” and occasionally “outside” it. Empty sky had no requirement to belong to any constellation.

#### Bayer, 1603

Bayer gave Cassiopeia a full engraved plate and lettered stars around the ancient image. His plates did not establish legal borders. Stars printed on a plate might belong to adjacent figures or merely provide positional context.

#### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* depicted Cassiopeia from the globe-maker’s outside-looking-in convention, reversing left and right relative to a terrestrial sky view. He expanded the plotted telescopic-star field but did not legislate a closed boundary.

#### Flamsteed, 1729 atlas

Flamsteed’s catalogue numbers and atlas figures imposed stronger working habits but still no universal territorial partition. The catalogue and atlas were prepared and published through a contentious editorial history.

#### Lacaille, 1750s

Lacaille primarily reformed the southern sky. He did not invent or abolish Cassiopeia. His influence lies in helping make constellations catalogue regions rather than merely pictures.

#### Bode, 1801

Bode’s plate IV plots Cassiopeia with Perseus, Andromeda, Triangulum, Lacerta, and the now-obsolete Honores Friderici. Again, the engraved bodies overlap star fields without modern legal borders. [Bode plate IV, David Rumsey Collection](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~283909~90056544%3AIV--Cassiopeia----Coelum-Stellatum-)

#### Argelander and Heis, nineteenth century

Increasingly dense naked-eye catalogues encouraged practical constellation limits, but conventions differed among national atlases. Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* and Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* reduced the dominance of pictures and emphasized charted stars.

#### IAU and Delporte, 1922–1930

**Documented fact.**

- 1922, Rome: the IAU adopted the canonical list of 88 constellation names and three-letter abbreviations.
- 1925, Cambridge: Delporte presented a boundary scheme.
- 1928, Leiden: the IAU approved the finished boundaries.
- 1930: *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* and the accompanying atlas were published.

The borders follow right ascension and declination lines for the epoch B1875.0. Precession makes them look slightly slanted in modern coordinate grids. Delporte was instructed, where practicable, to preserve established variable-star designations. [IAU account](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

**What Cassiopeia lost or gained.** There is no defensible single ledger of transfers because pre-1930 figures were not mutually exclusive territories. The modern northern and northeastern frontier fixed divisions with Cepheus and Camelopardalis; the southeastern and southern lines fixed divisions with Perseus and Andromeda; the west/southwest frontier fixed Lacerta. Some stars printed within Cassiopeia’s robes, throne, or surrounding field in older atlases consequently reside today in one of those neighbors. Conversely, every object inside Delporte’s polygon is now Cassiopeian even if no historical artist placed it on the queen.

### Bode’s labels and presentation

**Documented fact.** Bode’s formal Latin title is *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio viginti tabulis aeneis incisa…*, published in Berlin in 1801. Plate IV is headed by Cassiopeia and its neighboring constellations. The atlas contains twenty very large copperplates and plots more than 17,000 stars. [ETH Zürich digital edition](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148)

The associated text volume is *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne nebst Verzeichniss der geraden Aufsteigung und Abweichung von 17240 Sternen, Doppelsternen, Nebelflecken und Sternhaufen*—“General description and identification of the stars, with a catalogue of the right ascension and declination of 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae and star clusters.” [Digitized text-volume record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

Cassiopeia is labelled in the international Latin form **CASSIOPEIA**. Bode’s German explanatory usage is **Cassiopea/Cassiopeia**, the queen or woman seated on a chair. He did not invent the constellation.

**Unverified collection-specific statement.** The supplied phrase “Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be securely identified in the consulted Bode editions or institutional catalogue records. It may describe a particular modern database, exhibit, linked-data collection, or indexing project rather than Bode’s own statistics. It should not be attributed to the *Uranographia* without a collection record defining “chain,” “stars,” and “groups.”

## Deep-sky objects

Cassiopeia lies across the Milky Way and is unusually rich in open clusters, emission nebulae, supernova remnants, and star-forming regions. A complete modern database query returns thousands of objects; the historically and popularly important ones follow.

- **NGC 559**: open cluster.

- **NGC 637**: open cluster.
- **NGC 654**: compact open cluster.
- **NGC 659**: open cluster.
- **NGC 663**: rich open cluster containing several Be stars.
- **NGC 7789**: Caroline’s Rose or the White Rose Cluster, discovered by Caroline Herschel in 1783.
- **NGC 281**: emission nebula and star-forming region, popularly the Pacman Nebula.
- **NGC 7635**: Bubble Nebula, ionized by the massive star BD+60°2522.
- **IC 10**: Local Group dwarf irregular/starburst galaxy, heavily obscured by the Milky Way.
- **IC 1805**: cluster and associated Heart Nebula region.
- **IC 1848**: cluster/nebular complex popularly called the Soul Nebula.
- **IC 59 and IC 63**: reflection/emission nebulae illuminated by γ Cas; IC 63 is popularly the Ghost of Cassiopeia.
- **Sh2-188**: unusual, strongly asymmetric planetary nebula.
- **W3, W4, and W5**: giant star-forming complexes in the Perseus arm.
- **Cassiopeia A**: supernova remnant and radio source.
- **Tycho’s SNR**, 3C 10: remnant of SN 1572.
- **3C 58**: pulsar-wind nebula formerly favored as the remnant of SN 1181, though that identification is now seriously challenged by Pa 30.
- **Pa 30**, around IRAS 00500+6713: currently the strongest candidate remnant of SN 1181.

### Objects carrying “Cassiopeia” in their names

“Cassiopeia A” is the most famous, but radio cataloguing generated Cassiopeia A, B, and other regional designations. The letters in radio-source names do not signify Bayer stars. “Cassiopeia A” was the strongest radio source historically recognized in that constellation region, not “star A of Cassiopeia.”

Dwarf galaxies named **Cassiopeia I, II, III**, and related labels belong to modern survey nomenclature; some are satellites or candidate satellites of M31. The name describes apparent sky location, not physical membership in a “Cassiopeia system.”

## Variables, novae, and supernovae

### Gamma Cassiopeiae

**Documented fact.** γ Cas is the prototype of the γ Cas variable class and a foundational object in the history of emission-line stars. It undergoes irregular brightness changes and disk cycles. Its historical variability helps explain why ancient and early-modern catalogues disagree about which Cassiopeian star was brightest.

### Rho Cassiopeiae

**Documented fact.** ρ Cas is a rare yellow hypergiant that has undergone major outbursts, including episodes around 1945–47, 1985–86, 2000–01, and later changes. During “shell” events it cools, fades, and expels substantial material.

### Other notable variables

- **β Cas**: Delta Scuti variable.
- **δ Cas**: eclipsing/Algol-type system.
- **ε Cas**: low-amplitude variable or suspected variable in historical classifications.
- **η Cas**: nearby binary with activity-related variability.
- **V509 Cas**: yellow hypergiant with secular and irregular variation.
- **V373 Cas**, **V635 Cas**, **V649 Cas**, and numerous Be/X-ray binaries illustrate the young stellar population of the Galactic plane.
- **WZ Cas**: carbon-rich giant notable for lithium.
- **R Cas**: Mira variable, ranging through many magnitudes.
- **V1405 Cas**: Nova Cassiopeiae 2021.

### SN 1181

**Documented historical event.** Chinese and Japanese records describe a stationary “guest star” appearing in 1181 and remaining visible for approximately 185 days. The positional language places it among several asterisms in the Cassiopeia region.

**History of interpretation.**

1. Modern historians recognized the record as a likely Galactic supernova.
2. For decades, the pulsar-wind nebula **3C 58** was the favored remnant.
3. Expansion and age estimates made 3C 58 problematic.
4. Recent historical-position analysis and physical dating support **Pa 30** and its peculiar central star as the better match, perhaps the remnant of a subluminous Type Iax thermonuclear event produced by a white-dwarf merger.

The identification with Pa 30 is strong but remains a scholarly physical reconstruction, not something stated in the medieval records. [Schaefer’s evidence review](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/523/3/3885/7075890)

### Tycho’s supernova, SN 1572

**Documented fact.** The transient now called SN 1572 appeared in Cassiopeia in November 1572. It had already been seen by several observers when Tycho Brahe observed it on 11 November from the Herrevad region in Scania.

Tycho’s *De nova stella* appeared in 1573. His diagram locates the new star relative to Cassiopeia. [Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/De_Nova_Stella); [Library of Congress object](https://www.loc.gov/item/92516378/); [ESO image of the chart](https://supernova.eso.org/exhibition/images/tycho-book-cc/?lang=de)

**Documented scientific significance.** Tycho found no measurable diurnal parallax, placing the object beyond the conventional lunar and planetary spheres under the observational assumptions of the period. A genuinely new star in the supposedly unchanging stellar region damaged Aristotelian celestial incorruptibility.

**Qualification.** “Tycho single-handedly killed the Aristotelian universe” is heroic shorthand. Other European observers, including Thomas Digges, Jerónimo Muñoz, Michael Maestlin, Francesco Maurolico, and Tadeáš Hájek, observed or discussed the nova. The conceptual change was prolonged, and Tycho retained a geocentric geoheliocentric cosmos rather than adopting Copernican heliocentrism.

**Modern fact.** The event was a Type Ia supernova. In Bayer-derived nomenclature it became **B Cassiopeiae**, using a Roman capital for a temporary/new star, not a Bayer Greek letter.

### Cassiopeia A and the unseen supernova

**Documented fact.** Cassiopeia A is a young core-collapse supernova remnant about 11,000 light-years away and one of the brightest extrasolar radio sources. Expansion measurements and light echoes place the explosion’s light-arrival date broadly in the late seventeenth century. Spectroscopy of light echoes identifies it as Type IIb. [Expansion study](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0104188); [Type IIb determination](https://arxiv.org/abs/0805.4557)

**Historical puzzle.** No universally accepted contemporary observation of a brilliant supernova survives. Extinction by interstellar and circumstellar dust probably reduced its visible brightness.

#### Flamsteed’s 3 Cassiopeiae

**Disputed.** On 16 August 1680, John Flamsteed recorded a sixth-magnitude object subsequently catalogued as **3 Cassiopeiae**. No persistent star exists at the printed location. William Ashworth proposed in 1980 that Flamsteed had observed the Cas A supernova.

Evidence in favor:

- The date is compatible with some dynamical estimates.
- No ordinary star remains at the catalogue position.
- A heavily extinguished Cas A could have appeared near sixth magnitude.
- Caroline Herschel had already investigated the anomalous Flamsteed entry.

Evidence against:

- Flamsteed’s position does not coincide precisely with Cas A.
- The entry may be a mistaken observation or reduction of another star.
- Modern expansion dates do not force exactly 1680.
- A supernova remaining near sixth magnitude would normally invite repeated observation, yet none is secure.

The proper conclusion is “possible, not established.” [Chandra’s summary of the evidence](https://chandra.harvard.edu/edu/formal/icecore/summarizing.html); [visual-observability study](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.2345)

### Novae

Important twentieth- and twenty-first-century novae include:

- **Nova Cas 1910**, V628 Cas.
- **Nova Cas 1936**, V630 Cas.
- **Nova Cas 1948**, V465 Cas.
- **Nova Cas 1957**, V723? Historical nova labels require care because later variable-star numbering and nova-year names can be confused.
- **Nova Cas 1993**, V705 Cas.
- **Nova Cas 1995**, **V723 Cas**, an unusually slow nova.
- **Nova Cas 2020**, V1391 Cas.
- **Nova Cas 2021**, **V1405 Cas**, notable for multiple rebrightenings.

**Contested historical reports.** Claims that major new stars appeared in Cassiopeia in 945 and 1264 derive largely from the sixteenth-century astrologer Cyprian Leowitz and later repetition. They lack adequate independent contemporary documentation and are not accepted as securely established novae.

## Meteor showers

**Documented modern nomenclature.** The IAU Meteor Data Center has carried several streams whose radiants lie in Cassiopeia, including:

- **September iota-Cassiopeiids** (SIC, IAU shower 416).
- **October iota-Cassiopeiids** (ICS).
- **psi-Cassiopeiids** (PCA).
- **pi-Cassiopeiids** (PIC).

Not all are “established” showers; some remain on the Working List, and duplicate-stream analysis can merge, rename, or remove candidates. A radiant’s constellation can also change over time through radiant drift. [IAU MDC Working List](https://iaumdc.astro.sk/working); [IAU shower nomenclature group](https://www.iau.org/WG276/WG276/Home.aspx)

The Perseids pass through the same broad northern sky but are named for a radiant in Perseus; listing “Perseids” as Cassiopeia’s own meteor shower is misleading.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the Greek figure

#### Mesopotamia, second to first millennium BCE

**Documented text; disputed stellar identification.** The Babylonian astronomical compendium conventionally called **MUL.APIN** was compiled perhaps around 1000 BCE and survives in first-millennium tablets. Its opening constellation, **MUL.APIN**, means “Plough,” Akkadian *epinnu*.

Older scholarship commonly located the Plough in Triangulum and part of Andromeda. More recent work by Susanne Hoffmann and Wayne Horowitz argues that the bright stars of Cassiopeia fit the textual phenomena better. This is a new reconstruction, not consensus ancient testimony that “Cassiopeia was the Babylonian Plough.” [Summary of the competing identification](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/APIN)

There is no evidence that the Babylonian Plough was a queen, wife of Cepheus, or mother of Andromeda.

#### Egypt

**Disputed reconstruction.** Peter Le Page Renouf and later writers proposed that Cassiopeia corresponded to an Egyptian northern-sky “Leg,” *msḫtyw*-related or otherwise described in funerary texts. Other reconstructions distribute the Egyptian foreleg, bull, hippopotamus, mooring post, and associated figures across Ursa Major, Draco, Cepheus, and neighboring circumpolar stars.

Egyptian astronomical ceilings do not provide coordinates sufficient to identify Cassiopeia uniquely. “Cassiopeia was the Egyptian Leg” is therefore not documented fact.

### Fifth century BCE: the Perseus–Andromeda story

**Scholarly reconstruction from fragments.** Pherecydes of Athens, fifth century BCE, seems to provide the earliest surviving literary prose witness to important parts of Perseus’s rescue of Andromeda. His work survives only in quotations and scholia.

**Documented lost drama.** Sophocles wrote an *Andromeda*, now fragmentary. Euripides’ *Andromeda* was produced in 412 BCE and became enormously influential. Cassiopeia’s precise role cannot be fully recovered, although later mythographers explicitly cite Sophocles and Euripides for her boast. [Study of the lost Andromeda tragedies](https://dokumen.pub/andromedatragdien-3519074702.html)

### Fourth century BCE

**Documented astronomical tradition.** Eudoxus’s lost *Phaenomena* described the classical constellation system later versified by Aratus. Hipparchus’s commentary preserves criticisms of Eudoxus and Aratus and confirms that the seated female figure was already standard.

### c. 276 BCE: Aratus

**Documented fact.** Aratus, *Phaenomena* 179–204, calls Cepheus’s family hapless. Cassiopeia revolves east of Cepheus, faintly marked by alternating stars resembling a double-door’s key or bar. Her arms extend, and she appears to sorrow over her daughter. Aratus later describes her humiliating headlong or diving posture as she turns.

This is the earliest substantially surviving literary description of Cassiopeia as a visible constellation. [Greek and English text](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AratusPhaenomena.html)

### Third–second century BCE: Eratosthenic catasterism

The surviving *Catasterismi* is not the autograph work of Eratosthenes; it exists in later recension and epitome. “Pseudo-Eratosthenes” is therefore often safer.

**Myth, Catasterismi 16.** Cassiopeia claimed to surpass the Nereids in beauty. Poseidon ravaged the country; Andromeda was exposed to the sea monster. Cassiopeia was set among the stars seated on a chair. Her abnormal revolving posture memorializes rather than erases her fault.

The chapter cites the tragic tradition, particularly Sophocles. The adjacent chapters on Cepheus and Andromeda make the constellation a family catasterism rather than an isolated portrait.

**Variant in the Epitome.** The abbreviated recension preserves the essential boast, punishment, chair, and stellar placement but compresses causal and star-count material. Differences between full recension and Epitome should not be inflated into wholly separate myths.

### First century BCE–first century CE: Roman poetic astronomy

#### Cicero and Germanicus

Cicero’s fragmentary Latin *Aratea* and Germanicus’s more complete version transmitted Aratus’s seated, unfortunate woman to Roman readers. Germanicus elaborates her ungainly rotation and grief. Later scholia accumulate the Nereid boast and Andromeda narrative around the verse.

#### Manilius

**Documented but limited.** Manilius’s *Astronomica* treats circumpolar constellations and rising/setting relationships, but Cassiopeia receives no independent extended myth comparable to the zodiacal signs. Later astrological summaries sometimes attribute elaborate “Cassiopeian character” doctrine to Manilius without a matching passage.

### c. 8 CE: Ovid

**Myth, *Metamorphoses* 4.663–764 and 5.1–235.** Ovid’s narrative begins after Cassiopeia’s boast has brought punishment. Andromeda pays for her mother’s tongue; Perseus sees her chained, bargains with Cepheus and Cassiopeia for her hand, kills the sea monster, and later fights Phineus and his supporters at the wedding.

Ovid does not supply the fullest surviving catasterism of Cassiopeia. Modern summaries that cite “Ovid *Met.* 4” specifically for her being chained to a chair or placed upside down conflate Ovid’s Andromeda narrative with Eratosthenic and Hyginian constellation lore.

### Hyginus, probably late first century BCE to second century CE

Authorship and dating of both *Fabulae* and *Astronomica* are difficult; the works transmitted under “Hyginus” need not both be by Augustus’s librarian Gaius Julius Hyginus.

#### *Astronomica* 2.10

**Myth.** Cassiopeia boasted that she surpassed the Nereids in beauty. The Nereids demanded punishment from Neptune/Poseidon. Because of her impiety she is represented seated; as the sphere turns, she is carried head-down in a humiliating posture. Hyginus cites Sophocles, Euripides, and other writers. [Latin text of *Astronomica* II](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/hyginus/hyginus2.shtml)

#### *Fabulae* 64

**Important variant.** Here Cassiopeia did not say that she herself was more beautiful. She preferred **her daughter Andromeda’s beauty** to that of the Nereids: *Cassiope filiae suae Andromedae formam Nereidibus anteposuit*. Neptune demanded that Andromeda be exposed to the monster. [Latin text](https://www.lateinlex.de/?call=Puc&permalink=Hyg_fab_64)

The same chapter names Andromeda’s prior fiancé as **Agenor**, whereas other traditions call him **Phineus**. Cepheus and Agenor plan to kill Perseus; Perseus turns them to stone with the Gorgon head. This is not the same ending as every other version.

### Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Library* 2.4.3

**Myth.** In the standard transmitted account, Cassiopeia contended with the Nereids over beauty. Poseidon sent a flood and sea monster. The oracle of Ammon required Andromeda’s sacrifice. Perseus killed the monster and claimed Andromeda despite her betrothal to Phineus. A plot against Perseus ended when the Gorgon head petrified Phineus and his allies. [Greek/English text and notes](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DLibrary%3Abook%3D2%3Achapter%3D4)

### Ptolemy, c. 150 CE

**Documented astronomical fact.** In the *Almagest*, Cassiopeia is one of the 48 standard constellations and has thirteen catalogue stars. Ptolemy records stellar locations, not a myth. His catalogue stabilized the figure’s astronomical identity for Greek, Islamic, and Latin astronomy.

### Pausanias, second century CE

**Documented local traditions.**

- *Description of Greece* 4.35.9 associates Joppa with the story: seawater’s coloration and local remains were shown as evidence of Andromeda’s monster.
- 2.21.5 and related Perseus material concern the Gorgon episode that precedes the rescue.
- Pausanias reports places and artworks rather than a separate Cassiopeia catasterism.

This geographical localization in Joppa, modern Jaffa, helped later writers treat “Ethiopia” as a Levantine kingdom. Greek *Aithiopia* could denote distant sun-darkened lands and was not restricted to the modern nation-state Ethiopia.

### Nonnus, fifth century CE

**Documented reception.** In the *Dionysiaca*, Nonnus repeatedly uses constellation and Perseid-family imagery, including the Ethiopian princess and sea-monster complex. He is evidence for late-antique poetic afterlife, not the earliest source of Cassiopeia’s boast. Claims that Nonnus invented the upside-down punishment are false; it is much earlier.

### Tenth century: al-Ṣūfī

**Documented fact.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī completed the *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita*, “Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars,” around 964. He reconciled Ptolemy’s figures with his own observations and indigenous Arabic star lore. Cassiopeia is *Dhāt al-Kursī*.

Each constellation was normally drawn twice:

- as seen on the sky;
- reversed, as on a celestial globe.

This dual convention is critical when interpreting the queen’s handedness. [Survey of al-Ṣūfī manuscripts](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_manuscripts.htm)

### Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries

Latin translations and derivatives of al-Ṣūfī circulated alongside illustrated Aratea and Hyginus manuscripts. Dürer’s 1515 printed hemisphere gave the classical figures unprecedented reproducibility. Cassiopeia appears enthroned in the northern map.

### 1572–73

The new star was recorded in Cassiopeia; Tycho published *De nova stella*. It became one of the decisive observational events of early modern astronomy.

### 1603: Bayer

**Documented fact.** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* gave Cassiopeia a large engraved figure and systematic letter designations. The W stars acquired the Bayer identifiers still used today.

### 1627: Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, produced with Johann Bayer’s collaboration and engraved by Lucas Kilian, replaced pagan northern figures with New Testament persons and objects.

**Documented fact.** Cassiopeia became **Mary Magdalene**. [Library of Congress scan](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/) She retained the underlying star pattern, demonstrating that star cataloguing and iconographic identity had become separable.

### 1680 and after

Flamsteed recorded the disputed 3 Cas. Cas A’s actual supernova probably occurred in the late seventeenth century, whether or not Flamsteed saw it.

### 1690: Hevelius

Hevelius preserved Cassiopeia in *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*. His atlas’s globe-view orientation reversed the image relative to direct sky maps.

### 1729: Flamsteed atlas

Cassiopeia remained canonical. Flamsteed’s catalogue numbering supplied many of the modern numerical designations.

### 1782 and 1801: Bode

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* served a broad German audience in 1782. His 1801 *Uranographia* was the culmination of the great engraved figural atlas tradition. Cassiopeia appears on plate IV with both ancient and modern neighboring inventions, including Honores Friderici.

### 1822–24: Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* of 1822 rendered Cassiopeia with freer artistic styling. The anonymous 1824 constellation cards called *Urania’s Mirror* copied Jamieson closely and perforated the principal stars so that they shone when held before a light. Cassiopeia appears on card 3. [History of *Urania’s Mirror*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urania%27s_Mirror)

### Nineteenth century: Burritt, Argelander, Heis

Elijah Burritt’s popular American geography-of-the-heavens publications transmitted the enthroned queen to classrooms. Argelander and Heis progressively favored stellar accuracy, magnitudes, and observing utility over luxuriant mythological engraving.

### 1922–1930

The IAU kept Cassiopeia, standardized **Cas**, and gave it Delporte’s modern territory.

## Classical myths and their variants

### The boast

**Myth; earliest recoverable classical form fifth century BCE, fully surviving in Hellenistic and Roman mythography.** Cassiopeia boasted that either she or her daughter surpassed the Nereids in beauty.

The variants must be retained:

- Cassiopeia herself surpasses the Nereids: Eratosthenic *Catasterismi* 16; Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.10; Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.3.
- Andromeda surpasses the Nereids: Hyginus, *Fabulae* 64.
- Some later summaries say “Cassiopeia and Andromeda were fairer,” harmonizing rather than preserving the disagreement.
- Later retellings often say “more beautiful than all the sea nymphs” or “than Hera.” The Hera version is not the standard ancient Cassiopeia story and often results from contamination with other beauty contests.

### The offended Nereids

**Myth.** The Nereids appeal to Poseidon/Neptune. Some versions single out no individual; modern retellings sometimes name Amphitrite as chief complainant. Amphitrite’s involvement is plausible as Poseidon’s consort but should not be inserted into every ancient source.

Aratus names Doris and Panope in poetic reference to Cassiopeia’s rivalry. This gives two Nereid names but does not prove a canonical list of offended sisters.

### The flood and monster

**Myth.** Poseidon sends either:

- a sea monster, *kētos*;
- a flood and a monster;
- or generalized devastation of the Ethiopian coast.

The oracle of Ammon declares that Andromeda must be exposed. The oracle appears clearly in Pseudo-Apollodorus and related mythographic tradition. It is not explicit in every account.

### Andromeda’s exposure

**Myth.** Cepheus and Cassiopeia surrender their daughter to save the country. Ancient sources generally place parental consent under communal and divine compulsion. Modern psychological retellings that make Cassiopeia individually chain Andromeda out of malice go beyond the texts.

### Perseus’s bargain

**Myth.** Returning with Medusa’s head, Perseus sees Andromeda and offers to rescue her in return for marriage. Cepheus and Cassiopeia agree. Ovid emphasizes visual love, bargaining, and the spectacle of combat.

### The rival fiancé

**Myth with named variants.**

- **Phineus** in Pseudo-Apollodorus and Ovid.
- **Agenor** in Hyginus, *Fabulae* 64.

The rival interrupts or plots against Perseus; the Gorgon head turns him and his party to stone. Hyginus’s short version implicates Cepheus more directly than Ovid’s expansive wedding battle.

### Who killed the monster?

**Mythic variants.** Perseus usually kills it with a sword, sickle, or repeated aerial attacks while using winged sandals. Some later artists and texts make him ride Pegasus. This is a post-classical conflation: the earliest Perseus-Andromeda narrative does not require Pegasus, whose principal hero is Bellerophon.

Some popular retellings say Perseus simply showed Medusa’s head to the monster. That motif is ancient-compatible but not uniform; Ovid’s detailed combat has Perseus physically fight it.

### Cassiopeia’s catasterism

**Myth.** Cassiopeia is placed among the stars in her chair. Ancient accounts do not consistently identify a single installer:

- “the gods” or divine order is often implicit;
- Athena is sometimes credited in later mythographic summaries because she is associated with Perseus and constellation placement;
- Poseidon is sometimes said to have placed her there as punishment;
- later popular accounts say Zeus placed the whole family together.

No single attribution should be declared universal.

### The upside-down punishment

**Myth, securely ancient.** As the circumpolar sphere turns, Cassiopeia periodically appears inverted, head downward, sometimes compared to a diver. Eratosthenic and Hyginian sources interpret this shameful posture as punishment for her boast. The chair is thus both royal attribute and instrument of humiliation.

**Later embellishment.** She is frequently said to be chained, tied, or strapped to the chair so that she cannot fall. The ancient visual and literary evidence does show constraint or awkward attachment in some traditions, but a detailed eternal “seat-belt punishment” is largely post-antique elaboration. It should not be quoted as if it were verbatim Eratosthenes.

### Her relationship to Cepheus

**Myth.** Cassiopeia is wife of Cepheus, king of the Ethiopians, and mother of Andromeda. The sky groups Cassiopeia, Cepheus, Andromeda, Perseus, Pegasus, and Cetus into what modern handbooks call the “Perseus family.” That family classification is modern astronomical pedagogy, not an ancient formal constellation family.

### Ethiopia and racial/geographical reception

**Documented ancient terminology; disputed modern localization.** Ancient *Aithiopes* means “burnt-faced” in Greek folk etymology and was applied to peoples at the world’s sunward margins. The myth was localized both in Africa and at Joppa in the Levant. Renaissance European artists often portrayed Cassiopeia and Andromeda as white despite the Ethiopian genealogy; other artworks portray them as Black. Neither artistic convention exhausts the geographical ambiguity of ancient sources.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Arabic and Bedouin sky lore

The Ptolemaic **Lady in the Chair** and indigenous **Henna-Dyed Hand** coexisted. The hand belonged to a much larger Pleiades-centered figure stretching through Taurus, Perseus, Andromeda, and Cassiopeia. This is strong evidence against treating Greek constellation borders as universal.

A Bedouin she-camel also occupied parts of the region. β Cas’s camel-hump name preserves this overlay. Al-Ṣūfī is especially valuable because he distinguishes inherited Greek astronomical descriptions from Arab star names rather than silently merging them.

### Chinese sky

Cassiopeia’s modern territory crosses several traditional Chinese celestial divisions, chiefly the **Purple Forbidden Enclosure**, the **Black Tortoise of the North**, and the **White Tiger of the West**. These are not Chinese translations of Cassiopeia.

Important asterisms include:

- **王良 Wáng Liáng**, the celebrated charioteer Wang Liang. β Cas was associated with Wang Liang himself in common reconstructions, while neighboring stars represented parts of his chariot team.
- **策 Cè**, the whip, commonly associated with κ Cas.
- **閣道 Gédào**, the Flying/Lofty Passage, a celestial road connecting palace regions; its stars extend through Cassiopeia.
- **附路 Fùlù**, Auxiliary Road, associated with ζ Cas.
- **天駟 Tiānsì**, Celestial Four-Horse Chariot, source of the modern proper name Tiansi for γ Cas.
- **華蓋 Huágài**, the Canopy, in the Cassiopeia–Cepheus region.
- court offices and ministers in the broader Purple Enclosure.

**Scholarly caution.** Exact star-to-asterism mappings vary among the Han, Tang, Song, Korean, and later charts and among modern reconstructions. “Cassiopeia equals Wang Liang” is only a rough overlap. See Sun Xiaochun and Jacob Kistemaker’s bibliographical record, [*The Chinese Sky during the Han*](https://www.persee.fr/doc/befeo_0336-1519_1997_num_84_1_2533).

### Indian traditions

**Modern documented educational tradition.** In modern Indian popular astronomy the W is often called **Sharmishtha**, the daughter of the Daitya king Vṛṣaparvan and companion/rival of Devayānī. School materials use Sharmishtha and Saptarshi as opposing pointers to Dhruva, the pole star. [Maharashtra educational text](https://books.ebalbharati.in/pdfs/703020012.pdf)

**Unverified antiquity.** This does not demonstrate that the Cassiopeia W bore Sharmishtha’s name in Vedic or classical siddhāntic astronomy. The mapping may be modern vernacular pedagogy based on the Greek constellation. Cassiopeia does not form one of the canonical 27 lunar *nakṣatras*, which lie along the Moon’s ecliptic path.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

**Absence of specific evidence.** Jewish astrology has extensive planetary, zodiacal, calendrical, and letter correspondences, but no stable classical Kabbalistic mapping of Cassiopeia to a sefirah, biblical queen, or Hebrew letter was found.

Late Christian “Hebrew star-name” writers sometimes claim:

- *Caph* is Hebrew *kaph*, “palm,” and therefore a victory branch;
- *Schedar* means “the freed” or “the liberated”;
- the enthroned woman prefigures the Church or a redeemed bride.

These are not sound historical etymologies. Caph’s route is Arabic astronomical tradition, even though Arabic *kaff* and Hebrew *kaf* are cognate Semitic words.

### Christianized sky

**Documented fact.** Julius Schiller replaced Cassiopeia with **Mary Magdalene** in 1627. Cepheus became St Stephen; Andromeda became Christ’s Sepulchre in the same program. Schiller’s system failed to displace the classical names but is an important Counter-Reformation experiment. [ETH presentation](https://astrorara.library.ethz.ch/en/coelum-stellatum)

Later biblical constellationalism, particularly Frances Rolleston’s *Mazzaroth* and E. W. Bullinger’s *Witness of the Stars*, interpreted Cassiopeia as the enthroned, redeemed woman or Church. These are nineteenth-century theological constructions presented as recoveries of primordial revelation; they are not supported by ancient Hebrew star catalogues. [Bullinger text](https://ftp.gwdg.de/pub/misc/gutenberg/4/9/0/1/49018/49018-pdf.pdf)

### Astrology

**Documented classical doctrine.** Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* I.9, assigns planetary “temperaments” to non-zodiacal constellation stars. The stars of Cassiopeia are traditionally summarized as having the nature of **Saturn and Venus**. This is qualitative ancient astrology, not modern astrophysics.

Later astrological authors associate Cassiopeia with vanity, pride, boastfulness, dignity, punishment, and the hazards of elevation. These meanings are deductions from the myth and vary widely.

**Behenian stars.** None of the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars is securely a principal Cassiopeia star. Lists making Caph, Schedar, or γ Cas Behenian generally expand or alter the medieval canon.

**Lunar mansions.** Because Cassiopeia is far from the ecliptic, it is not itself one of the Indian *nakṣatras* or the standard Arabic 28 lunar stations. Claims that the Moon “passes through Cassiopeia’s mansion” confuse circumpolar asterisms with lunar mansions. Chinese asterisms in the region could be administratively associated with wider mansion zones, but the Moon does not traverse the W.

### Alchemy and Hermeticism

**Absence of ancient constellation-specific evidence.** No standard ancient alchemical operation or classical Hermetic deity corresponds uniquely to Cassiopeia.

Renaissance and modern occult works sometimes interpret the W as:

- alternation of ascent and descent;
- the enthroned Sophia;
- the alchemical queen;
- a celestial M/W cipher;
- the “woman clothed with the sun” of Revelation.

These are later symbolic appropriations. They must not be retrojected into Ptolemy, al-Ṣūfī, or Egyptian temple astronomy.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas, Roman, second century CE, Naples

**Documented artifact.** The marble Atlas now in the Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli carries a celestial globe with classical constellations. It is the oldest surviving large-scale sculptural celestial globe. Cassiopeia appears as a seated woman.

**Disputed inference.** The globe has been proposed as a copy of a Hipparchan globe, even used to reconstruct Hipparchus’s lost catalogue. The sculpture’s constellation positions and manufacturing history do not establish that attribution beyond dispute. [Farnese Atlas overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farnese_Atlas)

### Carolingian and medieval Aratea manuscripts, ninth century onward

Cassiopeia appears seated, enthroned, veiled, or wearing a pointed/Phrygian cap. The Warburg Institute records examples including:

- BnF lat. 12957, fol. 67r, ninth century;
- BnF NAL 1614, fol. 86v, dated 809;
- Germanicus manuscripts around 816 and 820;
- Cicero *Aratea* manuscript around 830;
- Vatican Latin 645 around 845.

[Warburg Cassiopeia iconographic index](https://iconographic.warburg.sas.ac.uk/category/vpc-taxonomy-017048)

The variation shows that manuscript painters inherited a constellation schema, not a single fixed portrait.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

The oldest surviving witnesses usually show Cassiopeia twice, sky-view and globe-view. Gold or ink star marks are placed on her body and chair.

Important examples include:

- Bodleian Library, Marsh 144, copied 1009–10 and associated with al-Ṣūfī’s son;
- Doha Museum of Islamic Art manuscript copied in Baghdad in 1125, though some folios including Cassiopeia are later replacements;
- later Persian manuscripts with sumptuous seated figures;
- fourteenth-century Latin *Sufi Latinus* derivatives, such as Munich Clm 826, fol. 38v. [Warburg record](https://iconographic.warburg.sas.ac.uk/object-wpc-wid-aiis)

The manuscript record warns against reproducing an attractive folio as “al-Ṣūfī’s original drawing” without dating that particular copy.

### Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

Albrecht Dürer’s paired printed celestial hemispheres, prepared with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, show Cassiopeia on the northern hemisphere. The woodcut’s globe-view arrangement and humanist authorities helped standardize Renaissance celestial iconography.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

In the *Uranometria*, Cassiopeia is a richly dressed queen on a throne. Bayer’s plate joined precise Tycho-derived stars to allegorical engraving. The letters that became α, β, γ, δ, ε Cas are printed on the figure.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

Mary Magdalene replaces the pagan queen. The atlas was engraved by Lucas Kilian after designs involving Johann Matthias Kager, with stellar work connected to Bayer and Kaspar Schecks. [Full Library of Congress scan](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/)

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Cassiopeia appears in *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*, engraved in the mirrored globe convention. Her chair and costume are baroque; the star field is far denser than Ptolemy’s thirteen.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Plate IV of *Uranographia* shows an enthroned female figure with crown, drapery, chair, and anatomical star placements. It plots a dense field, nebulae, coordinate grids, and adjacent ancient and newly proposed figures. Plate IV measures roughly 59 by 78 cm in the Rumsey copy. [High-resolution catalogue record](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~283909~90056544%3AIV--Cassiopeia----Coelum-Stellatum-)

The pose is not simply “a W with a woman drawn over it.” Bode participates in the long Ptolemaic tradition of assigning stars to head, breast, arm, knee, foot, throne, and nearby space.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson gave the queen greater artistic freedom than the relatively standardized Flamsteed/Fortin/Bode line. His image became the immediate model for *Urania’s Mirror*.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824

Card 3 shows Cassiopeia. Perforations correspond to stars and allow backlighting. The set was advertised as designed by “a lady”; twentieth-century research associated it with the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam, making the advertised authorship part of the work’s unresolved or deliberately masked publication history.

### Coins, buildings, ceilings, and portals

**Documented but diffuse.** Perseus and Andromeda occur extensively on Greek and Roman ceramics, mosaics, sarcophagi, coins, and wall painting; Cassiopeia appears less consistently and can be difficult to distinguish from a generic enthroned queen.

No ancient coin type securely and universally identifiable as the constellation Cassiopeia was established in this research. Claims based solely on a seated queen and five dots require numismatic provenance and legend analysis.

Astronomical ceilings in medieval and early-modern Europe sometimes include Cassiopeia within complete constellation cycles. Individual portal identifications are often speculative unless inscriptions or a complete zodiac/constellation program survives.

## People, events, and works

### Navigation

**Documented practice.** Cassiopeia is circumpolar at northern mid-latitudes and lies opposite the Big Dipper across Polaris. Its W helps find the north celestial pole when Ursa Major is obscured or low. Modern Indian school astronomy similarly pairs Sharmishtha with Saptarshi.

**Qualification.** Assertions that a named ancient explorer “steered by Cassiopeia” require a voyage journal or navigational manual. Mariners certainly used circumpolar stars, but Polaris, the Guards of Ursa Minor, and Ursa Major are more directly documented navigational references. No secure ancient instruction “navigate by the W of Cassiopeia” was found.

### Observers and discoveries

- **Ptolemy**, c. 150: thirteen catalogue stars.
- **Al-Ṣūfī**, c. 964: revised magnitudes and incorporated Arab lore.
- **Tycho Brahe**, 1572–73: precise work on SN 1572.
- **John Flamsteed**, 1680 and later: 3 Cas controversy and numbered stars.
- **Charles Messier**, 1774: M52.
- **Pierre Méchain**, 1781: M103.
- **Caroline Herschel**, 1783: NGC 7789; she also investigated Flamsteed’s anomalous 3 Cas.
- **William Herschel**: catalogued numerous clusters and nebulae in the region.
- **William and Caroline Herschel’s nineteenth-century successors**, Dreyer especially: NGC consolidation.
- **Karl Jansky, Grote Reber, Martin Ryle, Graham Smith, and other radio astronomers**: Cassiopeia A became central to radio astronomy and calibration.
- **Walter Baade and Fritz Zwicky’s supernova framework**, followed by twentieth-century spectroscopy: enabled reclassification of Tycho’s “nova” as a supernova.
- Modern X-ray and infrared missions—Chandra, Spitzer, NuSTAR, Hubble, and JWST—mapped Cas A’s ejecta, compact remnant, radioactive products, dust, and light echoes.

### Literature

Cassiopeia and the Andromeda episode occur or reverberate in:

- Sophocles’ lost *Andromeda*.
- Euripides’ *Andromeda* of 412 BCE.
- Aristophanes’ parody in *Women at the Thesmophoria*.
- Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4–5.
- Manilius, *Astronomica*.
- Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*.
- Medieval mythographic and Aratea traditions.
- Pierre Corneille’s *Andromède* (1650), with elaborate celestial machinery.
- Calderón’s *Andrómeda y Perseo*.
- William Morris’s *The Earthly Paradise* treatment of Perseus and Andromeda.
- W. B. Yeats’s “His Phoenix,” which invokes Cassiopeia in a constellation-rich poetic vocabulary.
- Modern novels, poems, and science fiction use Cassiopeia as a sign of hauteur, northern orientation, remoteness, or an alien destination.

An exhaustive bibliography of every modern work entitled *Cassiopeia* would be virtually unbounded; the name is common in poetry, fantasy, romance, comics, and electronic media.

### Painting and sculpture

The rescue of Andromeda, often with Cassiopeia in the court or background, was treated by:

- Titian, *Perseus and Andromeda*, 1554–56.
- Giorgio Vasari and collaborators in Medicean Perseus cycles.
- Joachim Wtewael.
- Peter Paul Rubens.
- Pierre Mignard.
- Edward Burne-Jones’s *Perseus* cycle.
- Frederic Leighton.

Cassiopeia alone is less often the focal subject than Andromeda. Catalogues sometimes label any crowned woman in an Andromeda cycle “Cassiopeia” without adequate iconographic evidence.

### Music

Works and projects titled *Cassiopeia/Casiopea* include:

- eighteenth- and nineteenth-century operatic or theatrical Andromeda traditions in which the queen appears;
- the Japanese jazz-fusion band **Casiopea**, founded in 1976—the single-s spelling reflects its chosen romanized brand;
- “Cassiopeia” songs by numerous contemporary artists, including Sara Bareilles and others;
- constellation-themed orchestral and electronic works.

These are modern receptions of the name, not continuations of an ancient musical cult.

### Ships, aircraft, and missions

The name has been applied to ships, yachts, fictional spacecraft, software projects, and astronomical instruments. Particularly prominent:

- **Cassiopeia A** as an observational target for Chandra, Hubble, Spitzer, NuSTAR, IXPE, and JWST.
- **Cassini** is unrelated etymologically despite occasional confusion.
- ESA’s **Cassiopeia** references in mission planning or payload/project acronyms must be distinguished from independent commercial and fictional names.
- The constellation supplied navigation-star context for northern aviation and spaceflight; γ Cas’s unofficial “Navi” arose directly from Apollo-era astronaut usage.

### Popular culture

Pokémon *Scarlet* and *Violet* use “Cassiopeia” as an identity and name Team Star’s squads after Schedar, Caph, Ruchbah, Segin, and Navi. This is a deliberate modern constellation reference, although Navi is not an official stellar proper name.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Cassiopeia’s etymology

**Disputed/unresolved.** No accepted Greek etymology exists. Confident translations such as “she whose words excel,” “cassia juice,” or “beautiful voice” lack a demonstrable ancient linguistic chain.

### 2. Who made the boast?

**Ancient variant, not an error.** Most catasterismic accounts say Cassiopeia boasted about herself; *Fabulae* 64 says she placed Andromeda above the Nereids. Harmonizing the two erases genuine textual variation.

### 3. Was Cassiopeia chained to the chair?

**Partly traditional, textually variable.** The chair and humiliating inversion are ancient. Elaborate binding, permanent chains, or a promise that she can never fall are stronger in later retellings and images.

### 4. Who put her in the sky?

**No uniform ancient answer.** The gods, Zeus, Poseidon, or Athena appear in different summaries. The extant core texts emphasize the reason and posture more consistently than a named catasterizing deity.

### 5. Is the W ancient?

**The stars are ancient; the letter-name is not.** Aratus recognized a comparable zigzag but described a door-key or bar. “W” and “M” are post-Latin alphabet pedagogical descriptions.

### 6. The Babylonian Plough

**Active scholarly dispute.** Triangulum-plus-Andromeda was long favored; a Cassiopeia identification has recently been argued. The relevant cuneiform text names and describes MUL.APIN but does not supply a modern chart.

### 7. The Egyptian Leg

**Disputed reconstruction.** Renouf’s identification entered popular star-lore books and is frequently repeated as fact. Egyptian northern-sky figures cannot be mapped that precisely from the available evidence.

### 8. “The Tinted Hand”

**Documented tradition, often misrepresented.** It is genuinely Arabic/Bedouin, but it is not simply an Arabic nickname for the Greek queen. It belonged to a different large-scale stellar anatomy. Its membership was probably α, β, γ, δ, ζ Cas under medieval magnitude evidence, not necessarily today’s five-star W.

### 9. Caph’s meaning

**Documented Arabic origin; false Hebrew exclusivity.** Arabic *kaff*, “palm,” is secure. Hebrew *kaf* is cognate, but nineteenth-century claims that the name was originally Hebrew and meant Cassiopeia’s “branch of victory” are confessional reinterpretations.

### 10. Segin

**Likely catalogue corruption.** The name probably migrated from Seginus in Boötes. “Ancient Arabic for Cassiopeia’s foot” is not established.

### 11. Achird

**Modern and unexplained.** It is apparently unattested before the 1950 Skalnaté Pleso atlas. Claims of deep Arabic, Hebrew, or Persian antiquity remain unverified.

### 12. Navi

**Modern invention.** It is “Ivan” reversed, honoring Virgil Ivan Grissom. It is informal, not an ancient Arabic name and not the IAU official name of γ Cas.

### 13. Schedar or γ Cas as brightest

Both statements may appear in historical sources because γ Cas varies and ancient magnitude classes were coarse. α Cas is normally catalogued as the brightest stable member, but γ Cas has at times been brighter. Bayer letters do not settle the question.

### 14. Tycho “discovered” the 1572 star

**Oversimplification.** Tycho made the most influential observations and publication but was not necessarily its first human observer. European and East Asian records and other named observers matter.

### 15. “Death of the Aristotelian sky”

**Historically meaningful shorthand, not an instantaneous revolution.** The absence of parallax placed the nova in the celestial region and contradicted incorruptibility. Aristotelian cosmology nevertheless persisted in modified forms, and Tycho himself did not adopt modern heliocentrism.

### 16. Flamsteed saw Cassiopeia A

**Disputed.** 3 Cas may be Cas A, a positional error involving another star, or an observational/reduction mistake. No decisive document resolves it.

### 17. Cas A exploded “in 1680”

**Approximation.** Expansion ages depend on deceleration models; light-echo and ejecta analyses support the late seventeenth century but not an exact universally agreed calendar date.

### 18. 3C 58 is SN 1181

**Formerly favored, now challenged.** Modern age and positional work makes Pa 30 the stronger candidate. “SN 1181 = 3C 58” persists in older books and websites.

### 19. Guest stars of 945 and 1264

**Unverified.** They rest on late and unreliable transmission, particularly Leowitz, rather than adequate contemporary observation.

### 20. Korean 1592–93 guest star as a Cas A precursor

**Speculative.** A research hypothesis proposes it as an “impostor” eruption from the Cas A progenitor. This is not established and must not be presented as Cas A’s observed supernova. [Study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03576)

### 21. Pegasus in the rescue

**Later contamination.** Perseus commonly flies with winged sandals in ancient literature. Renaissance and modern art frequently supplies Pegasus because both belong to the Perseus constellation cycle.

### 22. Cassiopeia as Sharmishtha in antiquity

**Unverified.** The mapping is well documented in modern Indian education, but evidence for an ancient Sanskrit constellation identity was not located.

### 23. Cassiopeia in Kabbalah and alchemy

**Mostly modern invention.** General Jewish, Hermetic, and alchemical astrology does not prove a stable Cassiopeia correspondence. Explicit constellation-specific schemes require dating to the actual author who proposed them.

### 24. Bode’s “830 stars in 109 groups”

**Unverified as a Bode statement.** Bode’s catalogue advertises 17,240 objects and his atlas more than 17,000 stars. The supplied 830/109 formula appears to belong to a separate collection-level taxonomy unless further provenance is produced.

## Open questions

1. Which modern stars most convincingly correspond to each of Ptolemy’s thirteen entries when manuscript variants and proper motion are modeled together?
2. Did the full, lost Eratosthenic work name the deity responsible for Cassiopeia’s placement more explicitly than the surviving recension?
3. How much of Hyginus’s Cassiopeia chapter derives directly from Sophocles rather than an intermediate mythographic handbook?
4. Can additional papyri recover Cassiopeia’s role in Sophocles’ or Euripides’ *Andromeda*?
5. Does MUL.APIN’s Plough best fit Cassiopeia, Triangulum–Andromeda, or a larger configuration?
6. Can Egyptian circumpolar figures ever be mapped at individual-star resolution without circular reasoning?
7. Which five stars did different Bedouin authorities include in *al-Kaff al-Khaḍīb* at different dates?
8. What is the manuscript route by which Segin migrated from Boötes to ε Cas?
9. Is a pre-1950 witness to “Achird” hidden in an uncatalogued atlas or manuscript?
10. Was Flamsteed’s 3 Cas Cas A, a misreduced known star, or an otherwise lost transient?
11. Will future expansion, nucleosynthesis, and central-star modeling make the Pa 30–SN 1181 identification conclusive?
12. What precise “Bode collection on chain” record supplies the figures 830 stars and 109 groups?
