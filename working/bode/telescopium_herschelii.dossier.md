# Telescopium Herschelii: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Status: abolished / not adopted by the IAU.** Telescopium Herschelii is not one of the 88 constellations standardized at the International Astronomical Union’s first General Assembly in Rome in May 1922. It consequently has no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, boundary, area, or constellation code. The frequently encountered abbreviation **“TeH”** and genitive **“Telescopii Herschelii”** are modern, unofficial constructions.

The 1922 decision established the recognized constellation list; it did not pass a separate resolution individually “abolishing” Telescopium Herschelii. Eugène Delporte’s boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930. Thus:

- **1922:** excluded from the official list and thereby rendered obsolete for standardized astronomical nomenclature.
- **1928:** the replacement boundaries were approved.
- **1930:** Delporte’s boundary tables and atlas assigned every point in its former territory to official constellations, chiefly **Auriga**, with outlying portions in **Lynx** and, depending on how broadly Hell’s or later atlas figures are interpreted, **Gemini**. [IAU history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [Delporte, 1930](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

### Names and etymology

| Form | Language/date | Meaning and status |
|---|---|---|
| **Tubus Herschelii Maior** | Hell, Latin, 1789 | “Herschel’s greater tube/telescope.” *Tubus* is a tube, hence by metonymy a telescope; *Herschelii* is the Latin genitive of Herschel; *maior* means greater or larger. Hell’s spelling varies typographically between *Herschelii*, *Hershelii*, and later transcriptions such as *Hershelli*. |
| **Tubus Herschelii Minor** | Hell, Latin, 1789 | “Herschel’s lesser telescope.” A separate proposed constellation near Taurus and Orion, not simply a second name for the northern figure. |
| **Telescopium Herschelii** | Bode, Latin, 1801 | “Herschel’s telescope.” Bode retained the territory of Hell’s greater telescope, discarded the smaller constellation, changed *Tubus* to the Neo-Latin/learned Greek-derived *Telescopium*, and redrew the instrument. |
| **Herschels Teleskop** | German, Bode and later German works | “Herschel’s telescope.” No apostrophe is required in the traditional German form. |
| **Herschel’s Telescope** | English, nineteenth century | Direct vernacular translation. Spellings include *Herschell’s Telescope*. |
| **Le Télescope de Herschel** | French | Direct translation; used in descriptions of Jamieson-derived and *Urania’s Mirror* imagery. |
| **El Telescopio de Herschel** | Spanish | Direct modern translation. |
| **Il Telescopio di Herschel** | Italian | Direct modern translation. |
| **Telescopium Herschilii / Hersehelii** | nineteenth-century printed variants | Misspellings or typographical variants, not independent Latin names. *Urania’s Mirror* is particularly associated with *Herschilii*. |

**Documented absence:** there is no Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, or Sanskrit name for “Herschel’s Telescope.” The figure is an eighteenth-century European invention, centuries later than the relevant ancient star traditions. Any ancient name encountered in these stars belongs to an older constellation or asterism, not to Telescopium Herschelii.

### Position and figure

In Bode’s 1801 atlas the telescope occupies the faint eastern or southeastern reaches of historical Auriga, between the classical Charioteer and Lynx and above Gemini. It is a long, narrow instrument mounted on a stand, with its axis extending through the chain of faint stars now carrying ψ and Flamsteed designations in Auriga.

The figure does **not** represent a natural grouping recognized as a unified constellation before the 1780s. It was made by separating faint stars that earlier European catalogues treated as part of Auriga or as unformed stars around Auriga, Gemini, and Lynx.

The statement that all its stars went only to Auriga and Lynx is therefore a useful shorthand but too absolute: Bode’s assigned **o**, generally identified with ο Geminorum, is now in Gemini. Conversely, most of the recognizable tube and mounting are unquestionably in modern Auriga.

## Stars and objects

### Brightest star

**Documented identification:** the brightest star assigned an unambiguous Bode letter in the figure is normally given as **ψ² Aurigae = 50 Aurigae**, visual magnitude about 4.8. It is an orange K-type giant and carried Bode’s letter **a**.

It has no internationally approved traditional proper name. Modern astrological pages sometimes call it “Dolones III,” but that is an artificial numbering of an ancient collective asterism, not a historically documented individual proper name.

A contrary secondary statement calls θ Geminorum, magnitude approximately 3.6, the brightest star “in” Telescopium Herschelii. That results from treating a broad atlas footprint or surrounding field as the constellation. θ Gem was not one of the securely reconstructed lettered members of Bode’s telescope. For the constellation’s assigned star list, ψ² Aur is the defensible answer.

### The ψ Aurigae sequence

Modern ψ Aurigae is shared by a large series of stars:

- ψ¹ Aur = 46 Aur
- ψ² Aur = 50 Aur
- ψ³ Aur = 52 Aur
- ψ⁴ Aur = 55 Aur
- ψ⁵ Aur = 56 Aur
- ψ⁶ Aur = 57 Aur
- ψ⁷ Aur = 58 Aur
- ψ⁸ Aur = 61 Aur
- ψ⁹ Aur = HR 2568
- ψ¹⁰ Aur = 16 Lyncis

The sequence is an especially visible residue of nineteenth-century reshuffling. Bode had removed several of these stars from Auriga and lettered them within his telescope; later cataloguers restored them to Auriga, while the modern boundary put ψ¹⁰ in Lynx. Numbering discrepancies in online tables often arise from confusing Bode’s temporary letters with the restored ψ sequence.

### Proper name Jishui

Bode’s **o** is generally identified with **ο Geminorum**, now officially named **Jishui**. The Chinese name 積水/积水, *Jīshuǐ*, means “accumulated water,” “stored water,” or, in some translations, bilgewater or a water supply retained for making wine. It is an ancient Chinese single-star asterism; it is not a name derived from the telescope. [Jishui history](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Jishui)

### Dolones and Bouleges

The ψ Aurigae stars have sometimes been collectively associated with Greek **Βουλήγες** (*Boulēges/Bouleges*) and Latin **Dolones**.

- **Traditional/scholarly reconstruction:** *Bouleges* has been glossed as goads or ox-goads.
- **Disputed transmission:** *Dolones* may reflect Latinization, translation, or corruption in the star-name tradition. Latin *dolo/dolon* can denote a concealed weapon or pointed implement, but treating every ψ star as individually named “Dolones I, II, III…” is modern catalogue-making.
- Neither term was a name for Herschel’s telescope, and neither establishes an ancient predecessor constellation identical to it.

### Individual stellar characteristics

- **ψ² Aurigae / 50 Aur:** K-type orange giant, approximately magnitude 4.8; the brightest securely lettered Bode member.
- **ψ¹ Aurigae / 46 Aur:** luminous late-K or early-M supergiant, about magnitude 4.9; near the western continuation of the ψ group but not consistently included in Bode’s telescope.
- **ψ⁴ Aur / 55 Aur:** K-type giant, about magnitude 5.0.
- **ψ⁵ Aur / 56 Aur:** nearby F/G-type dwarf, magnitude about 5.2. Modern studies report infrared excess consistent with a debris disk; this has no historical connection with the constellation.
- **ψ⁷ Aur / 58 Aur:** K-type giant, about magnitude 5.0.
- **63 Aur:** K giant, about magnitude 4.9.
- **65 Aur:** G giant, about magnitude 5.1.
- **66 Aur:** K giant, about magnitude 5.2.
- **ο Gem / Jishui:** evolved star in modern Gemini, about fifth magnitude.

None has a classical proper name uniquely attached to Telescopium Herschelii.

### Asterisms within the same stars

The most important pre-existing asterism is Chinese **座旗, Zuòqí**, “Seat Flags” or “Flags beside the Seats.” Standard reconstructions place nine stars in eastern Auriga in it, including much of the ψ chain:

- ψ⁶ Aur — Zuoqi 1
- ψ⁴ Aur — Zuoqi 3
- ψ⁵ Aur — Zuoqi 4
- ψ² Aur — Zuoqi 5
- ψ⁷ Aur — Zuoqi 6
- ψ³ Aur — Zuoqi 7
- 51 Aur — Zuoqi 8
- ψ⁸ Aur — Zuoqi 9

Later Chinese catalogues added ψ¹, 47, 60, 62, 63, 64, and 66 Aur as supplementary stars. The exact determinative star and membership vary among historical reconstructions. The name is sometimes mistranslated “chairs” because 座 can mean seat, but the received asterism name is most often rendered “Seat Flags.” [Chinese Auriga table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auriga_in_Chinese_astronomy)

This is a genuine independent tradition in substantially the same stars and predates Hell by many centuries. It is not evidence that the Chinese recognized a telescope-shaped figure.

### Deep-sky objects

#### NGC 2281

The conspicuous deep-sky object within the historical footprint is **NGC 2281**, an open cluster in modern Auriga, visual magnitude about 5.4 and roughly 15–20 arcminutes across.

- **Documented:** William Herschel catalogued it as **VIII.71** and observed it as a cluster on 4 March 1788, describing a fairly rich scattering of relatively bright stars.
- **Disputed date:** historical catalogue research notes that Herschel had observed the field in his double-star work on 6 November 1782 without recording it unequivocally as a cluster. Sources therefore alternate between 1782 and 1788 as “discovery” dates. The secure cluster observation is 1788.
- **Modern nickname:** “Broken Heart Cluster” is a recent visual nickname, not a historical name from Hell or Bode.
- Other identifiers include Collinder 116, Melotte 51, OCL 446, and Herschel VIII.71. [Historical identification](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/ngc22a.htm); [Herschel catalogue reference](https://www.deepskycorner.ch/obj/ngc2281.en.php)

There is no Messier object in the figure. No nebula, galaxy, or cluster bears the official name “Telescopium Herschelii.” Modern “Herschel” survey objects and the Herschel Space Observatory’s H-ATLAS survey are unrelated namesakes.

### Variable stars, novae, and supernovae

No historically prominent nova or supernova is recorded under the obsolete constellation name. Searches of historical and modern catalogues did not locate a transient whose accepted designation uses Telescopium Herschelii.

Some stars in the wider former field are now catalogued variables or suspected variables under **Auriga**, **Gemini**, or **Lynx** designations. Those names postdate or bypass the obsolete constellation. It would be misleading to rename them retrospectively as variables “in Telescopium Herschelii.”

### Meteor showers

No recognized meteor shower uses Telescopium Herschelii as its radiant name. Radiants in this region are catalogued under Auriga, Gemini, or Lynx. Claims of “Telescopium Herscheliids” could not be traced to an IAU Meteor Data Center shower or a historical observing catalogue.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 150 CE: no telescope constellation

**Documented absence:** Babylonian and Egyptian uranography contains no telescope, an instrument invented in the early seventeenth century. The faint stars later used by Hell and Bode have not been securely reconstructed as one Babylonian or Egyptian unit.

### c. 150 CE — Ptolemy

Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogued Auriga, Ἡνίοχος, the Charioteer, and its surrounding or “unformed” stars. The later telescope region was not separated as a constellation. Some of the relevant faint stars are below or near the practical naked-eye threshold of ancient catalogues.

**Boundary status:** Ptolemy did not draw modern territorial borders. A constellation was a figure plus separately listed unformed stars. Consequently, saying Telescopium “took territory from Ptolemy’s Auriga” is a modern shorthand, not a literal boundary transfer.

### c. 964 — al-Ṣūfī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* retained Ptolemy’s Auriga as **ممسك الأعنة, mumsik al-aʿinna**, “the holder of the reins.” Al-Ṣūfī compared Ptolemaic descriptions with Arabic star lore, but no Herschel-telescope precursor occurs. The faint eastern stars were components of the wider Auriga field or unformed stars, not an independent Bedouin telescope-like asterism. [Al-Ṣūfī Auriga manuscript image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Book_of_the_Fixed_Stars_Auv0127_auriga.jpg)

### Medieval Latin and Hebrew transmission

Arabic and Greek astronomy passed through Latin translations of the *Almagest* and Arabic astronomical works. The relevant figure remained **Auriga**, the Charioteer, commonly *Auriga*, *Agitator*, or *Erichthonius* in mythographic discussion.

Hebrew astronomical literature translated or transliterated the conventional constellations in various ways, but no documented medieval Hebrew figure corresponds to Telescopium Herschelii. Later claims that every modern constellation has a biblical or tribal equivalent are not supported by a premodern source for these particular stars.

### 1515 — Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s printed northern celestial map, made with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, showed the established classical northern figures. The later telescope area remained within or alongside Auriga. No telescope appears.

### 1603 — Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* likewise contained Auriga, Gemini, and the surrounding northern constellations but no Herschelian telescope. Bayer’s Greek-letter system did not originally extend the familiar ψ¹–ψ¹⁰ sequence in its present form to a new telescope figure.

### 1627 — Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* was published at Augsburg by Andreas Aperger, with assistance from Bayer and engravings associated with Lucas Kilian and Johann Matthias Kager. It replaced pagan northern figures with New Testament subjects.

The telescope did not yet exist as a constellation. Therefore Schiller did not Christianize “Telescopium Herschelii.” Its future stars were absorbed into Schiller’s Christian replacement for the Auriga region. The atlas must be consulted by star positions rather than by projecting a later telescope backward onto it. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/); [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720)

### 1690 — Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius’s posthumously published *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* retained Auriga and introduced Lynx in the sparsely populated northern area. The future telescope lay across the visual and cataloguing interface between Auriga and the newly formalized Lynx.

Hevelius did not invent Telescopium Herschelii. His importance is territorial: Hell and Bode later inserted their instrument into a space whose neighbors had become Auriga, Gemini, and Lynx.

### 1756/1763 — Lacaille’s unrelated telescope

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille introduced **le Telescope** in his 1756 southern planisphere and Latinized it as **Telescopium** in his 1763 catalogue.

This is the modern southern constellation Telescopium, retained by the IAU as **Tel**, genitive **Telescopii**. It is entirely distinct from Telescopium Herschelii.

- Lacaille’s Telescopium represents a generic refracting telescope among his scientific instruments.
- Hell’s and Bode’s northern figure represents an instrument associated specifically with William Herschel.
- Confusing the two is a recurrent popular error.

### 13 March 1781 — discovery of Uranus

William Herschel, conducting a review of the sky from New King Street, Bath, noticed an object in or near Taurus with a **seven-foot Newtonian reflector**, approximately 6.2 inches aperture. He initially reported it as a comet in “Account of a Comet,” read to the Royal Society on 26 April 1781. Subsequent orbit calculation established it as a planet. [Royal Society manuscript record](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?id=L%26P%2F7%2F192&src=CalmView.Catalog); [surviving comparable seven-foot telescope](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-11122)

Herschel proposed **Georgium Sidus**, “George’s Star,” honoring George III. “Herschel” or “Herschel’s planet” circulated elsewhere.

### 1782 — Bode’s first commemoration

Evidence from surviving copies and institutional catalogue descriptions indicates that Bode inserted **Herschels Teleskop** near Auriga in his *Vorstellung der Gestirne*, the German adaptation and enlargement of Fortin’s reduced Flamsteed atlas. The University of Oklahoma describes its 1782 copy as containing the figure. [OU exhibition record](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/atlas-stars.html)

This creates an important attribution issue:

- **Documented:** Bode pictured or added a Herschel telescope in the 1782 atlas tradition.
- **Documented:** Hell published the first dedicated proposal of the two named Latin constellations *Tubus Herschelii Maior* and *Minor* in 1789.
- **Therefore disputed/terminological:** Hell is often called the “inventor” of Telescopium Herschelii, but Bode apparently used a German Herschel-telescope figure earlier. Hell’s priority is secure for the formal paired *Tubi Herschelii* proposal; Bode may have priority for the basic commemorative figure in approximately the later northern location.

Surviving copies of Bode’s small atlas were sometimes updated by hand or reissued from old plates. A German study notes original 1782 engravings bearing later additions associated with the 1805 edition. Each alleged 1782 image must therefore be dated as an impression or state of a plate, not merely by the engraved plate’s original date. This is the most significant unresolved bibliographical question.

### 1782–1784 — Bode and the name Uranus

Bode advocated **Uranus**, Latinized from Greek Οὐρανός, because the new planet beyond Saturn could appropriately bear the name of Saturn’s mythological father. His proposal appeared in the early 1780s and was further promoted in his 1784 writing on the new planet.

The name was not instantly universal. *Georgium Sidus*, “Herschel,” and “Uranus” coexisted for decades; the British *Nautical Almanac* did not finally adopt Uranus until the nineteenth century. Thus “Bode named Uranus” means that he proposed and successfully promoted the eventual international name, not that he possessed sole legal naming authority.

### 1789 — Hell’s formal paired invention

At Vienna, the Jesuit astronomer **Abbé Maximilian Hell** published:

*Monumenta, aere perenniora, inter astra ponenda. Primum serenissimo regi Angliae Georgio III., alterum viro celeberrimo Friderico Wilhelmo Herschel proposita et dedicata*.

The title consciously echoes Horace’s *monumentum aere perennius*, “a monument more lasting than bronze.” The pamphlet was printed by Johann Thomas von Trattnern and contains 40 pages and four engraved plates. [Bibliographical record](https://mek.oszk.hu/05500/05575/05575.pdf); [Linda Hall description and plates](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/maximilian-hell/)

Hell proposed three constellations:

1. **Psalterium Georgii**, George’s Psaltery or Harp, honoring George III.
2. **Tubus Herschelii Maior**, Herschel’s Greater Telescope.
3. **Tubus Herschelii Minor**, Herschel’s Lesser Telescope.

The two telescope plates commemorated Herschel and the discovery. The smaller telescope chart carries a legend by Uranus’s discovery position: *Planeta novus detectus ab Herschel 1781 die 13 Martii*—“new planet discovered by Herschel on 13 March 1781.”

#### Which instruments did Hell mean?

The best-supported reconstruction is:

- **Tubus Maior:** Herschel’s approximately 20-foot reflector, his principal survey instrument.
- **Tubus Minor:** the seven-foot reflector with which Uranus was discovered.

The **40-foot telescope** was completed in 1789 and was spectacularly famous, making it an attractive retrospective identification for “Maior.” But Hell’s detailed paired symbolism is better understood as contrasting the twenty-foot survey telescope with the seven-foot discovery instrument. Modern statements that the constellation directly depicts the forty-foot telescope require scrutiny and are not supported merely by the word *Maior*.

### 1801 — Bode’s Uranographia

Bode published at Berlin:

*Uranographia, sive astrorum descriptio viginti tabulis aeneis incisa ex recentissimis et absolutissimis astronomorum observationibus*.

The atlas contains twenty large principal copperplates and plots more than 17,000 stars, with thousands of nebular objects incorporated from contemporary catalogues. Its companion volume, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, catalogued 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. [Digital atlas record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035); [companion catalogue](https://books.google.com/books/about/Allgemeine_Beschreibung_und_Nachweisung.html?id=NUlRAAAAcAAJ&output=html_text)

Bode:

- kept the site of Hell’s **Tubus Herschelii Maior**;
- renamed it **Telescopium Herschelii**;
- omitted Hell’s separate **Tubus Minor**;
- pictured what appears to be the smaller, seven-foot discovery telescope rather than Hell’s twenty-foot “major” instrument;
- supplied its stars with letters **a–p**, with divided *p¹/p²* and an additional capital **B**;
- placed it between Auriga, Lynx, and Gemini.

This was not a merger in the geometrical sense. Bode did not combine both of Hell’s star fields into one constellation: he suppressed the southern minor field and retained/redesigned the northern major field.

The atlas’s immediate companions label it in Latin; later Bode publications and German editions use **Herschels Teleskop**.

### 1805 — revised *Vorstellung der Gestirne*

Bode’s later edition of his smaller atlas clearly shows Herschel’s telescope beside Auriga. The Linda Hall Library describes it as an accurate depiction of the seven-foot discovery instrument. Its discussion sometimes attributes the figure directly to the first 1782 edition, but the surviving plate-state problem means the exact dating of every addition requires copy-specific collation. [Linda Hall Bode exhibit](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/27-the-flamsteed-industry-1729-1805/32-bode-johann-vorstellung-der-gestirne/)

### 1822 — Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson included **Telescopium Herschelii** in *A Celestial Atlas*, London, 1822. The telescope appears with Lynx/Auriga on an elegantly simplified, hand-colored plate. Jamieson drew substantially on Bode and on the Flamsteed–Fortin atlas tradition. [Jamieson plate record](https://www.meisterdrucke.uk/fine-art-prints/Alexander-Jamieson/321262/The-Constellations-%28Plate-IV%29-Auriga%2C-Camelopaardalis%2C-Telescopium-Herschelli%2C-from-%27A-Celestial-Atlas%27-by-Alexander-Jamieson.html)

### 1824 — *Urania’s Mirror*

The constellation appears as **Telescopium Herschilii** on the Lynx card of *Urania’s Mirror; or, a View of the Heavens*, first advertised in November–December 1824.

- The cards were engraved by Sidney Hall.
- They were long attributed to “a lady.”
- Peter Hingley’s 1994 research identified the likely designer as the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam.
- Their figures closely copy Jamieson.
- Perforations allowed the cards to be held toward a light so their stars shone through.

The telescope’s card placement with Lynx helped later readers think of it as principally a Lynx constellation, although most of Bode’s identified stars now lie in Auriga. [Royal Astronomical Society](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror); [Smithsonian object record](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1460911)

### 1830s — Burritt

Elijah H. Burritt’s American *Atlas, Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens*, circulating in editions from the early 1830s and represented by an 1835 “New Edition,” continued the popular school-atlas tradition in which obsolete figures survived longer than they did in research catalogues. [AMNH copy](https://lbry-web-007.amnh.org/digital/items/show/18699); [David Rumsey record](https://www.davidrumsey.com/maps5787.html)

### c. 1850 — Aspin/Wyld planisphere

An *Improved Moveable Planisphere* associated with Jehoshaphat Aspin, Griffith, and James Wyld depicts Telescopium Herschelii as a small telescope on a tripod beneath Lynx. This documents continued pedagogical use after professional astronomers had begun omitting it.

### 1843–1885 — scientific rejection by omission

The figure was omitted by several influential nineteenth-century scientific atlases and catalogues, including works associated with:

- Friedrich Wilhelm Argelander, 1843;
- Richard A. Proctor, 1876;
- J. Rosser, 1879;
- Charles Pritchard, 1885.

This gradual omission, rather than an international decree, is how the figure first died. Professional positional astronomy needed stable constellation references; a faint honorary instrument without broad acceptance had little institutional advantage.

### 1899 — Allen

Richard Hinckley Allen’s *Star-Names and Their Meanings* stated that the constellation was becoming obsolete. Allen incorrectly dated Hell’s formation to **1781** and said it was first published by Bode in 1800. These dates were repeatedly copied. Hell’s dedicated pamphlet is securely dated **1789**, while Bode’s Uranographia is dated **1801**, although publication work and plate dating can produce “1800” in bibliographies. [Allen’s entry](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Telescopium_Herschelii%2A.html)

### 1922–1930 — standardization

The IAU Commission on Notations and Units adopted its list of 88 principal constellations and three-letter abbreviations at Rome in 1922. Telescopium Herschelii was absent.

Delporte then devised borders along right ascension and declination lines for equinox B1875.0, with instructions where possible to preserve existing variable-star constellation assignments. The boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in 1930. They left no residual Herschel-telescope territory.

### Present

Telescopium Herschelii survives as:

- an obsolete constellation in histories of cartography;
- an atlas image reproduced from Bode, Jamieson, and *Urania’s Mirror*;
- an unofficial reconstructed asterism;
- a useful case study in eighteenth-century commemorative constellation making.

It is not a recognized astronomical subdivision of the sky.

## Invention and reception

### Purpose

**Documented modern invention:** the figure honored William Herschel and the instrumental discovery of Uranus.

It also participated in a larger commemorative system:

- Herschel proposed *Georgium Sidus* for George III.
- Hell placed George’s Psaltery and Herschel’s telescopes among the stars.
- Bode advocated the classical planetary name Uranus but still honored Herschel through the telescope constellation.
- The instrument thus joined royal patronage, astronomical discovery, and technological progress in a single visual program.

### Was it placed where Uranus was found?

This often-repeated claim needs qualification.

Uranus was discovered close to ζ Tauri in Taurus, not in the later northern telescope’s principal star field in eastern Auriga. Hell’s **minor** telescope near Taurus and Orion, not the northern **major** telescope, most directly marked the discovery location. The Linda Hall copy shows the Uranus discovery inscription on the minor-telescope chart.

Therefore:

- **Documented:** Hell linked the paired telescopes with Herschel and Uranus.
- **Documented:** the minor telescope’s plate explicitly marks the discovery.
- **Misleading popular claim:** Bode’s northern Telescopium Herschelii was itself placed exactly where Uranus was discovered.
- **Better reconstruction:** Bode retained the major telescope’s northern field while illustrating the smaller discovery instrument there.

### Reception pattern

The figure’s reception divides into two streams:

1. **Professional astronomy:** weak and declining—many research atlases omitted it during the nineteenth century.
2. **Popular and educational cartography:** comparatively durable—Jamieson, *Urania’s Mirror*, school atlases, and planispheres preserved the visually attractive instrument.

It never acquired the widespread cataloguing utility enjoyed by Lacaille’s southern Telescopium.

## Myths and legends

### Classical mythology

Telescopium Herschelii has **no classical myth, catasterism, or ancient mythographic identity**.

It does not occur in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’ *De Astronomia/Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus or the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus’ *Aratea* or its scholia;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias;
- the ancient Latin mythographers.

Any attempt to give it the myths of Erichthonius, Myrtilus, Hippolytus, or the Charioteer is a transfer from **Auriga**, whose territory supplied many of the stars. Those myths belong to Auriga, not to the eighteenth-century telescope.

### A modern catasterism

Hell’s 1789 act can nevertheless be described as a deliberate **modern catasterism**: an instrument and living astronomer were placed in the sky to make their fame “more lasting than bronze.” Unlike a classical catasterism, no god performs the translation. The astronomer-cartographer does so through publication and the authority of a star chart.

### Later legend

A weak modern legend treats the telescope as the actual forty-foot instrument with which Herschel discovered Uranus. This conflates three telescopes:

- the seven-foot discovery reflector;
- the twenty-foot survey telescope;
- the forty-foot giant completed in 1789.

Herschel did not discover Uranus with the forty-foot telescope.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Greek and Roman

The area belonged to or bordered **Auriga**, Greek Ἡνίοχος, “Rein-holder/Charioteer,” Latin *Auriga*. The faint ψ-region was peripheral to the iconic pentagon and the Goat-and-Kids grouping. Collective names such as *Bouleges* and *Dolones* attach to part of the faint eastern field, but not to a telescope-shaped ancient constellation.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī’s name for Auriga, **mumsik al-aʿinna**, means “holder of the reins,” a close semantic rendering of the Greek figure. Bright-star Arabic lore emphasizes Capella, the Kids, and nearby Pleiades-related figures. No securely attested Bedouin asterism has been found matching Bode’s complete telescope outline.

This is an absence-of-evidence finding, not proof that every relevant faint star was culturally unnamed.

### Persian

Persian astronomical catalogues largely inherited Ptolemaic and Arabic constellation structures for this region. No premodern Persian Herschel-telescope antecedent exists. Later Persian terminology for the constellation is a translation of the European historical name, not an independent figure.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No historical Hebrew or Kabbalistic source examined identifies Bode’s ψ Aurigae chain as Herschel’s Telescope or as an equivalent ancient unit. Broad correspondences between constellations, the tribes of Israel, Hebrew months, or the sefirot vary by author and generally map the zodiac, not this eighteenth-century extra-zodiacal invention.

Claims assigning Telescopium Herschelii to a tribe, Hebrew letter, angel, or sefirah should be labeled **modern esoteric invention** unless accompanied by a post-1789 textual source.

### Chinese

Chinese **Zuoqi, Seat Flags**, is the strongest independent mapping and overlaps substantially with the tube and mounting. ο Gem’s **Jishui**, “Accumulated Water,” supplies another indigenous Chinese identity at the southern edge.

These mappings have no historical relationship to Herschel; the overlap is positional.

### Indian astronomy

The traditional Indian nakṣatra system follows the lunar path and does not create a counterpart to this high-northern telescope figure. The southern edge near Gemini lies broadly in the longitude range of Gemini-related nakṣatras, but assigning the whole telescope to one mansion would be geometrically and historically false.

### Lunar mansions

Because most of the telescope lies well north of the ecliptic, it is not itself a lunar-mansion figure. Chinese Zuoqi is placed in the wider sector of the **Zī (Turtle Beak)** mansion in common reconstructions. This is a sectoral association, not identity with the mansion’s determining stars.

### Astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* supplies planetary natures for major stars or portions of ancient constellations, not for Telescopium Herschelii. There is no ancient Ptolemaic delineation “for Herschel’s Telescope.”

Modern astrological sites assign zodiacal longitudes and influences to its stars by reconstructing the obsolete constellation. These are **modern astrological syntheses**, not recoveries of an ancient telescope tradition. Similarly, no star in the figure is securely one of the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No eighteenth- or nineteenth-century source located assigns a stable alchemical emblem or Hermetic doctrine to Telescopium Herschelii. Modern interpretations of the telescope as spiritual sight, revelation, Uranian consciousness, or technological gnosis are symbolically plausible but remain **modern inventions** unless tied to a named text and date.

## Iconography

### Ancient monuments

There can be no depiction of Telescopium Herschelii on the Farnese Atlas, ancient coins, Greco-Roman ceilings, cathedral portals, or medieval calendars: the figure did not yet exist.

The Farnese Atlas and medieval Aratea show Auriga or the established classical sky. Any claimed telescope in such art is anachronistic pattern projection.

### al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts show Auriga in paired views—one as seen in the sky and one as on a globe—under the Arabic rein-holder tradition. They supply important evidence for the prior ownership of the field, but no telescope.

### Dürer, 1515

Dürer’s northern map shows the classical Auriga. The later telescope stars occupy blank or subsidiary stellar space without an independent image.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer depicts the Charioteer with reins, goat, and kids. No instrument intrudes into the eastern field.

### Schiller, 1627

Schiller replaces pagan figures with Christian subjects, but because the telescope had not been invented, there is no one-to-one Christian substitute for it. Statements such as “Schiller replaced Herschel’s Telescope with Saint X” reverse chronology.

### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius’s Auriga and Lynx establish the iconographic neighbors into whose margins the telescope was later inserted.

### Hell, Vienna, 1789

Hell’s four engravings are the earliest securely dated dedicated iconographic program:

- a combined chart;
- Tubus Herschelii Maior;
- Tubus Herschelii Minor;
- Psalterium Georgii.

The plates are diagrammatic celestial proposals rather than richly mythological figure paintings. Their captions make the commemorative purpose explicit.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s plate is the canonical image. The telescope is mechanically rendered, mounted on a stand among the stars east of Auriga. Its placement visually competes with the Charioteer and Lynx while following the cartographic convention of mapping an object over selected stars.

A high-resolution reproduction is available in the [Wikimedia Telescopium Herschelii collection](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Telescopium_Herschelii).

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson simplifies and decoratively colors Bode’s scientific-instrument imagery. His atlas served as the direct visual source for the later constellation cards.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824

The Lynx and Telescopium Herschilii card converts Jamieson’s design into a domestic teaching object. Its misspelling became one of the best-known printed variants. Pinholes mark the stars; the figure could literally be illuminated from behind.

### Burritt, United States, 1830s

Burritt’s atlas continued the schoolroom afterlife of obsolete constellations. Such atlases prioritized a memorable “geography of the heavens,” retaining pictures after research astronomy had begun discarding them.

### Buildings, coins, and ceilings

No coin, permanent public ceiling, cathedral portal, or calendrical cycle securely identified in this research depicts Telescopium Herschelii under its own name. Telescopes and Herschel appear widely in scientific art, but resemblance alone does not establish this constellation.

## People, events, and works

### Maximilian Hell

Hell was an Imperial and Royal Astronomer in Vienna and a Jesuit. His *Monumenta* is the formal source for the paired Latin Tubi Herschelii. The work also reflects his interest in royal patronage and the public commemoration of astronomical achievement.

### William Herschel

The figure commemorated:

- his discovery of Uranus, 13 March 1781;
- his skill in constructing reflecting telescopes;
- the seven-foot discovery instrument;
- the larger twenty-foot instrument central to his surveys;
- by association, the forty-foot telescope completed in 1789.

It should not be assumed that every star or deep-sky object in the drawn figure was discovered by Herschel. NGC 2281 is a genuine and unusually apt exception.

### George III

George III was Herschel’s patron and appointed him to royal service. Hell’s larger program honored the king through **Psalterium Georgii** and honored Herschel through the two telescopes. The constellations therefore memorialized a patronage network as well as an astronomical event.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode’s role was larger than merely copying Hell:

- he had apparently inserted a German Herschel telescope in the *Vorstellung der Gestirne* tradition before Hell’s 1789 publication;
- he championed the name Uranus;
- in 1801 he selected one of Hell’s two sites, renamed it, changed the represented instrument, and assigned a distinctive letter series;
- his Uranographia gave the constellation its canonical visual form and its lasting Latin name.

### Alexander Jamieson, Richard Rouse Bloxam, and Sidney Hall

Jamieson transmitted the Bodean figure into British popular astronomy. Bloxam and Hall, through *Urania’s Mirror*, gave it its most widely reproduced decorative form.

### Joseph Cornell

Twentieth-century artist Joseph Cornell collected nineteenth-century celestial images and incorporated obsolete constellations and astronomical ephemera into his constructions. Scholarship on his source files notes Bode and Burritt imagery, including discredited constellations such as Telescopium Herschelii, as part of his visual vocabulary. [Princeton chapter discussing Cornell](https://assets.press.princeton.edu/chapters/s8743.pdf)

### Navigation, ships, and missions

No explorer is known to have navigated by “Telescopium Herschelii” as a recognized navigational constellation. Its stars are faint and its life was short; practical navigation relied on brighter standard stars.

No ship or space mission was located whose official name derives specifically from the obsolete constellation. The **Herschel Space Observatory** honored William Herschel directly, not Bode’s constellation. Likewise the H-ATLAS survey means Herschel Astrophysical Terahertz Large Area Survey and is not a revival of Bode’s atlas title.

## Boundary history

### Ptolemy

No lines: Auriga consisted of figured and unformed stars.

### Bayer

No exclusive polygonal borders. The future telescope field was peripheral Auriga/Gemini space.

### Hevelius

Lynx supplied a named neighbor in previously sparse territory, but still without modern exhaustive boundaries.

### Hell

Hell explicitly carved two figures from stars around Auriga, Gemini, Taurus, and Orion:

- the major telescope in the Auriga–Lynx–Gemini area;
- the minor telescope near Taurus–Orion and Uranus’s discovery field.

### Bode

Bode suppressed the southern minor constellation and assigned a–p/B labels to one northern Telescopium Herschelii. This temporarily removed stars from ordinary Auriga and Gemini labelling in his own catalogue system.

### Nineteenth century

Cataloguers who rejected the telescope restored its stars to Auriga or treated them by standard positional catalogue numbers. There was no single internationally recognized reversal date.

### Delporte

The B1875.0 rectilinear boundaries divided the entire field among Auriga, Lynx, and Gemini. Most lettered members fell in Auriga, ψ¹⁰ Aur became 16 Lyncis, and ο Gem remained in Gemini.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Hell 1781 versus Hell 1789

**Erroneous or weakly supported claim:** Hell invented the constellation in 1781.

**Evidence:** Hell’s surviving dedicated pamphlet is dated 1789. Allen’s 1899 entry gives 1781, evidently importing the date of Uranus’s discovery into the constellation’s history. Several modern encyclopedia pages copied Allen.

**Conclusion:** 1789 is the documented publication date for Hell’s paired proposal. An earlier Hell manuscript has not been demonstrated.

### 2. Bode or Hell as inventor

**Disputed attribution.**

- Hell has secure priority for the named paired Latin constellations *Tubus Herschelii Maior* and *Minor* in a dedicated 1789 publication.
- Institutional descriptions of Bode’s 1782 *Vorstellung der Gestirne* report a German **Herschels Teleskop** near Auriga.
- Some “1782” plates are later states or later impressions with additions.

**Conclusion:** describe Hell as formal inventor of the paired Tubi; describe Bode as a possibly earlier independent commemorator and certainly the creator of the canonical 1801 Telescopium Herschelii. Copy-specific bibliography is still needed to settle priority absolutely.

### 3. Seven-, twenty-, or forty-foot telescope

**Documented:** Uranus was discovered with a seven-foot reflector.

**Best reconstruction:** Hell’s minor tube represents the seven-foot instrument; his major tube represents the twenty-foot instrument.

**Documented Bodean transformation:** Bode retained the major constellation’s location but pictured a smaller discovery-type telescope.

**Popular error:** Uranus was discovered with Herschel’s forty-foot telescope. The forty-foot was completed eight years later.

### 4. One telescope or two

Hell created two separate figures. Bode did not simply “combine all their stars”; he retained the northern field, dropped the southern one, and renamed/redesigned what remained.

### 5. Where Uranus was discovered

Uranus was discovered near ζ Tauri. Hell’s minor constellation and its inscription correspond most closely to that location. Bode’s northern telescope is displaced well north of it.

### 6. Brightest star

ψ² Aur is the brightest securely assigned Bode member at about magnitude 4.8. Claims for θ Gem use a wider reconstructed footprint rather than Bode’s core membership.

### 7. Stars went to Auriga and Lynx—or Gemini too?

Most went to Auriga and a small number to Lynx. Bode’s **o**, identified as ο Gem/Jishui, requires Gemini to be included in a complete account. Secondary summaries that say only Auriga and Gemini or only Auriga and Lynx are each simplifying different reconstructions.

### 8. Official abbreviation “TeH”

**Modern invention:** “TeH” is useful in databases and astrological tables but was never adopted by the IAU. The same applies to an asserted official genitive *Telescopii Herschelii*. The Latin genitive is grammatically valid; it is not institutionally official.

### 9. *Herschilii*, *Hershelli*, and similar spellings

These are historical spelling and engraving variants, not separate constellations. The correct possessive Latin based on Herschel is normally **Herschelii**.

### 10. IAU “abolition in 1930”

This is imprecise. The constellation failed to enter the 1922 official list. Delporte’s 1930 publication completed the territorial replacement. No evidence was found of a 1930 resolution naming and abolishing Telescopium Herschelii individually.

### 11. Confusion with Lacaille’s Telescopium

Lacaille’s southern Telescopium is an official IAU constellation. Telescopium Herschelii is northern and obsolete. Images, star lists, meteor radiants, and genitives for the southern constellation are frequently misapplied to the Herschel figure.

### 12. “Bode’s atlas contained 830 stars in 109 groups”

The supplied phrase concerning “830 stars in 109 groups” or a Bode “collection on chain” could not be matched to Bode’s Uranographia, its companion catalogue, or a recognized institutional description. Bode’s 1801 companion catalogue advertises **17,240** entries; the atlas has twenty principal copperplates. The unexplained numbers may describe a museum object, database collection, or another catalogue, but no reliable connection was traced. It should remain **unverified**.

### 13. Hoaxes

No hoax originating specifically in Telescopium Herschelii was found. The famous 1835 “Great Moon Hoax” concerned John Herschel and an imaginary giant telescope, but it did not use Bode’s constellation. Associating the hoax with Telescopium Herschelii is a later thematic juxtaposition, not a historical link.

## Open questions

1. **Plate-state priority:** does an untouched, demonstrably first-state 1782 copy of Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* contain Herschels Teleskop, or do all surviving examples with it represent later revision or manuscript addition?

2. **Hell’s precise instrument keys:** a complete transcription and translation of the 1789 *Monumenta* is needed to determine whether Hell explicitly identifies the major tube as the twenty-foot telescope and the minor as the seven-foot, rather than leaving the association to the plates.

3. **Bode’s complete star cross-identification:** several faint stars labelled h, i, k, n, and B require a plate-coordinate reduction against modern Gaia positions. Published HIP identifications are plausible but should be independently recomputed.

4. **ο Geminorum:** Bode’s “o” is commonly cross-identified with ο Gem/Jishui, but a direct reading of the catalogue row and its 1801 coordinates would provide stronger confirmation.

5. **Atlas reception:** the two unnamed 1890s works said in secondary literature to retain the figure should be identified bibliographically rather than cited collectively.

6. **Non-Western faint-star catalogues:** Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Korean, and Indian sources may contain individual or supplemental stars in the field, but no complete indigenous equivalent of Bode’s outline has been demonstrated.

7. **“830 stars in 109 groups”:** the provenance of this description remains unidentified.
