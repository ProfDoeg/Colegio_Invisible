# Turdus Solitarius: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

### Status

**Abolished / not adopted by the International Astronomical Union.** Turdus Solitarius has no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, or boundary.

The IAU’s first General Assembly at Rome in May 1922 adopted the canonical constellation names and three-letter abbreviations. Turdus Solitarius was absent; strictly speaking, therefore, it was **excluded rather than individually “abolished.”** Eugène Delporte’s boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930. Those boundaries assigned the former figure’s principal stars to **Hydra and Libra**. [IAU history of the constellations](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [original 1922 list and its anomalies](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html)

**Present disposition:** the core figure—σ Librae, 4 Librae, and 54–58 Hydrae—now straddles southern Libra and Hydra. The faint eastern extremity sometimes included 60 Hydrae remains in Hydra.

### What the name means

- **Latin:** *Turdus Solitarius*, “Solitary Thrush.” *Turdus* is Latin for a thrush; *solitarius* means solitary, isolated, or living alone.
- **Historical genitive:** *Turdi Solitarii*. This occurs in historical and retrospective usage but was never standardized by the IAU.
- **IAU abbreviation:** none. Modern abbreviations such as “Tur” found in databases are retrospective conveniences, not IAU designations.
- **French, Le Monnier:** *Constellation du Solitaire*; simply *le Solitaire*.
- **German, Bode:** *der Vogel Einsiedler*, “the hermit/solitary bird,” in his instructional atlas; *Turdus Solitarius* on the monumental 1801 plate.
- **German, Ideler/Allen:** *Einsiedler* and *Drossel*, respectively “hermit/solitary one” and “thrush.”
- **English:** Solitary Thrush, Solitaire, Solitaire Bird, Hermit Bird; Thomas Young’s **Mocking Bird**; Alexander Jamieson’s **Noctua**, “Owl.”
- **Spanish:** *Tordo Solitario*, *Mirlo Solitario*, or *Pájaro Solitario*. The living blue rock thrush is *roquero solitario*.
- **Italian:** *Tordo Solitario*; the blue rock thrush is traditionally *passero solitario*.
- **French zoological vernacular:** *merle solitaire* or modern *monticole bleu*.
- **Portuguese:** *Tordo Solitário*; the living bird is *melro-azul*.
- **Russian:** *Одинокий Дрозд*, “solitary thrush.”
- **Chinese modern translations:** 畫眉座/画眉座 or 鶫座/鸫座 occur in modern lists of obsolete constellations. These are translations, not an ancient Chinese constellation.
- **Persian, Arabic, Hebrew:** no historically independent translation or traditional constellation name for Turdus Solitarius was found. Modern descriptive translations are possible, but presenting one as an inherited astronomical name would be misleading.

### Which bird?

This is the dossier’s central identification problem.

**Documented fact:** Le Monnier’s 1776 announcement calls the bird:

> “le Solitaire (oiseau des Indes & des Philippines)”

and says that Pingré and Brisson supplied its figure. He directs the reader to volume II of Brisson’s *Ornithologie*. That evidence points specifically toward the bird then called the Philippine or Oriental solitaire—the blue rock thrush, originally classified by Linnaeus in 1758 as *Turdus solitarius*, now *Monticola solitarius*. [Brisson, *Ornithologie*, vol. II](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/item/111092); [modern taxonomic record](https://birds-europe.linnaeus.naturalis.nl/linnaeus_ng/app/views/species/taxon.php?id=130869)

**Documented fact:** Le Monnier also says that the constellation was preserved “in memory of the voyage to Rodrigues,” associating it with Alexandre-Guy Pingré’s 1761 transit-of-Venus expedition.

**Later tradition:** Richard Hinckley Allen in 1899 asserted that the name meant the Rodrigues solitaire, *Pezophaps solitaria*, the extinct flightless pigeon related to the dodo, and therefore called *Turdus* erroneous. That interpretation became the dominant twentieth- and twenty-first-century popular account. [Allen, *Star Names*, pp. 418–19](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Turdus%2A.html)

**Best scholarly reconstruction:** the evidence supports a conflation:

1. The **commemorated place and expedition** were Rodrigues and Pingré’s voyage.
2. The **bird actually named and engraved** was Brisson’s “solitaire of the Indies and Philippines,” the blue rock thrush.
3. Later writers inferred that a constellation commemorating Rodrigues must represent the extinct Rodrigues solitaire.

Le Monnier never says in the surviving announcement that his constellation represents *Pezophaps solitaria*. The extinct pigeon interpretation is plausible as an associative reading but conflicts with his explicit reference to Brisson and with the passerine shown on the engraving.

### The constituent stars

Le Monnier’s original chart can be reconstructed as follows:

| Le Monnier’s figure | Modern identification | Modern constellation | Magnitude, approximately | Notes |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Heart/breast; γ Scorpii | **σ Librae**, 20 Librae | Libra | variable 3.20–3.46 | Brightest star; now officially **Brachium** |
| Eastern claw/foot | **58 Hydrae**, E Hydrae | Hydra | 4.42 | Officially named **Solitaire** in 2024 |
| Branch tip | **60 Hydrae** | Hydra | 5.83 | Faint eastern extremity |
| Lower eastern leg | **57 Hydrae** | Hydra | 5.77 | B9 V |
| Adjacent leg star | **56 Hydrae** | Hydra | 5.23 | G8/K0 III |
| Adjacent leg star | **55 Hydrae** | Hydra | 5.63 | A0 III |
| Upper legs/body junction | **54 Hydrae**, m Hydrae | Hydra | 5.15 | Visual binary; δ Turdi Solitarii in later reconstruction |
| Upper western leg | **4 Librae** | Libra | about 5.7 | Part of the later owl pattern |

This identification is given most explicitly in the modern reconstruction attached to the IAU-related All Skies Encyclopaedia project. [All Skies Encyclopaedia: Solitaire](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Solitaire)

Some accounts omit 58 and 60 Hydrae or define the figure as “4 Librae and 54–57 Hydrae.” That shorter list usually describes **Jamieson’s Noctua** or a simplified later asterism, not every plotted star on Le Monnier’s plate.

## Stars and objects

### σ Librae — Brachium

**Documented fact:** σ Librae is the brightest star of the figure. Le Monnier labeled it γ Scorpii because it belonged to the older conception of Libra as the Scorpion’s claws and retained the historical designation Gamma Scorpii. The IAU regularized it as σ Librae in 1930.

- Current designation: σ Librae, 20 Librae
- Historical Turdus designation: γ Turdi Solitarii in retrospective descriptions
- Variability: semiregular variable, type SRb
- Visual range: approximately magnitude 3.20–3.46
- Spectral class: M2.5 III
- Official proper name: **Brachium**, approved by the IAU Working Group on Star Names in 2017

**Etymology:**

- Latin *brachium*, “arm,” inherited from its location in the older Libra figure.
- Latin *cornu*, “horn,” also occurs.
- Arabic-derived forms include *Zuben el Genubi*, from Arabic *al-zubānā al-janūbiyya*, “the southern claw”; and forms of *Zubān al-ʿAqrab*, “the Scorpion’s claw.” These forms were not unique to σ Librae and were also applied to other Libra stars.
- “Ankaa,” occasionally attached to it in compilations, is non-unique and is now reserved officially for α Phoenicis.

The designation history embodies the Greek–Arabic–Latin transition from the **Scorpion’s claws** to the independent Balance. [σ Librae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_Librae)

### 58 Hydrae — Solitaire

**Documented fact:** 58 Hydrae is a magnitude-4.42 orange/red giant, catalogue identifiers HR 5526, HIP 72571, HD 130694. It also carries the non-Greek Bayer-style designation **E Hydrae**.

It had also been catalogued as **6 Librae**, showing how unstable the Hydra–Libra assignment remained before modern boundaries.

On 31 October 2024 the IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Solitaire** as its proper name, explicitly preserving the obsolete constellation. This does not revive Turdus Solitarius; it names one star inside modern Hydra. [WGSN 2024 Annual Report](https://exopla.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2024_Annual-Report-WGSN_for2024.pdf)

### 54 Hydrae

- Modern designations: 54 Hydrae, **m Hydrae**
- HD 129926; HIP 72197
- Combined magnitude about 5.15
- A visual binary, approximately F-type primary plus G-type companion
- Retrospectively identified as δ Turdi Solitarii
- The lowercase *m* designation is from Benjamin Apthorp Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina*, not Bayer’s 1603 atlas.

### 55–57 Hydrae

- **55 Hydrae:** HD 130158, HIP 72323; magnitude 5.63; A0 III.
- **56 Hydrae:** HD 130259, HIP 72357; magnitude 5.23; G8/K0 III.
- **57 Hydrae:** HD 130274, HIP 72378; magnitude about 5.77; B9 V.

They form the compact leg/body group that made the bird legible on Le Monnier’s engraving. Modern basic catalogue data are summarized in the [Hydra star list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stars_in_Hydra).

### 60 Hydrae

60 Hydrae, HD 132851 and HIP 73566, magnitude about 5.83, spectral type A4 IV, lies at the end of the branch or perch in the original reconstruction. Its omission from many popular descriptions is one reason different sources give different membership lists.

### Internal and successor asterisms

**Documented historical asterisms:**

- Le Monnier’s Solitaire: eight reconstructed principal stars.
- Bode’s Turdus Solitarius: substantially the same patch, depicted as a small passerine perched above Hydra’s tail.
- Young’s Mocking Bird: a British relabeling of the patch.
- Jamieson’s Noctua: an owl made mainly from σ Librae, 4 Librae, and 54–57 Hydrae; some drawings stretch farther toward southern Virgo.
- A modern binocular pattern among 54–59 Hydrae and nearby Libra stars has been compared with a “miniature Scorpius.” That is a recent observing nickname, not an inherited constellation.

### Deep-sky objects

**Negative finding:** no Messier object, NGC object, IC object, nebula, cluster, or galaxy was found whose recognized name derives from Turdus Solitarius, Solitaire, Mocking Bird, or Noctua.

Because the historical figure never acquired standardized boundaries, it is not methodologically sound to publish a definitive inventory of every deep-sky object “inside” it. Le Monnier described a rectangular vicinity roughly between right ascension 14h and 14h40m and declinations −15° to −25° for his epoch, while the actual plotted bird extends farther south in modern coordinates. Any modern object list would depend on an artificially chosen polygon.

### Variables, novae, and supernovae

- σ Librae/Brachium is itself a semiregular variable.
- No historically recorded nova or supernova is securely described as occurring “in Turdus Solitarius.”
- No recognized variable-star class, nova, or supernova remnant bears its name.
- Later variables in the same physical sky are catalogued under Hydra or Libra.

### Meteor showers

**Negative finding:** no established meteor shower is named for Turdus Solitarius, Solitaire, Mocking Bird, or Noctua. This differs from Quadrans Muralis, whose name survives in the Quadrantids.

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity to 1603: no bird

**Documented fact:** Turdus Solitarius did not exist in Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Indian, Hebrew, or medieval Latin uranography.

The same stars belonged to, or lay between:

- the late tail or “unformed” stars associated with Hydra;
- the Claws of Scorpius, later Libra;
- empty or weakly figured space between those inherited constellations.

Ptolemy’s Hydra ended iconographically near π Hydrae. The stars farther east were not a separate bird. His Libra material still preserved the older conception of the Scorpion’s claws. There is therefore no ancient catasterism of the “Solitary Thrush.”

### 1515 — Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s 1515 printed celestial maps preserve the Ptolemaic-Arabic constellation system. **Turdus Solitarius is absent.** The observation is chronologically important because claims that the bird has an ancient or Renaissance pedigree cannot be reconciled with these maps.

### 1603 — Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* contains Hydra and Libra/Scorpio but no Solitaire. The later labels 54–60 Hydrae are Flamsteed numbers; m Hydrae is still later, from Gould. Consequently, references to “Bayer letters” of Turdus are anachronistic unless they mean letters assigned by later cartographers in imitation of Bayer.

### 1627 — Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* predates the bird by nearly 150 years. In that atlas:

- Hydra became the **River Jordan**, *Iordanis Fluvius*.
- Libra became **Saint Philip the Apostle**, *Sancti Philippi Apostoli*.

Thus the future bird’s Hydra stars belonged to the Jordan figure, while σ and 4 Librae fell within Philip. Schiller could not have Christianized Turdus Solitarius itself. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/)

### 1660 — Cellarius

Andreas Cellarius reproduced both conventional and Schillerian celestial systems in *Harmonia Macrocosmica*. Again there is no Solitaire: the region appears as Hydra/Libra or their Christian replacements.

### 1690 — Hevelius

Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* and catalogue do not contain Turdus Solitarius. Claims that Hevelius invented or drew it confuse it with his genuine additions, or with later birds placed near Hydra.

### 1729 — Flamsteed

Flamsteed’s posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* likewise has no Solitaire. His star numbering supplied the later numbers 54–58 Hydrae, but it did not create the bird.

### 1761 — Pingré at Rodrigues

Alexandre-Guy Pingré travelled to Rodrigues to observe the transit of Venus. This is the voyage explicitly commemorated by Le Monnier.

**Correction of a recurrent claim:** the voyage was **Pingré’s, not Le Gentil’s**. Guillaume Le Gentil made his own famous transit expeditions in the Indian Ocean and Asia, which probably caused the confusion, but Le Monnier’s 1776 text names Pingré.

### 21 August 1776 — invention by Le Monnier

Pierre-Charles Le Monnier read his “Mémoire sur une nouvelle constellation” to the Académie royale des sciences on 21 August 1776. It was published as **“Constellation du Solitaire”**, pp. 561–62, with plate XVII.

His key statement reads:

> “J’ai observé la plus grande partie de ces Etoiles à mes deux quarts-de-cercles muraux, & la figure de la constellation du Solitaire (oiseau des Indes & des Philippines) a été préservé en mémoire du voyage en l’île Rodrigue, m’ayant été fourni par Mrs. Pingré et Brisson; voyez le tome ii de l’Ornithologie…”

Translation: “I observed the greater part of these stars with my two mural quadrants, and the figure of the constellation of the Solitaire—a bird of the Indies and the Philippines—has been preserved in memory of the voyage to Rodrigues Island, having been supplied to me by Messrs Pingré and Brisson; see volume II of the *Ornithology*…”

He concludes that the constellation will be neighbor to Corvus and Hydra on planispheres and celestial globes.

This is the earliest secure attestation. The constellation was both an observing aid and a voyage memorial; it was not presented as mythology.

### 1782 — Bode’s smaller atlas

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln*, a revised and expanded presentation based on the Flamsteed/Fortin tradition, includes the bird as the **Solitaire/Einsiedler bird**. Surviving online reproductions identify it in this edition, although plate states and later reissues require caution.

### 1795 — Fortin

The third edition of Jean-Nicolas Fortin’s French *Atlas Céleste de Flamstéed* depicts the Solitaire. The first 1776 Fortin edition cannot be assumed to include it merely because it shares the invention year; the addition is securely associated with the later edition.

### 1799 — Goldbach

Christian Friedrich Goldbach’s celestial atlas includes *Solitairvogel*, a German compound meaning “solitaire bird.” This shows continental uptake before Bode’s monumental atlas.

### 1801 — Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode placed **TURDUS SOLITARIUS** on the Virgo-region plate, at Hydra’s far eastern tail below Libra. The image is a small, normally flying/perching passerine, not a massive flightless pigeon.

Bode’s atlas consists of twenty large copperplate sheets—two hemispheric maps and eighteen detailed charts—and plots approximately 17,240 stars and numerous double stars, nebulae, and clusters. It was issued with the bilingual German/French catalogue *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*. [ETH-Bibliothek facsimile and metadata](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [companion catalogue](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

**Bode’s labels:**

- Plate: *Turdus Solitarius*
- German explanatory tradition: *der Vogel Einsiedler*
- French equivalent in the companion tradition: *l’Oiseau Solitaire* or *le Solitaire*

**Correction concerning Bode’s scale:** the supplied phrase “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be traced to Bode’s *Uranographia* or companion catalogue. The documented headline number is **17,240 stars** and more than one hundred constellation figures. “830/109” may belong to a later collection, database, or selection rather than Bode’s work itself.

### 1807 — Thomas Young’s Mocking Bird

Thomas Young’s *A Course of Lectures on Natural Philosophy and the Mechanical Arts* includes a celestial map in which the same region is called the **Mocking Bird**.

**Correction:** the Mocking Bird was Young’s usage, not Burritt’s. It is a relabeling rather than evidence that the American mockingbird had any connection with Le Monnier’s original Indian/Philippine bird.

### 1822 — Jamieson’s Noctua

Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* replaced the bird with **Noctua**, the Owl. Jamieson refers to Le Monnier’s predecessor as *L’Ermite Oiseau*—an awkward French “Hermit Bird”—rather than using *Turdus Solitarius*.

Jamieson justified the owl partly by remarking on its frequent appearance on Egyptian monuments. That is an antiquarian rationale for his **modern invention**, not evidence of an Egyptian owl constellation in these stars.

### 1824–25 — *Urania’s Mirror*

Card 32 of *Urania’s Mirror* depicts Noctua perched at Hydra’s tail, together with Corvus, Crater, Sextans Uraniae, Hydra, Felis, Lupus, Centaurus, Antlia, Argo Navis, and Pyxis.

The cards were first issued in November 1824 and conventionally dated 1825. They were engraved by Sidney Hall and advertised as the work of “a lady”; later research attributed their design to the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam. [Smithsonian collection record](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1460911); [specialist description](https://www.splrarebooks.com/collection/view/uranias-mirror)

### 1829 — Reissig

Kornelius Reissig’s Russian atlas *Sozvezdiia predstavlennyia na XXX tablicakh* reproduces the European illustrated-atlas tradition and includes the Solitaire form. This is one route by which the figure entered Russian historical constellation lists.

### 1835 — Burritt and Hoffmann

Elijah Hinsdale Burritt’s popular American *Atlas Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens* adopted **Noctua/The Owl**, not the Mocking Bird.

A German *Himmelsatlas für Freunde und Liebhaber der Sternkunde* associated with Hoffmann also illustrates the successor bird tradition in the same period.

### 1844 — Smyth

Admiral William Henry Smyth’s *Bedford Catalogue* still lists Turdus Solitarius. Its appearance in a serious observing handbook shows that the name remained intelligible, but not that it enjoyed universal scientific recognition.

### 1845 — Bale globe gores

Celestial globe gores printed by Bale, now associated with the Royal Museums Greenwich collections, depict *Solitarius* as a bird standing on Hydra’s tail. This is among its later material appearances on a globe rather than a book atlas.

### Late nineteenth century — decline

Argelander’s and Heis’s influential scientific atlases favored practical star plotting and the increasingly conventional constellation set. Turdus Solitarius and Noctua disappeared because:

- neither possessed an ancient pedigree;
- they competed with Hydra and Libra for the same stars;
- different national atlases used different birds and names;
- variable-star and catalogue nomenclature demanded stable constellation ownership;
- the patch is faint apart from σ Librae and 58 Hydrae.

Allen’s 1899 *Star Names* recorded the figure chiefly as a historical curiosity and popularized the Rodrigues-solitaire correction.

### 1922–30 — exclusion and boundaries

At Rome in 1922 the IAU adopted its standard list; Turdus Solitarius and Noctua were omitted. In 1928 the rectangular boundaries drawn by Delporte were approved, and in 1930 they were published for equinox B1875.0.

The practical result was:

- σ Librae and 4 Librae → **Libra**
- 54–60 Hydrae and 58/E Hydrae → **Hydra**

The modern Hydra–Libra boundary cuts through the former bird. It did not preserve the outline.

### 2016–18 — Paperno’s *Abolished Constellations*

Artist Alexandra Paperno created separate ink works titled *Turdus Solitarius* (2016) and *Solitaire* (2018) in her *Abolished Constellations* series. This is a documented modern artistic afterlife, not revival as an astronomical constellation. [Paperno, *Abolished Constellations*](https://alexandrapaperno.com/abolished-constellations-paper/)

### 2024 — the name returns as a star name

The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Solitaire** for 58 Hydrae. This is the first official IAU recognition of a name derived from the lost figure, while leaving the constellation itself obsolete.

## Invention and reception

### What it honored

The primary evidence says that the figure commemorated **Pingré’s voyage to Rodrigues**, undertaken for the 1761 transit of Venus.

It was not stated to honor:

- a king or patron;
- Le Gentil;
- a machine or instrument;
- the dodo’s extinction;
- a Greek myth.

Its astronomical position probably also served Le Monnier’s wish to organize a relatively empty area near Hydra’s extended tail.

### Why a bird there?

Corvus already stood upon Hydra’s back. Le Monnier’s bird was placed farther east, making a second avian marker along the serpent. The choice fitted the eighteenth-century atlas habit of filling “unformed” spaces with memorable zoological or technical figures.

The visual emptiness is real: after σ Librae and 58 Hydrae, most members are only fifth or sixth magnitude. This faintness helped prevent wide independent recognition.

### Reception by major uranographers

- **Ptolemy:** impossible; figure did not exist.
- **Bayer:** absent.
- **Schiller:** absent; its future stars were divided between Saint Philip and the Jordan.
- **Hevelius:** absent.
- **Flamsteed:** absent as a figure, though his catalogue numbers later identified its Hydra stars.
- **Fortin 1795:** accepted the Solitaire.
- **Goldbach 1799:** accepted it as *Solitairvogel*.
- **Bode 1801:** canonized the Latin *Turdus Solitarius* in the most ambitious atlas of the period.
- **Thomas Young 1807:** transformed it into the Mocking Bird.
- **Jamieson 1822:** transformed it into Noctua.
- ***Urania’s Mirror* 1824–25:** adopted Jamieson’s owl.
- **Reissig 1829:** retained the Solitaire-type figure.
- **Burritt from the 1830s:** adopted Noctua.
- **Argelander and Heis:** did not make it part of the enduring scientific canon.
- **IAU 1922:** excluded it.
- **Delporte 1930:** assigned the sky to Hydra and Libra.

## Myths and legends

### Classical mythology

**Negative finding:** there is no Greek or Roman myth, catasterism, or ancient literary testimony for Turdus Solitarius.

Accordingly it does not occur in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus’ *Phaenomena*;
- the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus’ *Aratea*;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias;
- Nonnus;
- the Vatican or later classical mythographers.

No god, hero, or king was said to place this bird among the stars. Any purported classical “myth of Turdus Solitarius” is a modern fabrication or a transfer from Hydra, Corvus, Libra, or generic owl symbolism.

### The neighboring Hydra and Corvus myth

**Ancient myth, not a myth of Turdus:** in the standard Greek catasterism, Apollo sent the raven to fetch water. The raven delayed at a fig tree, returned with a water snake, and blamed the snake. Apollo placed Raven, Cup, and Hydra together in the sky and condemned the raven to thirst. Versions occur in Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* 41 and Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* II.40.

This story explains Corvus and Crater on Hydra; it does not explain Le Monnier’s second bird at the tail.

### Libra and the Scorpion’s claws

**Ancient astronomical tradition:** the Libra stars were earlier the Scorpion’s claws, Greek *Chelai*. Roman astronomy increasingly treated them as the Balance, associated with equity and the autumnal equinox. σ Librae’s history as γ Scorpii and its Arabic claw names preserve that older identity.

Again, this is inherited lore of σ Librae, not a myth created for the solitary bird.

### Egyptian owl claim

**Documented nineteenth-century antiquarian claim:** Jamieson said it was strange that the owl had not been placed among the constellations given its frequent carving on Egyptian monuments.

**Assessment:** owls certainly occur in Egyptian writing and art, especially as the hieroglyph conventionally transliterated *m*. No evidence was found for an ancient Egyptian owl constellation occupying σ Librae and 54–57 Hydrae. Jamieson’s argument is reception history, not historical Egyptian uranography.

### Rodrigues-solitaire legend

**Later tradition:** the constellation represents the extinct Rodrigues solitaire, a flightless pigeon known particularly from François Leguat’s account of his 1691–93 stay.

**Evidence:** Rodrigues is explicit in Le Monnier’s dedication, but the bird identification is not. The passerine engraving and Brisson citation point elsewhere. The extinct-pigeon story is therefore best labeled **later interpretive tradition**, popularized by Allen and modern retellings.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian and Egyptian

No distinct Babylonian or Egyptian asterism corresponding exactly to Le Monnier’s eight-star bird was located.

The region’s zodiacal stars participate in the much older Scorpion/claw complex, but equating that complex with Turdus Solitarius would erase major differences in scale and meaning.

### Greek and Roman

σ Librae belonged to the Scorpion’s claws and then Libra. The Hydra stars were either part of the serpent’s extended region or unformed stars. No bird existed there.

### Arabic and al-Sufi

Al-Sufi’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* followed Ptolemy’s figures while recording Arabic nomenclature and observations. He did not know a Solitaire constellation.

For σ Librae, the important Arabic tradition is the claw vocabulary:

- *al-zubānā al-janūbiyya*, southern claw;
- *zubān al-ʿaqrab*, claw of the Scorpion.

No secure Bedouin bird asterism using the Turdus stars was found. Popular claims that “al-Sufi called it the solitary bird” are unsupported.

### Persian

Persian Islamic astronomy inherited the Ptolemaic/al-Sufi constellation structure. No Persian Turdus-equivalent was located before the European figure was imported. Ulugh Beg’s catalogue likewise predates and does not contain it.

### Chinese

The relevant indigenous Chinese asterism is **折威, Shé Wēi**, usually glossed “Executions,” “Breaking Majesty,” or “Punishment of the Powerful,” in the enclosure of the lunar mansion **亢宿, Kàng**, the Neck.

Reconstructions commonly associate Shé Wēi with σ Librae, 50 Hydrae, 3, 4, and 12 Librae, with additional stars depending on the source and epoch. Thus it overlaps the western bird principally at σ Librae and 4 Librae. It is not a Chinese thrush or owl. [Chinese entry for 折威](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%8A%98%E5%A8%81)

54 Hydrae is sometimes catalogued as **Shé Wēi Zēng Sān**, “third added star of Shé Wēi,” reflecting later supplementary-star mapping.

### Indian

The Libra portion lies in the broad sidereal domain associated with **Viśākhā**, whose commonly used yogatārā or reference stars are α and β Librae, not the faint Hydra group. No Indian solitaire-bird asterism in these exact stars was found.

It is safer to say that lunar-mansion longitude crosses the region than to identify Turdus with Viśākhā.

### Arabic lunar mansions

The Libra/Scorpius claw region is associated with **al-Zubānā**, the sixteenth Arabic lunar mansion, “the two claws.” The named mansion centers on the brighter Libra claw stars, especially α and β Librae. σ Librae lies within the broader claw tradition, but the Hydra members of Turdus are not the mansion’s defining pair.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No premodern Hebrew constellation corresponding to Turdus Solitarius was found. Hebrew astronomical and astrological works generally transmitted zodiacal Libra/Scorpio and Hydra-related material rather than this late French bird.

Likewise, no historically sourced Kabbalistic assignment of Turdus Solitarius survives. Modern occult diagrams that assign a Hebrew letter, tribe, angel, tarot card, or sefirah specifically to the constellation are modern inventions unless they cite a post-1776 source.

### Christianized sky

Schiller’s 1627 assignments divide the later figure:

- Hydra stars → the **River Jordan**
- Libra stars → **Saint Philip the Apostle**

There was therefore no single saint or object replacing Turdus; Schiller predates it.

### Astrology

Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* I.9, assigns the stars at the extremities of the Scorpion’s Claws the nature of Jupiter and Mercury, and those in the middle portions the nature of Saturn with some Mars. σ Librae belongs to this inherited claw environment, though matching each description to a modern star is not always certain. [Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* I](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/ptolemy/tetrabiblos/1b%2A.html)

Turdus Solitarius itself has no traditional Hellenistic astrological character because it was invented in 1776.

None of its principal stars is among the canonical fifteen **Behenian fixed stars**. Nearby Algorab in Corvus is Behenian, but it does not belong to Turdus. Claims that Brachium or Solitaire has an ancient Hermetic sigil were not substantiated.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No premodern alchemical or Hermetic reading of Turdus Solitarius is possible under that name. Later symbolic readings—solitude, hermitage, extinction, nocturnal wisdom through Noctua—are modern associative interpretations. They may be legitimate artistic traditions, but they are not recoverable ancient doctrine.

## Iconography

### Objects that cannot depict it

Because the figure was invented in 1776, it is absent from:

- the Farnese Atlas;
- Greco-Roman celestial globes;
- al-Sufi manuscripts;
- medieval Aratus manuscripts;
- cathedral zodiac portals and medieval calendars;
- Dürer’s 1515 maps;
- Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria*;
- Schiller’s 1627 atlas;
- Cellarius’s 1660 maps;
- Hevelius’s 1690 atlas;
- Flamsteed’s 1729 atlas.

Claims of Turdus on these works result from mistaking Corvus, Hydra, an owl emblem, or a damaged figure for the eighteenth-century constellation.

### 1776 — Le Monnier and Le Gouaz

Plate XVII accompanying Le Monnier’s memoir is the first image. The engraver is identified in modern bibliographical work as Yves-Marie Le Gouaz. The bird is a small perching passerine supported by the plotted stars.

The caption and text make the image scientific as well as emblematic: it maps Le Monnier’s mural-quadrant observations.

### 1795 — Fortin

Fortin’s later *Atlas Céleste* received the Solitaire into the Flamsteed-derived French atlas tradition. This helped transmit it to Bode.

### 1799 — Goldbach

Goldbach’s atlas labels the bird *Solitairvogel*. The image demonstrates that the figure was not solely Bode’s creation.

### 1801 — Bode

Bode’s copperplate shows the bird perched over Hydra’s tail below Libra, facing roughly along the serpent’s extension. The label *Turdus Solitarius* makes Linnaean zoological Latin visually explicit. [High-resolution Bode image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Turdus_Solitarius.JPG)

### 1807 — Young

Young’s celestial map uses the Mocking Bird name. Its imagery belongs to the British reworking of continental figures rather than to Le Monnier’s ornithological source.

### 1822 — Jamieson

Jamieson draws a conspicuous owl, **Noctua**, in the same neighborhood. It is not simply Bode’s thrush with a changed caption: the artistic body and sometimes the stellar reach were redesigned.

### 1824–25 — *Urania’s Mirror*

Card 32 presents a richly colored owl perched on Hydra. The card’s star holes could be held to light. This is the best-known popular image descended from Turdus, although it depicts the successor Noctua.

### 1829 and 1835

Reissig’s St Petersburg atlas and German instructional charts continued the figure in eastern and central European print culture. Burritt’s American atlas made the owl more familiar to nineteenth-century English-speaking readers than Bode’s thrush.

### Globe gores, c. 1845

Bale’s printed globe gores depict *Solitarius* standing on Hydra’s tail. Their survival in a museum collection confirms that the name persisted in material globe production after Jamieson’s attempted replacement.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

**Negative finding:** no securely documented ancient coin, monumental ceiling, cathedral portal, or traditional calendar depicts Turdus Solitarius as such.

The living blue rock thrush appears on Maltese one-lira coins because it is Malta’s national bird. That iconography belongs to *Monticola solitarius*, not directly to the constellation, though the shared Linnaean name makes it relevant to the identification dispute.

## People, events, and works

### Pierre-Charles Le Monnier

Le Monnier invented the constellation, observed most of its stars with two mural quadrants, and presented it to the French Academy in 1776.

### Alexandre-Guy Pingré

Pingré supplied or helped supply the bird figure and made the Rodrigues expedition commemorated by the constellation. His 1761 transit observations are the historical event most directly attached to it.

### Mathurin-Jacques Brisson

Brisson’s *Ornithologie*, volume II, supplied Le Monnier’s zoological reference. This connection is decisive evidence for the blue-rock-thrush side of the controversy.

### Yves-Marie Le Gouaz

Le Gouaz engraved the first constellation plate. Whether he, Le Monnier, Pingré, or Brisson determined the precise bird pose cannot presently be established.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode disseminated the Latin name and created its most famous chart appearance. He did not invent the constellation.

### Thomas Young

Young introduced the Mocking Bird label into British scientific illustration in 1807.

### Alexander Jamieson

Jamieson redesigned the space as Noctua in 1822 and supplied an Egyptian-antiquarian explanation for the owl.

### Elijah Hinsdale Burritt

Burritt popularized Jamieson’s owl in American astronomical education. He did not originate the Mocking Bird.

### François Leguat and the Rodrigues solitaire

Leguat’s account and illustration are the principal early witnesses to the extinct Rodrigues solitaire. The connection to the constellation was made explicit by later writers, particularly Allen, rather than unambiguously by Le Monnier.

### Navigation and exploration

No explorer or navigator was found who demonstrably steered by “Turdus Solitarius” as a recognized navigational constellation. Its stars could of course be observed in navigation, but they would normally have been referred to through Libra, Scorpio, Hydra, catalogue coordinates, or individual stellar designations.

### Literature, music, architecture, ships, and missions

No historical ship, spacecraft, astronomical mission, building, or major musical work securely named for the constellation was found.

A modern literary title, *Constellation du Solitaire*, exists, but title identity alone does not establish deliberate reference to Le Monnier’s constellation. Such claims require authorial or textual evidence.

Alexandra Paperno’s visual-art series is the clearest documented modern cultural use.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Rodrigues solitaire or blue rock thrush?

**Disputed.**

- **For Rodrigues:** Le Monnier explicitly commemorated a voyage to Rodrigues; later writers naturally associated “Solitaire” with the island’s famous extinct bird.
- **Against:** Le Monnier calls it an Indian and Philippine bird, cites Brisson volume II, and the engraving is a passerine. *Turdus solitarius* was Linnaeus’s 1758 name for the blue rock thrush.
- **Conclusion:** the constellation commemorates Rodrigues, but its named and pictured bird is most directly identifiable as the blue rock thrush. “Rodrigues solitaire” is a later conflation, not a secure quotation of Le Monnier’s intent.

### 2. Was *Turdus* a zoological mistake?

**Conditional.**

Allen’s statement that *Turdus* is erroneous is correct **only if** the intended bird was *Pezophaps solitaria*. It is not erroneous for the eighteenth-century classification of the blue rock thrush, whose Linnaean basionym is precisely *Turdus solitarius*.

Modern taxonomy places the blue rock thrush in *Monticola* and the family Muscicapidae rather than in *Turdus*, but Bode’s 1801 use was historically intelligible.

### 3. Le Gentil or Pingré?

**Resolved by the primary text:** Pingré.

Le Gentil’s transit voyage is frequently blended with Pingré’s in popular memory. Le Monnier explicitly names Pingré, and Rodrigues was Pingré’s station.

### 4. Who invented the Latin name?

Le Monnier invented *Constellation du Solitaire* in French. Bode’s atlas made **Turdus Solitarius** canonical in Latin. Whether an intervening catalogue used exactly that binomial before Bode requires further bibliographical checking, but the phrase fits Linnaeus’s already established bird name and was not a random mistranslation.

### 5. Who invented the Mocking Bird?

**Thomas Young, 1807**, on the evidence found. Attributing it to Burritt is a common compression. Burritt popularized **Noctua/The Owl**.

### 6. Who invented Noctua?

The first securely traced full atlas appearance is Jamieson’s 1822 *Celestial Atlas*. An earlier anonymous use remains possible but was not found. Jamieson is therefore the conventional and best-supported attribution.

### 7. Did Noctua simply replace Turdus?

Only approximately. It occupied substantially the same region and borrowed its most obvious stars, but its outline could extend toward other Libra/Virgo stars. “Replacement” describes cartographic succession, not exact identity of boundaries.

### 8. Was it abolished in 1922 or 1930?

Both dates are used loosely.

- **1922:** it was omitted when the accepted constellation names and abbreviations were adopted.
- **1928:** Delporte’s boundaries were approved.
- **1930:** the boundary tables and atlas were published.

Thus “abolished in 1922” is convenient but not the wording of a specific resolution against Turdus.

### 9. Did Bode catalogue 830 stars in 109 groups?

**Unverified and probably conflated.** The consulted Bode records state 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters in the companion catalogue and more than one hundred figures in the atlas. No Bode source located here supports the exact “830 stars in 109 groups” formulation.

### 10. Does it have an official abbreviation?

No. “Tur” and the grammatical genitive *Turdi Solitarii* may be used by historical databases, but only the 88 accepted constellations received IAU abbreviations.

### 11. Was there a classical, Egyptian, or Bedouin solitary-bird constellation?

No evidence was found. The ancient stories and names belong to Hydra, the Scorpion’s claws/Libra, Corvus, Shé Wēi, or lunar mansions. Transferring them to Turdus is a modern syncretism.

### 12. Hoaxes and invented lore

The contested-material search found no famous dedicated hoax concerning Turdus Solitarius. The main errors are cumulative rather than fraudulent:

- describing the Rodrigues solitaire identification as Le Monnier’s explicit statement;
- assigning Pingré’s voyage to Le Gentil;
- calling Bode the inventor;
- making Burritt inventor of the Mocking Bird;
- giving the figure an IAU abbreviation or 1930 boundary;
- supplying ancient myths merely because neighboring Hydra and Libra have them;
- mistaking Jamieson’s Egyptian-art remark for evidence of an Egyptian constellation.

## Open questions

1. **Earliest exact occurrence of the Latin binomial:** Bode’s 1801 plate is secure, but a complete search of every intermediate Fortin, Bode, and catalogue issue could reveal earlier printed *Turdus Solitarius*.

2. **Authorship of the bird drawing:** Le Monnier says Pingré and Brisson supplied the figure, while Le Gouaz engraved it. Their precise division of responsibility is unclear.

3. **Plate-state differences:** Bode’s 1782 *Vorstellung*, later reissues, Fortin’s editions, and separately issued charts may not all contain identical outlines or labels.

4. **Exact Bode star letters:** Later writers reconstruct γ and δ Turdi Solitarii, but a diplomatic transcription of the 1801 companion catalogue’s complete Turdus entry would be valuable.

5. **Noctua’s full membership:** simplified accounts give σ Librae, 4 Librae, and 54–57 Hydrae, while broader readings of Jamieson’s artwork reach toward southern Virgo. Figure, catalogue, and nominal “boundary” should be distinguished.

6. **The “830 stars in 109 groups” phrase:** its source remains unidentified.

7. **Rodrigues intention:** it remains possible that Le Monnier deliberately played on two birds called “solitaire,” but no surviving explicit statement proves this.
