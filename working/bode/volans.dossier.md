# Volans: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary key

**Status: KEPT.** Volans is one of the 88 constellations recognized by the International Astronomical Union. The IAU adopted the name and abbreviation at Rome in 1922; Eugène Delporte’s boundary, approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930, remains authoritative.

**Modern constellation.** Volans is not in Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, classical Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, or Indian uranography. Its documented history begins with Dutch navigation in 1595–97. Consequently, there are no authentic classical myths, catasterisms, Ptolemaic astrological qualities, Behenian stars, or ancient lunar mansions belonging specifically to Volans.

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** directly supported by a contemporary primary source or official catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion drawn by modern specialists from primary materials.
- **Tradition:** a recurrent interpretation whose precise inventor may be unknown.
- **Disputed:** supported by conflicting evidence or attribution.
- **Legend/myth:** a story not presented as empirical history.
- **Modern invention:** an explicitly modern symbolic or narrative construction.
- **Unverified:** repeated in secondary literature but not located in an early or authoritative source during this investigation.

### What the figure is

**Documented natural-history interpretation.** Flying fishes are tropical and subtropical marine fishes that escape predators by accelerating through the water, breaking the surface, and gliding on greatly enlarged pectoral fins. “Flying” is conventional rather than aerodynamic powered flight.

**Historical reconstruction.** The choice of animal fits what Dutch mariners on the East Indies route actually encountered. Contemporary and near-contemporary accounts treat flying fish as conspicuous ocean creatures, sometimes landing aboard ships. The stars were therefore organized around a recognizable seafaring experience, rather than an inherited Mediterranean myth.

**Traditional pictorial narrative.** Early globes and atlases place Volans near the ship Argo and before Dorado/Xiphias. Bayer and later artists make Dorado look as though it is pursuing the flying fish. This is a visual relationship, not a Greek catasterism.

## Stars and objects

### The historical and modern figure

The conspicuous modern outline is normally drawn through six principal lights:

- α Volantis
- β Volantis
- γ² Volantis
- δ Volantis
- ε Volantis
- ζ Volantis

One common outline makes two triangles sharing ε:

- α–β–ε
- γ²–ζ–ε

and extends ε–δ as the tail. This geometrical convention is clearly recognizable in early-modern charts, though the IAU defines only the surrounding area, not an official stick figure.

De Houtman’s printed 1603 catalogue contained a small group under *De Vlieghende Visch*. Contemporary accounts differ between five, seven, and—when duplicated or subsidiary stars are counted—more entries. The modern machine-readable reconstruction of the early southern catalogues counts **seven** Volans entries in de Houtman and **eight** in Halley. [Verbunt and van Gent, “Early star catalogues of the southern sky”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.0946)

| **δ Volantis** | 4.0 | Luminous F-type star | No traditional proper name. |

| **ε Volantis** | 4.35 | B-type subgiant, multiple-star system | A telescopic companion makes it a notable double/multiple system. |

**Documented nomenclatural fact.** None of Volans’s traditional Bayer stars has an ancient Arabic proper name. The IAU Working Group on Star Names’ historical-name programme therefore does not supply Volans with an equivalent of Sirius, Canopus, or Fomalhaut. Commercial “star names” are not official.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

A recurrent popular phrase, “12 Bayer/Flamsteed stars,” is misleading.

- **Documented:** Lacaille assigned the Greek letters α through κ, with subdivisions for γ and κ, to twelve stars in the constellation.
- **Documented:** Flamsteed’s original observational programme was made from England and did not cover this far-southern sky. Volans therefore has **no authentic Flamsteed-number sequence**.
- Numbers sometimes printed beside southern stars are from later catalogues—Gould, Brisbane, Cape, HD, HR, HIP, SAO, and others—not Flamsteed’s *Historia Coelestis Britannica*.

The list often rendered “9 G. Vol,” “29 G. Vol,” and so forth consists of Gould designations, not Flamsteed numbers.

### Asterisms

**Historical figure:** the fish itself is the only durable asterism internal to Volans.

**No ancient internal asterism is documented.** The region was too far south for the classical Mediterranean, Mesopotamian, Iranian, northern Indian, Chinese, and central Arabian systems to preserve these stars as an independent recognizable group.

### Double and multiple stars

- **γ Volantis:** γ², the golden/yellow giant, and γ¹, the fainter yellow-white component, make an easy small-telescope pair.
- **ε Volantis:** a multiple system whose visible companion is accessible to modest telescopes under good southern skies.
- Several fainter systems in modern double-star catalogues lie inside Delporte’s boundary, but they do not belong to the historical fish outline.

### Variable stars

- **R Volantis** and **S Volantis** are long-period variables. The Astronomical Society of Southern Africa has specifically encouraged further monitoring because historical light curves were incomplete.
- Several Bayer stars, notably γ² and β, have at times been flagged as suspected low-amplitude variables. Such catalogue flags should not be confused with the large changes of Mira-type R and S Vol.
- Modern all-sky surveys list many additional eclipsing, pulsating, rotational, and irregular variables inside the IAU region. Their membership is positional, not iconographic.

### Novae, supernovae, and other transients

**Milky Way novae.** No famous naked-eye historical nova is securely assigned to Volans. Its far-southern position, faint foreground field, and late introduction explain the absence of ancient records under its name.

**Supernovae in NGC 2442.** The Meathook Galaxy has hosted recorded extragalactic supernovae, most notably **SN 1999ga**, seen in one of its spiral arms. ESO’s account explicitly notes the recent supernova in the tightly folded arm. [ESO, “Two Views of a Lopsided Galaxy”](https://www.hq.eso.org/public/news/eso1115/)

**Tidal-disruption event ASASSN-19bt.** This stellar disruption by a supermassive black hole occurred in a galaxy lying in the direction of Volans and became an early tidal-disruption event observed by TESS. Calling it “a star of Volans” would be wrong: it was an extragalactic transient merely located within the constellation’s modern boundary. [NASA visualization description](https://nas.nasa.gov/pubs/gallery.html)

### Deep-sky objects

There are **no Messier objects** in Volans.

#### NGC 2442/2443: the Meathook Galaxy

**Documented fact.** NGC 2442 is a distorted barred/intermediate spiral roughly 50 million light-years away. Its two asymmetric arms produce the hook shape behind the modern nickname “Meathook Galaxy.” The distortion probably resulted from gravitational interaction, although the responsible companion has not been conclusively identified. [ESO object page](https://www.hq.eso.org/public/images/ngc2442/)

**Catalogue controversy.** Herschel described what appeared to be a double nebular structure. Dreyer catalogued the parts as **NGC 2442** and **NGC 2443**. Modern astronomy recognizes them as parts of one galaxy; NGC 2443 is therefore treated as a duplicate designation or a bright portion of NGC 2442 rather than a separate galaxy.

#### NGC 2434

An elliptical galaxy in the NGC 2442 group. It is one of the brighter telescopic galaxies in Volans but has no widely accepted popular name.

#### NGC 2397

A flocculent spiral galaxy, also associated with the small NGC 2442 galaxy group. Supernova **SN 2006bc** was observed in it.

#### AM 0644-741: Lindsay–Shapley Ring

A collision-produced ring galaxy about 300 million light-years away. Its blue star-forming ring is approximately 150,000 light-years in diameter. It is also called the **Lindsay–Shapley Ring** after its investigators. [NASA/Hubble object page](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/hubble/blue-stars-ring-nucleus-of-galaxy-am-0644-741/)

**Name controversy.** “Graham’s Object,” “Das Rheingold,” and “Nibelungen Ring” occur in twentieth-century observing literature for the same or closely associated ring-galaxy identification. They are not official IAU names and have never displaced AM 0644-741/Lindsay–Shapley Ring.

#### Other catalogue objects

Objects placed within the Delporte polygon include:

- NGC 2305 — galaxy
- NGC 2307 — galaxy
- NGC 2348 — sparse/open cluster identification
- NGC 2397A and NGC 2397B — galaxies
- NGC 2466 — galaxy
- NGC 2601 — galaxy
- NGC 2788A — galaxy
- IC 2202 — galaxy

Catalogue membership varies slightly among databases when old coordinates, duplicate entries, or object types are revised. A useful positional compilation lists twelve NGC/IC entries. [Deep-sky catalogue list](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/volans-deepsky-objects)

### The Volans–Carina association: the “Volans stream” lead

**Verified but terminology corrected.** In 2018 Jonathan Gagné, Jacqueline Faherty, and Eric Mamajek characterized the **Volans–Carina Association**, a nearby, approximately 90-million-year-old stellar population at roughly 75–100 pc. It had previously appeared as “Group 30” in Oh et al.’s Gaia-based work. Their initial sample contained nineteen likely and forty-six candidate members.

It is a young stellar association or moving group, not in the usual sense a long, tidally disrupted halo stellar stream. Later kinematic literature may discuss such associations under the broad heading of disk streams, which likely explains the phrase “Volans stream.” The safest formal name is **Volans–Carina Association (VCA)**. [Discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04420)

### Meteor showers

Older summaries stating “no meteor shower” are obsolete.

#### Volantids

**Documented modern discovery.** A short outburst radiating from Volans was detected around 31 December 2015 and named the **Volantids**. It is IAU Meteor Data Center shower no. **758**, code **VOL**. The radiant is near RA 123°, Dec −72°, with geocentric speed near 30 km/s.

The stream was detected again around 2020 and in the 2025–26 apparition. Its episodic behavior is consistent with a compact stream associated with a Jupiter-family-comet-like orbit; a unique parent has not been securely established. [SETI Institute report](https://www.seti.org/news/volantid-meteors-2025-2026/), [contemporary discovery report](https://astronomynow.com/2016/02/25/surprise-discovery-of-new-southern-hemisphere-meteor-shower/)

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1595: no Volans

**Documented absence.** Volans does not occur in:

- Babylonian star lists or *MUL.APIN*;
- Egyptian decanal lists;
- Eudoxus or Aratus;
- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hipparchus;
- Ptolemy’s *Almagest*;
- the *Tetrabiblos*;
- Latin celestial catalogues derived from Ptolemy;
- al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita*;
- the classical Chinese Three Enclosures and Twenty-Eight Mansions;
- Indian nakṣatra catalogues.

Its principal stars lie around declinations −65° to −75°. They are invisible from Babylon, Alexandria, most of Arabia and Persia, northern India, and the historical Chinese capitals. Ptolemy’s southern limit was far north of most of Volans.

The Farnese Atlas, medieval Aratea manuscripts, Arabic Ptolemaic manuscripts, and Dürer’s 1515 celestial maps therefore cannot depict Volans.

### 1595–97: the first Dutch voyage

**Documented historical setting.** Petrus Plancius instructed the navigator Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser—also written Petrus Theodori or Peter Dircksz Keyser—to make southern-star observations during the first Dutch expedition to the East Indies under Cornelis de Houtman.

Keyser observed from the ship and during stops in the Indian Ocean and East Indies. He died in Java in September 1596. His observational material returned to the Netherlands with the surviving expedition in 1597.

Frederik de Houtman participated in the voyage and is associated with the observations. Precisely how the labor was divided among Keyser, de Houtman, and Plancius is not recorded.

### 1597 or 1598: Plancius–Hondius globe

**Documented first graphic attestation.** The twelve new southern constellations appeared on a celestial globe designed by Petrus Plancius and engraved or manufactured with Jodocus Hondius in Amsterdam. Bibliographies differ between late 1597 and early 1598; “1598” is the conventional date.

Volans was shown as a flying fish. No surviving document definitively establishes whether Keyser, de Houtman, or Plancius personally invented the figure. Plancius had the editorial control necessary to turn positions into constellation images.

### 1602–03: Blaeu globes

Willem Janszoon Blaeu incorporated the new southern constellations into his globes, initially through the Plancius-Keyser tradition and subsequently with revised data associated with de Houtman. The proximity and apparent chase between Dorado and the flying fish were retained.

### 1603: de Houtman’s printed catalogue

**Documented first surviving printed catalogue.** Frederik de Houtman appended southern-star declinations to his Malay–Dutch–Malagasy language book, *Spraeck ende woord-boeck inde Maleysche ende Madagaskarsche talen*. Its constellation heading is **De Vlieghende Visch**.

This catalogue, printed at Amsterdam by Jan Evertsz. Cloppenburch, is the oldest surviving printed catalogue based directly on the Dutch southern observations. A modern re-identification finds seven entries under Volans. [WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/ko/title/Spraeck-ende-woord-boeck-inde-Maleysche-ende-Madagaskarsche-talen-met-vele-Arabische-ende-Turcsche-woorden-...-%3A-Noch-zijn-hier-byghevoecht-de-declinatien-van-vele-vaste-sterren-staende-omtrent-den-Zuyd-pool-.../oclc/68675342), [modern catalogue analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.0946)

### 1603: Bayer’s *Uranometria*

**Documented first atlas depiction.** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* gave the figure the Latin title **Piscis Volans**. It appears on Bayer’s south-polar plate and its stars also appear in relation to Argo Navis.

Bayer made the constellation famous but did not originate the underlying observations. His imagery derives from the Dutch globe tradition.

The fish is depicted out of the water, fins spread, beside Argo. Dorado is positioned behind it in a way readily read as pursuit.

### 1605: Paulus Merula

Merula’s *Cosmographiae generalis libri tres* recorded early alternative names:

- *Hirundo marina*
- Dutch *Zeezwaluwe*

This is valuable evidence that the figure was conceptualized zoologically as the “sea swallow,” a widespread early name for flying fish.

### 1627: Kepler and Schiller

Kepler’s *Tabulae Rudolphinae* printed southern material derived from the Keyser/de Houtman tradition under supplementary classes.

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Augsburg, 1627, replaced Volans and Dorado together with **Abel the Just**, *Abelis Iusti*. In Schiller’s plate LIII, the two fish constellations supplied stars for Abel’s body and surroundings. Schiller did not discover new stars; he imposed a biblical figure on Bayer’s geometry. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/)

### 1650s–60s: Cellarius and globe transmission

Andreas Cellarius included both conventional and Schillerian heavens in the *Harmonia Macrocosmica* (1660/61), helping preserve Schiller’s Christianized reinterpretation as an antiquarian curiosity. Conventional globe makers retained *Piscis Volans*.

### 1675: Edward Sherburne

Sherburne’s *The Sphere of Marcus Manilius* listed the recent southern constellation and recorded synonyms such as:

- *Piscis Volans*
- *Volucris*
- *Volatilis*
- *Passer Marinus*
- *Hirundo Marina*
- *Chelidon Thalassia*

These are natural-history glosses, not classical names despite their Latin and Greek dress.

### 1679: Edmond Halley

Halley’s Saint Helena catalogue contained eight stars under **Piscis Volans**. Modern analysis identifies all eight. Halley’s work supplied later atlas makers with improved southern positions.

### 1688–90: Coronelli and Hevelius

Vincenzo Coronelli’s globes included the flying fish.

Johannes Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia*, published at Gdańsk in 1690 by Elisabeth Hevelius, depicted **Piscis Volans** on the grand south-polar plate. The image shows the fish with extended wing-like fins amid Hydrus, Pavo, Indus, Phoenix, Tucana, Grus, Chamaeleon, Musca, Xiphias/Dorado, Ara, and Triangulum Australe. [Hevelius south-polar plate description](https://raremaps.com/gallery/detail/114613/hydra-pavo-indus-phoenix-toucan-grus-chameleon-piscis-volans-xiphias-musca-ara-triangulum-australe)

### 1721–29: Senex and Flamsteed

John Senex’s planispheres used *Piscis Volans*.

Flamsteed’s posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* included the southern figure in its general cartography, although Flamsteed himself had not observed its stars from Greenwich and did not give them genuine Flamsteed numbers.

### 1742: Doppelmayr

Johann Gabriel Doppelmayr’s *Atlas Coelestis* retained *Piscis Volans* as a winged fish.

### 1751–56: Lacaille

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed the southern sky at the Cape of Good Hope. His planisphere and catalogue retained the flying fish while introducing new neighboring constellations, including **Pictor** and **Mensa**.

Lacaille assigned the Greek letters used today, including divided γ and κ designations. His work transformed Volans from a small early-voyage figure into a more systematically catalogued constellation.

### 1760–95: French atlas tradition

Gabriel-Philippe de La Hire labelled it **Le Poisson Volant**. Fortin’s reduced French edition of Flamsteed and its revisions by Le Monnier, Lalande, and Méchain retained the winged fish and the apparent Dorado pursuit.

### 1782: Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne*

Bode’s compact German atlas labelled it **der Fliegende Fisch** and showed it beside Argo, with Dorado—called a swordfish in Bode’s German terminology—apparently behind it.

### 1799: Goldbach

Christian Friedrich Goldbach’s atlas retained the flying-fish-and-pursuer composition, illustrating how established the visual narrative had become by the end of the eighteenth century.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented centerpiece.** Johann Elert Bode’s *Joannis Elerti Bode Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio*, published in Berlin “apud autorem” with financial support from Friedrich von Hahn, depicts the constellation as **PISCIS VOLANS** on the southern polar chart.

The atlas contains twenty large copperplate charts. Volans appears as a naturalistic fish with greatly extended pectoral fins, placed immediately beside the remnants of Argo Navis and before Xiphias/Dorado. The star points and catalogue lettering are superimposed on the engraved body. The image therefore preserves both strands of the tradition:

1. the navigators’ observed tropical animal;
2. the atlas convention of a flying fish escaping a larger predatory fish.

Bode’s atlas was descriptive rather than mythographic. Its accompanying material catalogs positions and magnitudes; it does not give Volans a classical catasterism. The full title claims reliance on the “most recent and complete observations of astronomers.” The work plotted over 17,000 stars and numerous nebulae, while its constellation imagery deliberately preserved figures then current—including many that the IAU later rejected.

Digital facsimiles: [ETH Zürich](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [Aix-Marseille University](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035).

### 1822–25: Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* (London, 1822) included the southern constellations in planispheric treatment.

*Urania’s Mirror* (London, 1824; commonly dated 1825 for later issues) copied much of Jamieson’s iconography. The cards concentrated heavily on northern figures and did not give Volans the kind of independent card accorded to famous northern constellations. Where the southern polar field is summarized, it remains the flying fish.

The anonymous author of *Urania’s Mirror* was identified in 1994 as the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam. The RAS describes the images as substantially copied from Jamieson. [Royal Astronomical Society](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror)

### 1835: Burritt

Elijah H. Burritt’s *Geography of the Heavens* and accompanying atlas label the figure **Piscis Volans, the Flying Fish** on the southern circumpolar map.

### 1844–45: shortening to Volans

**Documented name reform.** John Herschel, in “Farther Remarks on the Division of Southern Constellations,” proposed reducing cumbersome multiword southern names. *Piscis Volans* became **Volans**. Herschel observed that Lacaille had himself sometimes abbreviated constellation names.

Francis Baily adopted the recommendation in the British Association Catalogue of 1845. The shortened form then spread through nineteenth-century scientific catalogues.

This change did not mean “the IAU deleted *Piscis*” in 1922; the one-word form had already been standard for roughly three-quarters of a century.

### Argelander and Heis

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* was principally designed for stars visible from central Europe and thus did not give Volans the observational prominence of southern catalogues.

Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* similarly reflects northern observing priorities. Volans persisted on comprehensive planispheres and catalogues, but not as a useful naked-eye constellation for German observers.

### 1890s–1910s: Gould and historical reconstruction

Benjamin Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* supplied systematic southern designations and photometry. Gould numbers are now occasionally mistaken for Flamsteed numbers.

J. L. E. Dreyer’s NGC consolidated the nebular and galactic objects now geographically “in Volans.”

E. B. Knobel reconstructed de Houtman’s old catalogue in 1917. Modern astrometric comparison has accepted much of his work but revised a number of identifications across the southern catalogue.

### 1922: IAU retention

At its first General Assembly in Rome, the IAU adopted 88 constellation names and standard three-letter abbreviations. Volans was retained as **Vol**. [IAU historical account](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### 1928–30: Delporte’s boundary

Delporte drew an official polygon in right ascension and declination referred to B1875.0. It was approved at the IAU’s Leiden General Assembly in 1928 and published in 1930.

The boundary converted Volans from a pictured fish into a precisely defined area. Precession makes the originally horizontal and vertical B1875 lines appear slightly tilted on modern J2000 charts.

### 2015–26: new phenomena bearing the name

- 2015: the Volantid meteor outburst was discovered.
- 2018: the Volans–Carina Association was characterized and named.
- 2020 and 2025–26: further Volantid activity strengthened the case for an episodic meteoroid stream.

## Invention and reception

### Who invented Volans?

The most defensible attribution is:

> **Observed by Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser, with Frederik de Houtman involved in the voyage and later independent/revisional observations; converted into a constellation within the Plancius cartographic programme; first surviving representation on the Plancius–Hondius globe of 1597/98; first atlas depiction by Johann Bayer in 1603.**

Individual credit is **disputed** because the evidentiary stages differ:

- Keyser was commissioned to observe.
- De Houtman preserved, extended, and printed southern positions.
- Plancius controlled the cosmographic project and globe design.
- Hondius engraved or manufactured the globe.
- Bayer disseminated the figure internationally.

Calling it “invented by Bayer” confuses popularization with first appearance.

### What it honored

Volans did not honor a monarch, patron, instrument, or saint. It represented an animal familiar from the tropical voyage.

The selection belongs to a larger programme of southern images drawn from navigation, natural history, and European encounters with the East Indies: toucan, bird of paradise, peacock, chameleon, flying fish, dorado, and related figures.

### The Dorado pursuit

**Documented iconographic fact:** Bayer, Hevelius, Fortin, Bode, and others arranged the two fish so Dorado/Xiphias appears to chase Volans.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** dolphinfish pursue flying fish in nature, and sailors could directly witness the predator-prey behavior.

**Unverified as a contemporary verbal story:** no located text by Keyser, de Houtman, or Plancius explicitly says, “We placed Dorado there because it is chasing Volans.” The chase is nevertheless unmistakable in repeated cartography and biologically appropriate.

### Boundary development

#### Ptolemy

No Volans existed. Most of its stars fell outside Ptolemy’s observable/catalogued sky. It was not carved from a Ptolemaic constellation in the ordinary sense.

#### Plancius and Bayer

A small star group was placed between Argo, Dorado, and the south-polar region. Bayer drew it against or beside Argo’s hull.

#### Hevelius

Hevelius preserved the group in the south-polar chart. His figure was pictorial; borders were not exclusive legal lines.

#### Lacaille

Lacaille added Pictor and Mensa in adjacent blank spaces and regularized Volans’s stellar nomenclature. Thus the region later touching Pictor and Mensa had not possessed those neighbors under Bayer.

#### Bode

Bode preserved the flying fish and surrounding early-modern figures at maximum pictorial elaboration. He did not suppress Volans or absorb it into Argo.

#### Delporte

Delporte fixed Volans between Carina, Pictor, Dorado, Mensa, and Chamaeleon. The modern boundary contains the traditional principal stars.

No prominent Volans Bayer star is known to have been transferred into a neighbor in the manner of notorious boundary casualties such as 10 Ursae Majoris. Its chief historical alteration was environmental: Argo Navis became Carina, Puppis, and Vela, while Lacaille’s Pictor and Mensa occupied previously unnamed surroundings.

## Myths and legends

## Classical Greek and Roman corpus

### Catasterisms

**Documented absence.** There is no Volans entry or flying-fish catasterism in:

- Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi*;
- the anonymous Epitome of the *Catasterismi*;
- Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica*;
- Hyginus, *Fabulae*;
- the scholia to Aratus;
- the scholia to Germanicus;
- Ovid’s *Fasti* or *Metamorphoses*;
- Manilius, *Astronomica*;
- Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*;
- Pausanias, *Description of Greece*.

No god or hero is said in those texts to have placed a flying fish among the stars. Every web page calling the Dorado chase “the Greek myth of Volans” is modern conflation.

### Argo connection

**Modern association built from placement.** Because Volans lies alongside Argo Navis in Bayer and Bode, writers sometimes fold the fish into Jason’s voyage. No ancient Argonautic source knows the constellation. Apollonius Rhodius, Valerius Flaccus, Orphic Argonautica, Pseudo-Apollodorus, and Hyginus contain sea creatures and marvels, but none provides a Volans catasterism.

### “Fish saved the crew”

A story that flying fish landed on ships and supplemented sailors’ food is historically plausible and occurs in later maritime discussion. It is not a surviving foundation legend from Keyser or de Houtman and should be labeled **reconstruction/tradition**, not documented motive.

## Christian tradition

Schiller’s 1627 replacement is the only sustained premodern religious reinterpretation specific to these stars. Volans and Dorado were merged into **Abel the Just**.

This is not an assertion that Abel had ancient celestial associations. It is a deliberate seventeenth-century Christian rewriting of Bayer’s sky.

## Modern popular “myth”

The usual modern narrative is:

> Dorado, the predatory dolphinfish, chases the flying fish, which leaps from the sea beside the ship Argo.

This story is a fair verbalization of the maps and animal behavior. Its classification is **modern iconographic narrative**, not classical myth.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian and Egyptian

**No demonstrated antecedent.** The stars are below the practical horizon of the relevant traditions. Neither *MUL.APIN* nor known Egyptian decanal and Ramesside star-clock systems provides a convincing counterpart.

Claims that Volans was an Egyptian fish, the Nile tilapia, or a form of Hatmehit are **unverified modern analogies**. Those deities and images exist, but there is no positional evidence linking them to Volans.

### Greek and Latin

There is no ancient constellation. Classical Greek knew flying fish as zoological curiosities, and later writers could call marine animals *chelidōn* (“swallow”), but this is not evidence for a stellar *Chelidon Thalassia*.

Sherburne’s Greek-styled *Chelidon Thalassia* is an early-modern learned translation.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī’s tenth-century *Book of the Fixed Stars* follows Ptolemy’s 48 constellations and does not contain Volans.

The stars are too far south for the classical Bedouin asterisms documented across central Arabia. No securely identified Bedouin asterism using α, β, γ, δ, ε, or ζ Volantis was located.

Modern Arabic translations should not be attributed to al-Ṣūfī.

### Persian

Classical Persian astronomical works inherited Ptolemaic/Arabic constellations and do not provide a Volans figure. Modern Persian *māhī-ye parande* is a translation.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic traditions

No biblical *mazzarot*, rabbinic constellation list, medieval Jewish astronomical table, or standard Kabbalistic zodiacal scheme places a flying fish here.

Schiller’s Abel should not be called a Jewish or Kabbalistic mapping: it is a Christian atlas produced in Augsburg.

### Indian astronomy

The nakṣatras follow the ecliptic and do not cross Volans. Classical Indian constellation catalogues influenced by Greek astronomy likewise offer no equivalent.

Southern Indian and maritime communities undoubtedly possessed southern-sky knowledge, but no published identification securely equating one of their named asterisms with Volans’s exact principal stars was located.

### Chinese astronomy

Volans did not belong to the ancient Three Enclosures and Twenty-Eight Mansions.

After Western charts entered China through Jesuit collaboration with Xu Guangqi and Johann Adam Schall von Bell, the southern sky was organized into the **Twenty-three Southern Asterisms**. Here the imported figure became:

- **飛魚, Fēiyú**, “Flying Fish.”

The conventional star sequence is:

- α Vol — 飛魚一, Fēiyú yī, first star of Flying Fish
- γ Vol — 飛魚二
- β Vol — 飛魚三
- κ Vol — 飛魚四
- δ Vol — 飛魚五
- ζ Vol — 飛魚六

This is Chinese astronomical history, but specifically the Chinese reception of European southern uranography. [Chinese astronomy summary and references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volans_in_Chinese_astronomy)

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No canonical Renaissance alchemical or Hermetic text attaches a distinct operation, metal, element, or planetary intelligence to Volans.

Modern esoteric writers may interpret the fish as volatile matter, spiritual ascent, escape from the waters, or the alchemical *volatilization* suggested by Latin *volans*. Such readings are **modern inventions based on wordplay**, not documented Bode-era traditions.

### Astrology

Volans is non-zodiacal and was unknown to Ptolemy. Therefore:

- it has no qualities in the *Tetrabiblos*;
- none of its stars is one of the fifteen Behenian fixed stars;
- no Arabic lunar mansion, Indian nakṣatra, or Chinese lunar mansion crosses it;
- no ancient paranatellon is assigned to it.

Modern “constellation astrology” pages assign meanings such as adaptability, escape, maritime skill, or soaring above adversity. These are **modern inventions**, generally extrapolated from the picture. Tables placing Volans’s stars at tropical zodiacal longitudes describe precessing coordinate positions, not an ancient Volans astrology.

## Iconography

### Objects that cannot contain Volans

- **Farnese Atlas**, Roman copy of a Hellenistic celestial globe, Naples: no Volans.
- **Medieval Aratea manuscripts:** no Volans.
- **al-Ṣūfī manuscripts**, tenth century onward: no Volans.
- **Dürer, Stabius, and Heinfogel celestial maps, Nuremberg 1515:** no Volans.

Any image identifying Volans in these objects is an anachronistic overlay.

### Dated pictorial sequence

- **1597/98, Amsterdam — Plancius/Hondius globe:** earliest surviving representation; flying fish in the south-polar sky.
- **1602/03, Amsterdam — Blaeu globes:** the fish transmitted with improved southern data.
- **1603, Augsburg — Bayer, *Uranometria*:** first atlas depiction, titled *Piscis Volans*; fish beside Argo and before Dorado.
- **1627, Augsburg — Schiller:** Volans and Dorado replaced by Abel the Just; engravings associated with Lucas Kilian and Johann Matthias Kager.
- **1660/61, Amsterdam — Cellarius:** conventional and Christianized skies reproduced in the *Harmonia Macrocosmica*.
- **1688, Venice/Rome globe tradition — Coronelli:** winged fish retained.
- **1690, Gdańsk — Hevelius:** lavish south-polar engraving; fins exaggerated into wings.
- **1721/22, London — Senex:** planispheric *Piscis Volans*.
- **1729, London — Flamsteed atlas:** included in southern mapping despite lack of Greenwich observations.
- **1742, Nuremberg — Doppelmayr:** conventional flying fish.
- **1756, Paris — Lacaille planisphere:** winged fish, with newer scientific-instrument constellations nearby.
- **1760, France — La Hire:** *Le Poisson Volant*.
- **1776–95, Paris — Fortin/Le Monnier/Lalande/Méchain:** flying fish with Dorado pursuit retained.
- **1782, Berlin — Bode, *Vorstellung der Gestirne*:** *Fliegende Fisch*.
- **1799 — Goldbach:** two-fish pursuit scene.
- **1801, Berlin — Bode, *Uranographia*:** naturalistic *Piscis Volans*, large copperplate southern chart.
- **1822, London — Jamieson:** southern-planisphere reception.
- **1824/25, London — *Urania’s Mirror*:** derivative of Jamieson; no major independent Volans card tradition.
- **1835, United States — Burritt:** *Piscis Volans, the Flying Fish*.
- **Later nineteenth century:** scientific atlases progressively reduce or omit pictorial bodies in favor of lines, magnitudes, and coordinates.
- **Twentieth century onward:** IAU charts show boundaries and stars, not an official fish image.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

No ancient coin, cathedral zodiac portal, medieval calendar, or Renaissance ceiling can securely be said to depict Volans as a constellation.

Flying fish occur independently in decorative arts, heraldry, natural-history illustration, and maritime imagery. Without stars or a label, such images are not evidence of the constellation.

No securely documented major architectural ceiling devoted specifically to Volans was found. Comprehensive modern planetarium ceilings may include it as one of the 88.

## People, events, and works

### Navigators and observers

- **Petrus Plancius:** minister, cartographer, and organizer of the southern-star programme.
- **Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser:** navigator and principal observer during the 1595–97 voyage.
- **Frederik de Houtman:** voyager, reviser, and first printer of the surviving southern catalogue.
- **Jodocus Hondius:** globe engraver and manufacturer associated with the first representation.
- **Willem Janszoon Blaeu:** globe maker who transmitted and revised the southern constellation data.
- **Johann Bayer:** made *Piscis Volans* internationally familiar in 1603.
- **Edmond Halley:** catalogued eight Volans stars from Saint Helena.
- **Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille:** supplied the familiar Greek letters and improved positions.
- **Johann Elert Bode:** produced its most monumental Enlightenment-era rendering.
- **John Herschel:** proposed shortening the name.
- **Francis Baily:** implemented Herschel’s proposal in a major catalogue.
- **Eugène Delporte:** drew the modern legal boundary.

### Navigation

Volans itself is too faint to have been a major oceanic navigation constellation. Its brightest stars are only around fourth magnitude.

Southern navigators instead relied more heavily on:

- the Southern Cross and Centaurus for the south-polar direction;
- Canopus for latitude and course checks;
- Magellanic Clouds and other bright southern markers;
- solar and stellar altitude measurements.

Thus “explorers steered by Volans” is **unverified as a specific historical practice**. The constellation arose from navigation, but that does not mean it became a standard navigational tool.

### Scientific objects and discoveries

- William Herschel’s nebular observations underlie NGC 2442/2443.
- John Herschel’s southern surveys contributed to knowledge of the Volans galaxy field.
- John Louis Emil Dreyer regularized the NGC designations.
- Eric Lindsay and Harlow Shapley are attached to the Lindsay–Shapley Ring.
- Modern ESO and Hubble imaging popularized the “Meathook” and ring-galaxy imagery.
- Gagné, Faherty, and Mamajek named and characterized the Volans–Carina Association.
- Southern radar and video meteor networks established the Volantids.

### Ships, aircraft, and missions

The Latin word *volans* appears widely in zoological species names—*Draco volans*, *Petauroides volans*, and others—but those are not named for the constellation.

No major historical ship central to the constellation’s creation was itself named *Volans*. Modern boats, aircraft, amateur observatories, software projects, and fictional spacecraft use the name, but most draw on the Latin “flying” or the animal rather than a demonstrable direct reference to Bode’s figure.

No IAU or major national space mission named simply **Volans** was located. “Volans” does appear in project and instrument names at smaller institutional scales, requiring case-by-case verification.

### Literature, art, and music

Unlike Orion, Argo, or the zodiacal constellations, Volans has little independent canonical literary reception. Most appearances occur in:

- astronomical poetry cataloguing the constellations;
- educational verse and children’s books;
- science fiction using the formal genitive *Volantis*;
- astronomical art based on Bayer, Hevelius, or Bode.

“Volantis” in modern fantasy and gaming is usually an invented place-name exploiting Latin resonance; it is not necessarily an astronomical reference.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. “Bayer invented Volans”

**Verdict: misleading.**

Bayer published the first celestial-atlas depiction in 1603. The figure already existed on the Plancius–Hondius globe of 1597/98 and in the Dutch observational programme. Attribution to Bayer arose because *Uranometria* was more accessible and influential than the globe.

### 2. Keyser versus de Houtman versus Plancius

**Verdict: genuinely unresolved in individual detail.**

- Keyser was the commissioned observer.
- De Houtman was present, later observed independently or revised the catalogue, and printed it.
- Plancius likely organized or finalized the constellation scheme.
- The surviving sources do not say who first drew the fish around these exact stars.

Joint attribution is therefore better than an exclusive claim.

### 3. 1597 or 1598?

**Verdict: bibliographic uncertainty, not two separate inventions.**

The Plancius–Hondius globe is variously dated late 1597 or 1598. “1598” remains standard; “1597/98” is more precise.

### 4. Five or seven original stars?

**Verdict: source/counting problem.**

Some historical summaries say the flying fish had five stars, reflecting a concise constellation count or a particular version of the globe tradition. Modern reconstruction of de Houtman’s printed catalogue counts seven entries. Halley later catalogued eight. Lacaille eventually lettered twelve.

The numbers refer to different documents and counting conventions, not necessarily a single corrupted record.

### 5. Is α Volantis the brightest?

**Verdict: no.**

γ² Volantis is normally the brightest apparent star. Bayer-type letters do not invariably run in brightness order, and Volans’s surviving letters were chiefly systematized by Lacaille, not by Bayer’s original plate.

Sources calling β or α the luminary often use rounded or differing photometric values, confuse “alpha” with “brightest,” or reproduce old data.

### 6. Flamsteed numbers

**Verdict: widespread catalogue error.**

Volans has no genuine Flamsteed numbers. Expressions such as “12 Bayer/Flamsteed stars” conflate two designation systems. Numerals marked “G.” are Gould numbers.

### 7. Did the IAU shorten the name?

**Verdict: not originally.**

John Herschel proposed *Volans* in 1844; Francis Baily adopted it in 1845. The IAU standardized an already established short form in 1922.

### 8. Was Volans nearly abolished?

**Verdict: no evidence found.**

The requested searches for *Volans* with “abolished,” “obsolete,” “dropped,” and related terms found frequent confusion with genuinely obsolete constellations, but no evidence of a serious IAU proposal to abolish Volans. It belonged to the well-established southern set and was retained.

### 9. Dorado or Xiphias?

This controversy properly belongs to the pursuer but affects Volans’s story.

- *Dorado* originally referred to the dolphinfish/mahi-mahi, a large predator of flying fish.
- Some early atlas makers rendered the constellation **Xiphias**, swordfish.
- The change probably reflects zoological or translation confusion rather than two separate constellations.

Calling Dorado simply “a goldfish” is particularly misleading: the tropical dolphinfish is not the domestic carp called a goldfish.

### 10. Is the pursuit a myth?

**Verdict: traditional iconographic reading, not classical myth.**

The maps visibly stage the pursuit; natural dolphinfish prey on flying fish. But no first-hand statement by the constellation’s creators explaining the arrangement was located.

### 11. NGC 2442 versus NGC 2443

**Verdict: duplicate/part identification.**

The supposed “double nebula” became two NGC entries. Modern imaging shows one distorted galaxy; NGC 2443 is generally a duplicate or part of NGC 2442.

### 12. The “Volans stream”

**Verdict: ambiguous phrase.**

The well-established named object is the **Volans–Carina Association**, a young nearby moving group. It may be discussed in literature on disk streams, but it is not a classic thin tidal stream like GD-1 or Palomar 5.

A different “stream” is the Volantid meteoroid stream. The two must not be conflated.

### 13. “No meteor showers”

**Verdict: outdated after 2015.**

Older constellation references correctly reported none at their publication dates. The discovery of the Volantids superseded them.

### 14. Ancient Arabic, Egyptian, or Babylonian names

**Verdict: no evidence found.**

Modern translated names are repeatedly presented online without chronological qualification. None can be traced to the primary corpora. The stars’ declinations make an ancient northern identification improbable.

### 15. Astrological qualities

**Verdict: modern invention.**

No Ptolemaic, Arabic medieval, Renaissance judicial-astrology, Behenian, or mansion-based qualities attach to Volans. Claims such as “gives a quick mind but danger by drowning” derive from recent symbolic astrology unless a specific modern author is cited.

### 16. “Flying” versus gliding

**Verdict: zoological precision, not an etymological error.**

Flying fish glide rather than flap their fins in sustained powered flight. Early-modern sailors nevertheless consistently described them with equivalents of “flying,” “sea swallow,” and *volans*.

### 17. Merriam-Webster’s “first known use 1890”

A dictionary date for English use of the shortened proper noun **Volans** does not date the constellation’s invention. The Latin participle and *Piscis Volans* are documented centuries earlier; Herschel used the shortened astronomical form in 1844.

### Dedicated contested-material search result

Searches in English, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Dutch, and Latin combinations for *Volans/Piscis Volans* with “dispute,” “misidentification,” “etymology,” “hoax,” “obsolete,” “abolished,” “error,” and “controversy” found:

- real disputes over credit and globe dating;
- erroneous Bayer-only attribution;
- confusion over star counts;
- erroneous Flamsteed terminology;
- inconsistent brightest-star claims;
- the NGC 2442/2443 duplicate;
- ambiguity around “Volans stream”;
- outdated claims that no meteor shower exists;
- modern myth and astrology presented without dates.

No substantial Volans-specific hoax, forged ancient source, or serious abolition campaign was located.

## Open questions

1. **Division of authorship.** No surviving log or letter conclusively states which participant first selected the flying-fish outline.

2. **The lost Keyser catalogue.** Keyser’s original working catalogue does not survive independently. It must be reconstructed through Plancius, Hondius, Bayer, de Houtman, and later copies.

3. **Exact globe dating.** Late 1597 versus 1598 remains a production-history question.

4. **Original explanatory text.** No contemporary statement by Keyser, de Houtman, or Plancius explicitly explains why Dorado was placed in pursuit.

5. **Bode’s workshop sources.** Bode clearly inherited Bayer/Hevelius/Fortin iconography, but a plate-by-plate study could further distinguish which anatomical features came from which model.

6. **Indigenous southern traditions.** The absence of Babylonian, Greek, Arabic, or Chinese antecedents does not mean the stars were unnamed to southern African, Australian, Indonesian, Polynesian, or South American peoples. It means no securely sourced equivalence to Volans’s exact star group was established here. Responsible identification would require community-specific ethnoastronomical sources rather than visual guesswork.

7. **Volantid parent body.** The stream is real and recurrent, but its precise parent and long-term encounter history remain under investigation.

8. **Volans–Carina membership.** Gaia-era candidate lists continue to be refined; the association’s outer extent and overlap with neighboring young populations are active research questions.

9. **AM 0644-741’s older nicknames.** “Graham’s Object,” “Das Rheingold,” and “Nibelungen Ring” need a dedicated bibliographic study to establish exactly when each first appeared and whether every use referred to precisely the same object.
