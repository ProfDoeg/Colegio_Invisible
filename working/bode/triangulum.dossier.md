# Triangulum: Research Dossier

> **Status — documented fact:** **Triangulum was retained by the International Astronomical Union.** Its official abbreviation is **Tri**, its Latin genitive **Trianguli**, and its present territory is the 132-square-degree polygon standardized by Eugène Delporte and published in 1930. It is not an abolished constellation.  
> **Related but abolished:** Johannes Hevelius’s adjacent **Triangulum Minus** was abandoned; its principal stars—6 (ι/TZ), 10, and 12 Trianguli—now lie inside official Triangulum.

## Method and evidentiary labels

The following labels distinguish kinds of evidence:

- **Documented fact:** directly attested by a primary text, historical atlas/catalogue, official standard, or modern professional astronomical source.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a reasoned identification or historical inference, not explicit in the source.
- **Tradition:** a claim transmitted in historical literature but not independently demonstrable.
- **Disputed:** supported and challenged in scholarly literature, or dependent on contested identifications.
- **Myth:** a narrative offered by an ancient mythographer or scholiast, recorded as literature rather than physical history.
- **Legend:** a later narrative without contemporary corroboration.
- **Modern invention:** a modern symbolic, occult, national, or popular interpretation.
- **Unverified claim:** a claim encountered in secondary literature for which no adequate primary evidence could be located.

Ancient dates are approximate. “Eratosthenes” below normally means the surviving **Pseudo-Eratosthenic epitome** and related fragments, not necessarily Eratosthenes’ verbatim third-century-BCE text. The transmission problem is substantial: the original *Catasterisms* is lost, and the Epitome and *Fragmenta Vaticana* are independent abridgments of a lost fuller work.

**Documented fact:** *Triangulum* is Latin for “triangle,” from *tri-* “three” and *angulus* “corner, angle.” The genitive *Trianguli* follows the second-declension neuter noun.

**Correction of a frequent popular formulation:** Triangulum is sometimes called a constellation “of the western sky.” It is a northern constellation, prominent in Northern Hemisphere autumn and early winter evenings. “Western” is meaningful only at a particular observing time.

### Greek names

- **Δελτωτόν, Deltōton** — “the delta-shaped [figure].” This is the characteristic name in Aratus and the mythographic tradition. It derives from **δέλτα**, the Greek letter Δ.
- **Τρίγωνον, Trigōnon** — “triangle,” literally “three-angled.”
- The constellation heading in Ptolemy is conventionally transmitted as **Τριγώνου ἀστερισμός**, “constellation of the Triangle.”

**Documented fact:** Aratus, *Phaenomena* 233–238, calls it **Deltoton** and describes three sides, two comparable and a shorter third. He does not attach a myth there. [Aratus, Greek text and Mair translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0483); [English text](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AratusPhaenomena.html).

### Latin names

Historical Latin forms include:

- **Triangulum** — the standard Latin name.
- **Deltoton** and **Deltotum** — Greek transliteration and Latinized form.
- **Triangulus** — occasionally encountered, but *Triangulum* became standard.
- **Sicilia** — a mythographic or allegorical name identifying the figure with Sicily.
- **Trinacria** — “the three-promontoried [island],” a literary name for Sicily; more often an interpretation than a formal catalogue name.
- **Triangulum Septentrionale** or **Boreale** — “Northern Triangle,” used when distinction from Triangulum Australe was desirable.
- **Triangulum Majus** — Hevelius’s name for the old constellation after he placed a smaller triangle beside it.
- **Caput Trianguli** — “Head/Apex of the Triangle,” a Latin positional name for α Trianguli.

### Bode’s labels

**Documented fact:** On plate IV of Johann Elert Bode’s *Uranographia* (Berlin, 1801), the region contains Perseus, Cassiopeia, Andromeda, Honores Friderici, Lacerta, the two triangles, and adjacent figures. Bode retained Hevelius’s distinction between the larger ancient triangle and the smaller southern one. The figures appear under Latin atlas labels, commonly read as **Triangulum Majus** and **Triangulum Minus**, collectively **Triangula** in some tables and later descriptions. [Bode plate-IV collection description](https://www.sunflower-astronomy.com/Books/Bode_Uranographia/Bode_Uranographia.html); [ETH complete atlas](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148).

**Documented fact:** Bode published the atlas and its textual companion separately. The companion was the *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, containing positions for 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. The digital catalogue explicitly describes it as belonging to the *Uranographia*. [Aix-Marseille catalogue](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035).

**Unverified claim:** The brief’s wording “Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be matched to a statement in Bode’s 1801 atlas or companion text. Bode’s documented total is vastly larger. The numbers may refer to a particular digital collection, linked-data “chain,” modern visualization, or subset, rather than Bode’s historical catalogue. It should not be attributed to Bode without identifying that dataset.

### The figure and its stars

The naked-eye geometrical figure is normally drawn from:

- **α Trianguli** — Mothallah; the narrow western/eastern apex depending on map orientation.
- **β Trianguli** — the brightest star.
- **γ Trianguli** — one end of the short base.

Historical catalogues often made the base more articulate by including **δ Trianguli** between or near the relevant base stars. Ptolemy catalogued four rather than merely three stars:

1. the star at the apex;
2. the leading star of three on the base;
3. the middle base star;
4. the following base star.

Modern identifications normally associate these with α, β, δ, and γ Trianguli, although the exact mapping depends on epoch, catalogue reading, and the meanings assigned to “leading” and “following.”

### Present boundary

**Documented fact:** The IAU’s early standardization had two stages:

1. In 1922 the IAU adopted the canonical list and three-letter abbreviations.
2. Delporte subsequently drew unambiguous boundaries along lines of right ascension and declination, using the equinox of **1875.0**; these appeared in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* (1930).

Precession means that the boundary segments are no longer aligned exactly with present-day coordinate lines when expressed in J2000 or later coordinates, but the celestial territory is unchanged.

Triangulum’s modern limits occupy roughly RA 1h31m–2h50m and declination +25.6° to +37.4° in commonly quoted modern coordinates. It borders Andromeda to the north and west, Perseus to the northeast, Aries to the south and east, and Pisces along part of the southwest.

### Boundary history

**Ptolemy, c. 150 CE — documented fact:** Constellations were catalogued figures, not exhaustive bounded territories. Ptolemy assigned four stars to Triangulum and no “unformed” stars to it. Empty sky between figures had no legal ownership.

**Bayer, 1603 — documented fact:** Bayer mapped the ancient triangle and assigned Greek letters. His letter order was based broadly on magnitude classes and figure order, not a rigid brightest-to-faintest ranking. Hence β, not α, is the brightest star. [Royal Society history of Bayer designations](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsnr.2024.0027).

**Hevelius, catalogue dated 1687; atlas 1690 — documented fact:** Hevelius divided the area visually into **Triangulum Majus** and a newly formed **Triangulum Minus** immediately south. The latter consisted chiefly of modern 6/ι, 10, and 12 Trianguli. He did not erase the ancient triangle; he supplemented it.

**Flamsteed, catalogue 1725 and atlas 1729 — documented fact:** Flamsteed’s numbering extended across the regional constellation. Later usage did not sustain a separate little triangle consistently. The numbers 1–14 Trianguli remain positional catalogue designations, although some numbered stars later crossed official boundaries in other constellations.

**Lacaille — negative finding:** Lacaille’s southern reforms did not materially alter northern Triangulum. His **Triangulum Australe** is a separate far-southern constellation and must not be confused with either Triangulum or Hevelius’s Triangulum Minus.

**Bode, 1801 — documented fact:** Bode accepted and pictured Hevelius’s two triangles. His plates show pictorial territories but still not modern legal boundaries.

**Argelander and Heis, nineteenth century — documented fact:** Their atlases emphasized stars and practical observational geometry over elaborate mythological figures. They helped move uranography toward charted point fields, but did not create the present boundaries.

**Delporte, 1930 — documented fact:** All sky positions were assigned uniquely to one of 88 constellations. Triangulum retained the entire ancient core and absorbed the obsolete little triangle. Its modern territorial neighbours became exactly Andromeda, Perseus, Aries, and Pisces.

**What it lost or gained:** The significant gain was **Triangulum Minus**. The Babylonian reconstruction involving γ Andromedae does not represent a Western boundary transfer: γ Andromedae has remained in Andromeda in the classical and modern systems. No major classical Triangulum star was reassigned to a different IAU constellation.

## Stars and objects

## Principal and named stars

### β Trianguli — 4 Trianguli

**Documented fact:** β Tri is the constellation’s brightest naked-eye star, apparent magnitude about 3.0. It is a spectroscopic binary with an orbital period of about 31.4 days and lies roughly 127 light-years away.

**Modern name:** **Alaybasan** was reportedly approved by the IAU Working Group on Star Names in November 2025 for the primary component. Because this is a very recent addition, older reference works correctly list β Tri as having no formal proper name. The current IAU catalogue, rather than pre-2025 books, is controlling. [IAU star-naming project](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/naming_stars/).

**Etymology — modern formal adoption:** *Alaybasan* derives from an Albanian traditional name for the triangular group, associated with Elbasan. It is not a medieval Arabic name and should not be retrojected into Bayer, al-Ṣūfī, or Ptolemy.

### α Trianguli — 2 Trianguli

- Bayer: **α Tri**
- Flamsteed: **2 Tri**
- Formal proper name: **Mothallah**
- Historical forms: Metallah, Methallah, Elmuthalleth, Almutallath, Rasalmothallah, Ras al Muthallath
- Latin positional translation: **Caput Trianguli**

**Documented fact:** α Tri is a close spectroscopic binary/triple system about 63 light-years away, with combined visual magnitude about 3.4. It is classified as an ellipsoidal variable in modern catalogues.

**Etymology:** Arabic **رأس المثلث, raʾs al-muthallath**, “head/apex of the triangle.” The abbreviated **Mothallah** ultimately represents *al-muthallath*, “the triangle,” after repeated transliteration and truncation in European star-name literature. The IAU approved **Mothallah** in 2016 for the visually brighter component. [WGSN Bulletin 1](https://www.iau.org/static/science/scientific_bodies/working_groups/280/WGSN_bulletin1.pdf).

**Contested nomenclature:** Treating *Mothallah* as an immemorial proper name for α is misleading. The Arabic phrase was a positional description or constellation term; European catalogues transformed it into a one-word stellar name.

### γ Trianguli — 9 Trianguli

- Bayer: **γ Tri**
- Flamsteed: **9 Tri**
- Magnitude: approximately 4.0
- Type: rapidly rotating A-type main-sequence star
- Recent formal name: **Apdu**, reportedly approved in 2025.

**Etymology — modern formal adoption:** *Apdu* invokes the Babylonian plough tradition. It is a modern IAU proper-name assignment inspired by ancient Mesopotamian material, not proof that Babylonians individually called γ Trianguli by that name.

### δ Trianguli — 8 Trianguli

- Bayer: **δ Tri**
- Flamsteed: **8 Tri**
- Magnitude: approximately 4.8
- Distance: about 35 light-years
- Physical nature: close pair of Sun-like dwarf stars
- Recent formal name: **Deltoton**, reportedly approved in 2025.

**Modern commemoration:** The name revives the ancient Greek constellation name. The Greeks did not demonstrably use *Deltoton* as this individual star’s proper name.

### ι Trianguli / 6 Trianguli / TZ Trianguli

This multiple system formed the brightest corner of Hevelius’s **Triangulum Minus**.

- Bayer/Flamsteed: historically **ι Tri** and **6 Tri**
- Variable designation: **TZ Tri**
- Type: multiple system; RS Canum Venaticorum/ellipsoidal variability
- Recent formal name: **Triminus**, reportedly approved in 2025 in memory of the obsolete constellation.

The coexistence of ι and 6 illustrates that Bayer letters and Flamsteed numbers belong to independent systems.

### Named exoplanet-hosting and notable stars

- **HD 13189:** an evolved giant with a massive substellar companion whose mass lies near the planet/brown-dwarf boundary. This is a classification issue, not evidence that Triangulum has a uniquely defined “first planet.”
- **HD 9446:** hosts multiple planets.
- **XO-3:** hosts the massive transiting object XO-3 b.
- **Kepler and later survey targets:** many faint planetary systems lie within the official polygon; constellation membership is positional.

## Internal asterisms

### The principal Triangle

**Documented fact:** α, β, and γ form the conspicuous elongated triangle. If δ is included, the base becomes a short chain rather than a single geometrical edge.

### Triangulum Minus

**Documented fact:** Hevelius’s little triangle used modern 6/ι, 10, and 12 Tri. It is no longer a constellation. A separate dossier would be appropriate, but its history cannot be excluded because Bode deliberately pictured it.

### Informal modern patterns

Occasional observing guides use names such as “the Triangulum diamond” for β–γ–δ–7 Tri or include additional faint stars in a kite. These are modern informal asterisms and have no IAU standing.

## Messier and NGC objects

### M33 = NGC 598 — the Triangulum Galaxy

**Documented fact:** M33 is a nearly face-on spiral galaxy and a principal member of the Local Group. It is called the **Triangulum Galaxy** because it lies in the constellation, not because the galaxy itself is triangular.

- Probably seen by earlier observers, but securely catalogued by Charles Messier on **25 August 1764**.
- William Herschel resolved parts of it into stars and separately catalogued bright H II regions.
- John Herschel and J. L. E. Dreyer incorporated it and its nebulae into the General Catalogue and NGC traditions.
- Modern distance: approximately **2.7 million light-years**, with method-dependent values near 800–850 kpc.
- It is smaller than the Milky Way and M31 but is conventionally called the Local Group’s third-largest spiral.

[NASA’s M33 account](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubble-messier-catalog/messier-33/).

### Hubble’s 1926 distance and the “distance debate”

**Documented fact:** In “A Spiral Nebula as a Stellar System, Messier 33,” Edwin Hubble identified Cepheids and other variables in M33 and obtained:

- distance modulus **m − M = 22.1**;
- distance **263,000 parsecs**;
- approximately **850,000 light-years**.

His preliminary value had been about 930,000 light-years. Both were serious underestimates by modern standards, chiefly because the Cepheid scale’s zero point and stellar-population distinctions were not yet adequately calibrated. Hubble openly identified the zero point as the critical uncertainty. [Hubble 1926, pp. 253–254](https://articles.adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1926ApJ....63..236H).

**Historical significance:** The estimate was still far beyond contemporary small estimates of the Milky Way’s extent and supported M33’s status as an independent stellar system. It was not the sole event that “proved galaxies exist”; it belonged to a cumulative debate involving novae, Cepheids, radial velocities, stellar populations, and the earlier M31 work.

**Later correction:** Walter Baade’s recognition of distinct stellar populations and revision of the Cepheid calibration approximately doubled the extragalactic distance scale. Modern HST, eclipsing-binary, tip-of-the-red-giant-branch, JAGB, maser, and Cepheid studies continue to refine M33’s distance. A recent HST Cepheid analysis reports about 1.3% internal precision, although systematic calibration differences remain. [Recent M33 Cepheid study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00037).

### Nebulae and star-forming regions inside M33

These are extragalactic H II regions but have their own NGC identities:

- **NGC 604:** exceptionally large luminous star-forming region; roughly 1,500 light-years across in common estimates and containing hundreds of massive young stars.
- **NGC 595:** another major H II complex.
- **NGC 588:** bright H II region.
- **NGC 592:** H II region/star-forming complex.
- **IC 132, IC 133, IC 135, IC 136, IC 137, IC 139, IC 140, IC 142:** catalogued luminous regions associated with M33.

Hubble explicitly discussed NGC 588, 595, and 604 as diffuse nebulae with emission spectra in his 1926 study.

### Other galaxies within official Triangulum

- **NGC 672:** barred spiral galaxy interacting with IC 1727.
- **IC 1727:** Magellanic/barred irregular companion of NGC 672.
- **NGC 784:** nearby dwarf/irregular or late-type galaxy.
- **NGC 925:** barred spiral and member of the NGC 1023 group in many classifications.
- **NGC 890:** lenticular galaxy.
- Numerous much fainter PGC, UGC, 2MASS, and survey galaxies occupy the boundary.

### Quasars and high-energy objects

**Documented fact:** **3C 48**, within Triangulum’s official territory, was one of the first radio sources recognized as a quasar. Its initially puzzling spectrum and later redshift interpretation were central to the early-1960s quasar revolution. Statements calling it simply “the first quasar” require qualification: 3C 273 was the first whose redshift was successfully recognized, while 3C 48 had been observed and identified optically earlier.

## Variable stars

Prominent Galactic variables include:

- **R Trianguli:** long-period Mira variable, a red giant with a large visual amplitude.
- **V Trianguli:** eclipsing β Lyrae-type system.
- **TZ Trianguli / ι or 6 Tri:** spotted, ellipsoidal/RS CVn multiple system.
- **RW Trianguli:** nova-like cataclysmic variable containing an accreting white dwarf.
- **TX Trianguli, X Trianguli, and numerous survey variables:** eclipsing, pulsating, or rotational variables.
- **BG Trianguli:** nova-like cataclysmic variable studied for superhumps and accretion-disc behaviour. [Modern BG Tri study](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/516/2/2775/6673446).

Inside M33, historically important variables include Cepheids measured by Hubble and luminous blue variables such as **Var B** and **Var C**. M33’s variable-star population is too large for an exhaustive object-by-object catalogue here.

## Novae and supernovae

### Galactic novae

No famous naked-eye historical Galactic nova is securely assigned to Triangulum. Cataclysmic variables in the constellation undergo dwarf-nova or nova-like behaviour, but those terms should not be confused with a recorded classical “new star.”

### M33 novae

Hubble’s 1926 plates and historical plate searches recorded multiple novae in M33, including objects from 1919 and 1925. Modern surveys regularly discover additional M33 novae. These are novae **in the galaxy M33**, not Milky Way novae merely projected against Triangulum.

### M33 supernovae

No historically conspicuous naked-eye supernova is recorded in the constellation. M33 contains many supernova remnants, with roughly a hundred candidates known by the early twenty-first century. Their presence records past explosions, most of which were not individually observed from Earth. [Harvard–Smithsonian discussion](https://www.cfa.harvard.edu/news/supernovae-triangulum).

**Correction:** Some popular pages have described “SN 2008a” as occurring in M33. SN 2008A was not an M33 supernova; this appears to be a confusion with another galaxy or with an illustrated transient. It should not be repeated without a CBAT/TNS designation tied to M33.

## Meteor showers

**Negative finding:** No major currently established annual meteor shower is officially named for or securely associated with Triangulum. Older radiant lists contain weak or provisional “Triangulid” radiants, and automated video surveys can produce transient radiant groupings in this area, but these are not equivalent to a well-established shower such as the Perseids or Geminids. Claims that Triangulum has “no meteor showers” are acceptable at popular-guide level but overstate the negative if minor historical radiants are included.

## Chronology of attestation

### c. 1000–700 BCE: Babylonian *MUL.APIN*

**Documented text; disputed stellar reconstruction:** The Babylonian compendium conventionally called *MUL.APIN*, compiled perhaps around 1000 BCE and surviving in first-millennium copies, begins with **MUL.APIN**, “the Plough.” Modern Assyriologists have variously identified it with:

- α and β Trianguli, possibly γ Trianguli, plus γ Andromedae;
- much of Triangulum plus γ Andromedae;
- parts of Andromeda or even Cassiopeia in alternative reconstructions.

Hunger and Pingree’s often-cited reconstruction uses α and β Tri, perhaps γ Tri, and γ And. The identification is therefore a strong scholarly proposal, not a direct Babylonian label saying “these are the modern Triangulum stars.” [Survey reproducing the Hunger–Pingree identification](https://files.core.ac.uk/download/pdf/37326531.pdf); [summary of competing identifications](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/APIN).

The “Wolf, seeder of the Plough” has sometimes been identified with α Trianguli, but other reconstructions place it differently. This is too uncertain to present as an ancient proper name for Mothallah.

### Egyptian antecedents

**Negative finding:** No native Egyptian constellation can presently be identified securely with α–β–γ Trianguli. The later equation of the Greek triangle with Egypt or the Nile Delta is a Greek/Roman geographical interpretation, not evidence that pharaonic Egyptians recognized these stars as their Delta.

Egyptian astronomical ceilings and diagonal-star clocks preserve other systems. Their stellar identifications are often difficult, and no credible continuous line from a named Egyptian asterism to Triangulum was found.

### Late fifth or fourth century BCE: Eudoxan constellation tradition

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Aratus’s descriptive astronomy drew substantially on Eudoxus of Cnidus. Since Aratus treats Deltoton as an already recognized sign, many histories place it in the Eudoxan celestial sphere. Eudoxus’s relevant works survive only fragmentarily; consequently, “Eudoxus invented Triangulum” is not demonstrated.

### c. 275 BCE: Aratus

**First secure surviving Greek literary attestation:** *Phaenomena* 233–238 describes **Deltoton** below Andromeda and north of Aries. Two sides are comparable, the third shorter, and the sign is easy to recognize because one of its stars is conspicuous. No Sicily, Demeter, Nile, Hermes, or Zeus myth appears in Aratus’s own lines.

### c. 200–120 BCE: Hipparchus

**Documented tradition:** Hipparchus discussed Aratus’s constellation descriptions in his *Commentary on the Phaenomena of Eudoxus and Aratus*. He criticized geometrical and positional inaccuracies in the poetic sky. The ancient triangle was therefore established before Ptolemy.

### Hellenistic/early Roman period: Pseudo-Eratosthenic *Catasterisms*

**Mythographic tradition:** Chapter 20, **Deltoton**, transmits interpretations connecting the sign with the Greek delta, Zeus, Egypt/Nile, and Sicily. Because the surviving text is an epitome of uncertain date and authorship, a story present there cannot automatically be dated verbatim to Eratosthenes c. 250 BCE. [Textual-history review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2017/2017.02.48/).

### c. 10 CE: Manilius

**Documented fact:** Manilius, *Astronomica* 1.350–355, describes a three-star sign with two equal sides and one unequal, called Deltoton from its likeness. He offers geometry rather than a narrative catasterism.

### Early first century CE: Germanicus

**Documented literary elaboration:** Germanicus, *Aratea* around lines 230–240, expands Aratus by connecting Deltoton with the Nile’s rich waters. This is an important early Latin witness to the Egypt/Delta interpretation. [Germanicus digital text](https://ivanadob.github.io/aratea-data/text__germanicus.html).

### First or second century CE: Hyginus

**Mythographic tradition:** *De astronomia* or *Poetica astronomica* 2.19 supplies the fullest surviving compact list: Greek-letter shape; placement by Mercury/Hermes above Aries; first letter of Zeus’s name; Egypt; Egypt and Ethiopia divided by the Nile; and Sicily. [Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/hyginus/hyginus2.shtml); [English translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HyginusAstronomica2.html).

### c. 150 CE: Ptolemy

**Documented fact:** Ptolemy included **Trigōnon/Triangulum** among the 21 northern constellations and catalogued four stars. Ptolemy’s catalogue is positional and anatomical/geometrical, not a myth handbook. [Modern atlas keyed to the *Almagest*](https://tjohncan.github.io/star-schema/); [Peters and Knobel’s historical edition](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/Montignot%20Knobel%20Ptolemy%27s%20cat%201915.pdf).

### c. 150 CE: Farnese Atlas

**Negative iconographic finding:** Although contemporary in date with Ptolemy and preserving many classical constellations, the Farnese globe does **not** clearly depict Triangulum. Bradley Schaefer’s analysis lists Triangulum among the small ancient figures omitted without an obvious physical obstruction. [Schaefer’s study](https://www.phys.lsu.edu/farnese/JHAFarneseProofs.htm).

### Ninth-century Aratea manuscripts

Carolingian manuscripts such as Leiden Voss. lat. Q.79, Harley 647, and Basel AN IV 18 transmit Latin Aratean and Hyginian material with constellation illustrations or text-figures. Triangulum is typically a plain geometrical triangle, sometimes with dots at its vertices, rather than an anthropomorphic figure.

### c. 964: al-Ṣūfī

**Documented fact:** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābitah* retained Ptolemy’s figure as **Ṣūrat al-Muthallath**, “the Figure of the Triangle.” He catalogued four stars:

1. star at the apex, magnitude 3;
2. leading base star, magnitude 3;
3. middle base star, magnitude 5;
4. following base star, approximately magnitude 3.

[Al-Ṣūfī catalogue and translated table](https://www.astrologie-persane.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Al-Sufi-Hafez-Ihsan.pdf).

### 1009–1010: Oxford, Bodleian, Marsh 144

**Documented iconography:** The manuscript contains paired sky-view and globe-view diagrams of al-Ṣūfī’s constellations. Its triangular outline and stars are among the earliest securely dated surviving Islamic depictions. [Bodleian image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABook_of_the_Fixed_Stars_Auv0181_Triangulum.jpg).

### Medieval Latin period

Arabic *al-muthallath* entered Latin tables in many spellings. Translators and copyists produced forms including *Almutallath*, *Almutaleh*, *Mothallah*, *Methallah*, and *Elmuthalleth*. These are transliteration variants, not necessarily different indigenous names.

### 1515: Dürer–Stabius maps

**Documented fact:** Albrecht Dürer’s workshop engraved the northern and southern celestial maps designed with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel. Triangulum appears as the classical geometrical sign. The maps derive from the Ptolemaic/medieval astronomical tradition and place the figures as seen from outside the celestial sphere.

### 1540: Piccolomini

Alessandro Piccolomini’s *De le stelle fisse* represented constellations schematically and labelled stars with Roman letters. It was an important predecessor to Bayer’s systematic stellar lettering.

### 1551: Mercator

Gerardus Mercator’s celestial globe shows the triangle near Perseus, Andromeda, and Aries, maintaining the ancient figure.

### 1603: Bayer

**Documented fact:** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* gave the constellation its influential early-modern chart and Bayer letters. Bayer did not invent Triangulum. [RAS Bayer exhibit](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-ras/treasures-ras-uranometria-johann-bayer).

### 1627: Julius Schiller

**Documented fact:** Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, produced with Bayer’s assistance, replaced pagan/classical figures with Christian ones. Triangulum became the **Mitra Pontificalis Sancti Petri**, the pontifical mitre of Saint Peter. [ETH digitization](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720); [map record](https://www.geographicus.com/P/AntiqueMap/mitraepontificalisspetri-schiller-1627).

The nearby Aries was separately Saint Peter, Prince of the Apostles. Thus the triangle was his **mitre**, not Saint Peter himself.

### 1675: Sherburne

Edward Sherburne’s English treatment of Manilius helped spread forms such as **Mothallah** in English antiquarian astronomy. Some later sources blur whether he treated it as a constellation name or individual stellar name.

### 1687/1690: Hevelius

**Documented fact:** Hevelius introduced **Triangulum Minus**, renaming the old figure **Triangulum Majus**. Both appear with Musca on the relevant plate of the posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*. [Hevelius plate collection](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category%3AFirmamentum_Sobiescianum%2C_sive_uranographia).

### 1725–1729: Flamsteed

Flamsteed catalogued and numbered stars in the region. His observational authority helped stabilize the larger constellation while the little triangle gradually lost independent use.

### 1764: Messier

Messier catalogued M33. Its later popular name “Triangulum Galaxy” is positional and considerably later than the Messier designation.

### 1782 and 1801: Bode

Bode’s earlier popular atlas, *Vorstellung der Gestirne* (1782), and monumental *Uranographia* (1801) transmitted the classical figure to a broad German and European audience. In 1801 he retained the Hevelian large/small distinction and included dense telescopic star and nebula fields.

### 1822: Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* adopted the richly pictorial post-Bode style. Triangulum and its smaller neighbour appear in the Andromeda/Gloria Frederici region.

### 1824–1825: *Urania’s Mirror*

Card 5 contains **Gloria Frederici, Andromeda, and Triangula**. Its images closely copy Jamieson, with pinholes permitting the stars to shine when held against light. The long attribution to “a lady” was overturned by Peter Hingley’s evidence for the Rev. Richard Rouse Bloxam. [Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2013645050/); [history](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/uranias-mirror-or-a-view-of-the-heavens/).

### 1835: Burritt

Elijah H. Burritt’s *Geography of the Heavens* and accompanying atlas carried classical constellation lore into American schools. Triangulum survived; the increasingly obsolete additions were treated inconsistently between editions.

### 1843: Argelander

The *Uranometria Nova* charted naked-eye stars with restrained line work. Its scientific style contributed to the withdrawal of extravagant figural boundaries but did not abolish Triangulum.

### 1872: Heis

Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* continued the observational, star-centred tradition. Triangulum remained as a conventional constellation label and asterism.

### 1922: IAU

**Documented fact:** Triangulum was included among the 88 standardized constellations, with abbreviation **Tri**. The IAU did not separately retain Triangulum Minus.

### 1930: Delporte

**Documented fact:** Delporte’s boundary publication fixed Triangulum’s official territory. The old triangle’s recognition ceased to depend on pictorial outlines; every object inside the polygon thereafter belonged positionally to Triangulum.

### 2016–2025: formal star names

The IAU Working Group on Star Names formalized **Mothallah** in 2016. A 2025 naming round reportedly added **Alaybasan, Apdu, Deltoton,** and **Triminus**, deliberately representing Albanian, Babylonian, Greek, and obsolete-constellation heritage. These are modern assignments to particular stellar components.

## Classical myths and legends

Triangulum is an object-shaped constellation. It therefore has no biographical myth-cycle comparable to Perseus, Orion, or Heracles. Its surviving “myths” are explanations of its geometry and placement.

### The Greek letter delta

**Earliest secure appearance: Aratus, c. 275 BCE.**

**Documented literary fact:** Aratus calls the sign **Deltoton**, “delta-shaped.” This is an observational comparison rather than a catasterism: the three stars resemble Δ.

**Later transmission:** Manilius, Germanicus, the Pseudo-Eratosthenic corpus, Hyginus, scholiasts, medieval compilations, and Renaissance atlases repeated it.

### Hermes places the triangle above Aries

**Myth; first extant explicit witness: Pseudo-Eratosthenic/Hyginian tradition, surviving in early Imperial form.**

Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.19, says **Mercury**—Greek Hermes—was thought to have placed the triangle above Aries so that its conspicuousness could mark the otherwise faint Ram. The astronomical rationale corresponds to Aratus: Aries is difficult to trace, whereas Deltoton helps locate it.

This is the closest Triangulum comes to a conventional catasterism with a named placer. Hermes is the actor; the triangle is placed as a celestial marker.

### The first letter of Zeus’s name

**Mythographic/aetiological tradition:** Hyginus adds that the triangle forms the first letter in the name of Jupiter/Zeus, explaining the Greek form through **Διός, Dios**, the genitive of Zeus.

This version is sometimes paraphrased as “Zeus placed his initial in heaven.” That is not what Hyginus’s wording clearly says: Mercury performs the placement. The letter represents Zeus’s name.

**Contested philological presentation:** Calling Δ “the first letter of Zeus” is only intelligible through the oblique stem **Di-/Dios**, not the nominative **Zeus**. Popular retellings often omit this grammatical fact.

### Egypt or the Nile Delta

**Tradition; early Latin attestation in Germanicus, with mythographic elaboration in Hyginus.**

Germanicus describes the sign as a splendid gift or image generated by the Nile’s rich waters. Hyginus records:

1. that it represents the shape of Egypt;
2. that it represents Ethiopia and Egypt, with the Nile marking their boundary.

The stories are geographical allegories, not demonstrably native Egyptian myths. The resemblance between a triangle and the Nile Delta made the association attractive after the Greek name *delta* had itself become a geographical term.

### Sicily / Trinacria

**Mythographic tradition:** Hyginus concludes that others believed Sicily was represented. Sicily’s three capes led to the name **Trinacria**, “three-promontoried.”

### Sicily placed by Zeus at Demeter’s request

**Later-developed mythic form:** Later star-lore literature states that **Demeter/Ceres**, Sicily’s patron goddess, asked Zeus/Jupiter to place the island’s triangular image among the stars.

The compact extant Hyginus text available here says only that some think Sicily is pictured; it does not contain the full request scene. The fuller Demeter-at-Zeus narrative is associated in modern handbooks with the Pseudo-Eratosthenic/scholiastic tradition, but its exact wording and manuscript locus require caution.

**Finding:** The broad Sicily identification is ancient and secure. The dramatized formulation “Zeus placed Sicily in heaven at Demeter’s request” is a transmitted mythographic expansion, not securely traceable to Aratus and not present in the accessible compact text of Hyginus 2.19.

### Threefold division of the cosmos

**Tradition, weakly sourced:** Some later summaries claim that its three angles commemorate the division of the universe among Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades. This motif fits standard Greek myth, especially the division by lot after the Titanomachy, but it is absent from the accessible Aratus and Hyginus passages examined here.

**Assessment:** Ancient or medieval scholia may have joined the common “three divine brothers” motif to Deltoton, but modern websites often repeat it without edition, line, or manuscript. It remains **unverified as a Triangulum catasterism** in the sources consulted.

### Alleged links to Demeter’s wider myths

Demeter’s search for Persephone, her famine, her reception at Eleusis, and her gift of agriculture are narrated in the *Homeric Hymn to Demeter*, Apollodorus, Ovid, and later mythographers. Sicily also belongs to some versions of Persephone’s abduction.

**Important limitation:** Those myths concern Demeter or Sicily; they do not thereby become myths of the constellation. No ancient source located says that the stellar triangle acts in the abduction, search, Eleusinian episode, Triptolemus cycle, or Gigantomachy.

### Ovid

**Negative finding:** Ovid uses Sicily, Trinacria, Ceres, Proserpina, and triangular geography extensively, especially in *Metamorphoses* 5 and *Fasti* 4. No secure passage was found in which he identifies Deltoton/Triangulum as the stellar Sicily or narrates its catasterism.

### Pausanias

**Negative finding:** Pausanias records numerous local traditions about Demeter, Zeus, Hermes, and regional cults. No Triangulum catasterism was traced in the *Description of Greece*.

### Pseudo-Apollodorus

**Negative finding:** The *Bibliotheca* supplies the division of cosmic sovereignty and Demeter–Persephone mythology, but no explicit catasterism of Deltoton was located.

### Nonnus

**Negative finding:** Nonnus uses elaborate astral imagery and narrates Sicilian and divine myths, but no specific Triangulum catasterism was traced in the *Dionysiaca*.

### The *Fabulae* of Hyginus

**Negative distinction:** The relevant compact constellation account is in *Astronomica* 2.19, not one of the standard numbered *Fabulae*. Later citations saying merely “Hyginus” sometimes obscure which work is meant.

### Scholia to Aratus and Germanicus

**Documented transmission:** The scholia preserve and rearrange mythographic interpretations around Deltoton, including its divine-letter and geographical meanings. Because scholia are layered compilations copied and expanded across centuries, individual statements must be dated to manuscript or recension rather than automatically to Aratus.

The Basel, Bern, Sangermanensis, and Strozzianus scholia are not a single authorial work. The ninth-century Basel AN IV 18 manuscript is an important physical witness. [Basel manuscript description](https://ivanadob.github.io/aratea-data/desc__basle_bb_an_iv_18.html).

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Babylonian

### MUL.APIN, the Plough

**Documented name; disputed identification:** **MUL.APIN** means “Plough.” It heads the Babylonian compendium bearing the same modern title and belongs to the Path of Enlil.

The common reconstruction makes Triangulum the plough and γ Andromedae an associated human, handle, or component. Different scholars distribute the stars differently. It is therefore legitimate to call MUL.APIN a **Babylonian antecedent in substantially the same region**, but not a proven direct ancestor of Greek Deltoton.

No surviving evidence demonstrates that Greeks translated *APIN* into “triangle.” The resemblance could be genealogical, a remapping, or coincidence within a borrowed celestial framework.

## Arabic and Persian

### The Greco-Arabic constellation

Al-Ṣūfī’s **al-Muthallath** is a translation of the Greek geometrical figure within the Ptolemaic system. It is not an indigenous Bedouin animal or implement.

### Bedouin asterisms

**Negative finding:** Al-Ṣūfī normally supplies indigenous Arabic names where they exist, but no strong independent Bedouin asterism confined to the four Ptolemaic Triangulum stars emerged from the text examined. The most durable Arabic expression here is the translated **al-Muthallath** and the positional **raʾs al-muthallath**.

Claims that Mothallah is an ancient Bedouin personal star name should therefore be treated cautiously.

### Persian

Al-Ṣūfī was Persian, but wrote his astronomical work in Arabic. Persian **Mosallas** is the ordinary learned word for triangle, borrowed from Arabic. No separate pre-Islamic Iranian constellation securely matching these exact stars was located.

## Chinese

**Documented fact:** Modern Triangulum lies within the traditional western quadrant, the **White Tiger of the West**, and overlaps the lunar mansions **Kui** and **Lou**.

Traditional Chinese stars in the area belonged chiefly to:

- **Tiān Dàjiāngjūn, 天大將軍**, “Celestial Great General,” an 11- or 12-star asterism spanning parts of Andromeda, Perseus, and Triangulum. Reconstructions commonly include β, γ, and δ Trianguli.
- Additional or supplementary stars of the **Lou** and **Kui** mansions.
- A star associated in some maps with **Tiānhùn**, the “Celestial Pigsty.”

Modern Chinese **三角座** is a translation of the Western constellation, not the ancient Chinese asterism’s name. [Chinese-region table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangulum_in_Chinese_astronomy); [Tiān Dàjiāngjūn history](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Tiandajiangjun).

## Indian astronomy

Triangulum lies north of the ecliptic and does not itself define one of the classical 27 or 28 nakshatras. Its ecliptic longitudes place it broadly above the Aśvinī/Bharaṇī sector, depending on epoch and star, but physical proximity on a flat chart does not make its stars nakshatra markers.

**Negative finding:** No ancient Sanskrit constellation exactly matching α–β–γ Tri was securely identified. Modern Indian-language names meaning “triangle” are translations of the Western IAU figure.

## Hebrew and Jewish traditions

Modern Hebrew **Meshullash** is descriptive. Medieval Jewish astronomers working with Arabic scientific texts could transliterate or translate *al-muthallath*, but no independent biblical constellation securely corresponding to Triangulum was found.

### Kabbalistic mappings

**Unverified/modern invention:** Modern esoteric charts sometimes associate a celestial triangle with the three supernal sefirot, the patriarchs, the three letters of a divine name, or the “three pillars” of the Tree of Life. No canonical *Sefer Yetzirah*, *Bahir*, or *Zohar* passage was found identifying α–β–γ Trianguli with such a triad.

## Christianized sky

**Documented fact:** Schiller made it the **Pontifical Mitre of Saint Peter** in 1627. This was a deliberate confessional remapping intended to remove pagan images from the sky.

### Trinity readings

**Tradition/modern symbolic reading:** Any triangle readily suggests the Christian Trinity. Early-modern Christian writers sometimes used astronomical triangles as Trinitarian emblems.

**Finding:** Schiller’s explicit replacement was the **mitre**, not “the Holy Trinity.” General Trinity symbolism should not be presented as Triangulum’s official medieval Christian identity unless a particular author and edition are cited.

## Alchemical and Hermetic readings

Triangles have extensive elemental meanings in alchemy:

- upward triangle: fire;
- downward triangle: water;
- barred variants: air and earth;
- interpenetrating triangles: conjunction of opposites.

Hermetic, Masonic, and occult traditions also use triangles for deity, equilibrium, manifestation, or threefold cosmologies.

**Negative finding:** No early alchemical authority examined identifies the actual constellation Triangulum with a specific operation, element, or stage of the *opus*. Modern occult authors often transfer generic triangular symbolism to the constellation. That is a **modern symbolic application**, not demonstrated ancient star lore.

## Astrology

### Ptolemaic fixed-star nature

**Documented astrological doctrine:** In *Tetrabiblos* 1.9, Ptolemy assigns stars of Triangulum a planetary temperament like **Mercury**. This is qualitative judicial astrology, not modern astrophysics.

Later astrologers extrapolated interests in calculation, architecture, law, geometry, rhetoric, or masonry. Those occupational interpretations are later traditions, not Ptolemy’s explicit list.

### Behenian stars

**Negative finding:** None of Triangulum’s principal stars belongs to the standard medieval list of fifteen Behenian fixed stars. Claims otherwise probably confuse generic magical triangles with the constellation.

### Lunar mansions

- **Chinese:** the territory overlaps the Lou/Kui mansion region.
- **Arabic/Indian:** the stars are north of the zodiacal mansion belt and are not standard defining mansion stars.
- Later astrologers can project mansion longitude bands northward across them, but that is a coordinate extension, not proof of membership in an ancient asterism.

## Iconography

### Kugel Globe, first century BCE

**Scholarly identification:** A small triangular figure on the Kugel Globe has recently been proposed as the earliest surviving depiction of Triangulum. The identification is plausible but newer and less secure than the textual attestations.

### Farnese Atlas, Naples

**Negative finding:** Triangulum is not securely depicted. It is one of the small ancient constellations omitted from the globe. Any modern diagram claiming to show it on the Farnese Atlas should identify the carving explicitly; otherwise it likely projects a modern outline onto an absent figure.

### Carolingian manuscripts

- **Leiden Aratea**, Leiden University Library, Voss. lat. Q.79, c. 816: geometrical constellation images in an illuminated Germanicus tradition.
- **Harley 647**, British Library, ninth century: “figured poem” constellation outlines formed partly from text.
- **Basel AN IV 18**, probably Fulda, first third of ninth century: Germanicus and scholia.
- **NLW 735C**, around 1000: constellation cycle in the Germanicus tradition.

Triangulum normally appears as a simple outlined triangle with three or four stars. Manuscript reversals reflect celestial-globe versus terrestrial-observer viewpoints.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

- **Bodleian Marsh 144, 1009–1010:** paired sky and globe views.
- Later Persian and Islamic manuscripts, including fifteenth-century copies, elaborate the triangle decoratively but retain the Ptolemaic geometry.
- The mirrored pair is methodological: one figure shows the constellation as seen in the sky, the other as represented on a globe.

### Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

The Dürer–Stabius–Heinfogel northern map reduces Triangulum to an outlined geometrical sign among larger mythic bodies. Unlike an animal or hero, it required no anatomical orientation.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer’s plate fixes the early-modern stellar lettering and presents Triangulum in the Ptolemaic neighbourhood. It remains a geometric triangle rather than a mythic scene.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

The triangle becomes a richly ornamented papal/episcopal **mitre of Saint Peter**. The map is reversed in the globe tradition, described as the divine or exterior view.

### Hevelius, Danzig, 1690

Hevelius engraves **Triangulum Majus** and **Triangulum Minus** as adjacent outlined forms. His plate is central to the small triangle’s short-lived independent identity.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s plate IV is an extremely dense engraved star field. Both triangles appear beside Andromeda and below Perseus, with Musca Borealis and Honores Friderici nearby. The visual hierarchy embodies the late-eighteenth-century sky’s mixture of ancient, Hevelian, dynastic, and telescopic figures.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson inherited Bode-like pictorial composition while simplifying it for a smaller atlas. The triangles remain minor geometric signs beside the more spectacular Andromeda scene.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/1825

Card 5 presents **Gloria Frederici, Andromeda, and Triangula**. Perforations mark stars. The cards copied Jamieson closely and were marketed as an elegant educational device.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

**Negative finding:** No ancient coin type, classical monumental ceiling, or cathedral portal could be securely identified as depicting Triangulum specifically rather than a generic triangle, Sicily/Trinacria, Trinity emblem, or geometric ornament.

Sicilian **triskeles/Trinacria** coinage is related to Sicily’s three-cape symbolism, but it is not by itself an image of the constellation. Likewise, a triangular Trinity emblem on a church ceiling is not Triangulum unless accompanied by a star map or inscription.

## People, events, and works

### Astronomers and observers

- **Aratus:** earliest secure surviving literary description.
- **Hipparchus:** criticized and refined Aratean positional astronomy.
- **Ptolemy:** gave the canonical four-star catalogue.
- **al-Ṣūfī:** re-observed and transmitted the figure in Arabic.
- **Bayer:** supplied enduring letter designations.
- **Schiller:** Christianized it as Saint Peter’s mitre.
- **Hevelius:** introduced Triangulum Minus.
- **Flamsteed:** supplied enduring numbers.
- **Bode:** gave the region its grandest late-classical atlas treatment.
- **Messier:** catalogued M33.
- **William Herschel:** resolved stellar and nebular structure in M33 and catalogued bright regions.
- **John Herschel and Dreyer:** consolidated NGC nomenclature.
- **John Duncan and Max Wolf:** early twentieth-century M33 variable-star observations used by Hubble.
- **Edwin Hubble:** measured M33 Cepheids and established its scale as an external stellar system.
- **Walter Baade:** population distinctions corrected the extragalactic distance scale.
- **Eugène Delporte:** fixed official boundaries.

### Navigation

Triangulum’s stars are not first-magnitude navigational stars and are absent from standard modern nautical-navigation star lists. They can assist local star-hopping near Aries and Andromeda, but claims that famous explorers steered oceanic voyages by Triangulum could not be substantiated.

### Ceres and Sicily

A seductive coincidence links the Sicily tradition with **Giuseppe Piazzi’s discovery of Ceres at Palermo on 1 January 1801**. Ceres was discovered in Taurus, not in Triangulum. Therefore statements implying that Piazzi found Ceres “in the Sicilian constellation” are false or misleading. The coincidence is thematic—Ceres/Demeter, Sicily, and 1801—not positional.

### Literature and visual culture

Triangulum rarely appears as an independent classical literary personality because it lacks a hero or creature. Modern science fiction uses “Triangulum” primarily for M33 or an intergalactic setting.

Examples include:

- works titled after the **Triangulum Galaxy**;
- astronomical photography collections and music inspired by M33;
- fictional ships, factions, or missions named *Triangulum*.

These uses ordinarily refer to the galaxy or geometric symbol, not directly to the classical constellation.

### Ships, spacecraft, and missions

**Negative finding:** No major historical exploration ship was found whose name demonstrably derives from the constellation rather than the triangle generally.

No dedicated spacecraft has yet visited M33; it is far outside feasible mission distances. Hubble, Chandra, Spitzer, GALEX, JWST, ground-based surveys, and radio arrays have observed it remotely. “Triangulum mission” names encountered in speculative proposals or fiction should not be mistaken for launched intergalactic missions.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was Triangulum Babylonian?

**Disputed scholarly reconstruction.**

The Babylonian **MUL.APIN** certainly existed, and several respected reconstructions put much of it in modern Triangulum. But the cuneiform text does not supply modern catalogue designations, and proposals range across Triangulum, Andromeda, and Cassiopeia.

**Conclusion:** “A Babylonian antecedent probably occupied these stars” is defensible. “The Babylonians called Triangulum the Plough” is too categorical.

## 2. Was it Egyptian?

**Ancient tradition, but not an Egyptian antecedent.**

Germanicus and Hyginus associate it with Egypt or the Nile. No native Egyptian stellar figure in these stars has been demonstrated.

**Conclusion:** The Nile Delta is an ancient Greek/Roman interpretation of the Greek triangle, not currently evidence for pharaonic origin.

## 3. Did Eratosthenes write the surviving chapter?

**Disputed authorship/transmission.**

The original *Catasterisms* does not survive intact. Modern scholarship distinguishes the lost Eratosthenic work from the Epitome and fragments.

**Conclusion:** Cite “Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterisms* 20” or “the Eratosthenic tradition,” unless discussing reconstruction of the lost original.

## 4. Did Zeus or Hermes place it?

The extant Hyginian account assigns placement to **Mercury/Hermes**, while the shape supplies the initial of **Dios/Zeus**. Sicily-at-Demeter’s-request versions make Zeus the granter or placer.

**Conclusion:** These are variant narratives, not a single consistent catasterism. Popular summaries often combine them.

## 5. Does Δ begin “Zeus”?

**Philological dispute generated by oversimplification.**

The nominative Ζεύς begins with zeta; the genitive Διός and related stem begin with delta. Hyginus’s Latin explanation depends on the Greek inflected name.

**Conclusion:** “Delta is the first letter of *Dios*, the genitive/stem form of Zeus” is accurate. “Delta is the first letter of Zeus” is incomplete.

## 6. Triangulum Minor or Minus?

**Documented grammatical issue.**

Because *triangulum* is neuter, the adjective should be neuter **minus**, not masculine **minor**. “Triangulum Minor” appears repeatedly in English-language historical writing, including Allen, but is grammatically defective Latin. [Allen with editorial correction](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Triangulum_Minor%2A.html).

## 7. Which stars formed Triangulum Minus?

The stable modern identification is 6/ι, 10, and 12 Tri. Confusion arises because:

- 6 Tri is also ι Tri;
- it is variable TZ Tri;
- multiple systems complicate component naming;
- older charts reverse east and west;
- some modern drawings join a different nearby faint star.

The Hevelian chart and catalogue, not a modern stick figure, are the controlling evidence.

## 8. Mothallah: star name or constellation name?

**Documented transmission problem.**

Arabic *al-muthallath* means “the triangle”; *raʾs al-muthallath* means “head of the triangle.” European writers detached and corrupted parts of the phrase into a proper name for α.

**Conclusion:** Mothallah is now formally a star name, but historically it arose from a constellation/positional phrase. Spellings such as Metallah and Elmuthalleth are not distinct etymologies.

## 9. Is β “Deltotum”?

Some modern lists attach **Deltotum/Deltoton** to β Tri, while others use them for the whole constellation. A 2025 formal assignment reportedly gives **Deltoton to δ Tri**, creating another layer.

**Conclusion:** Historical *Deltoton* primarily named the constellation. Individual-star use is late and inconsistent; current IAU component assignments must be distinguished from old usage.

## 10. Is M33 triangular?

**Popular error.**

Its name comes from its position in Triangulum. Its disc is a flocculent spiral, not a triangle.

## 11. Did Hubble discover M33 or prove galaxies in 1926?

**Popular compression.**

Messier catalogued it in 1764, and earlier observers may have seen it. Hubble discovered and measured many of its variables, not the nebula itself. The external-galaxy conclusion was cumulative, with decisive M31 evidence already announced in 1924–1925.

## 12. Was Hubble’s distance “accurate”?

Hubble’s 850,000-light-year result was dramatically too small compared with the modern ~2.7-million-light-year value. It was nevertheless scientifically decisive in scale and method.

Calling it “accurate” erases the later Cepheid calibration history; calling it worthless erases its historical force.

## 13. Is M33 certainly a satellite of M31?

**Disputed dynamical interpretation.**

M33 and M31 are gravitationally associated Local Group members. Models differ on whether M33 has completed a close past passage around M31 or is on first infall. “Satellite of Andromeda” is plausible shorthand but stronger than the present orbital evidence warrants.

## 14. Is Triangulum II part of Triangulum or M33?

**Popular naming confusion.**

**Triangulum II** is an ultra-faint Milky Way satellite discovered in Pan-STARRS data and named for its sky position. It is neither a second version of the constellation nor a satellite of M33. Its classification as dwarf galaxy versus disrupting cluster has itself been debated. [Discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.05554); [reassessment](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02978).

## 15. Does the Farnese Atlas depict it?

**Contested in casual image annotation; negative in detailed study.**

Schaefer’s inventory finds no Triangulum. Generic small incisions should not be labelled as the constellation without positional and iconographic evidence.

## 16. “Every three stars form a triangle”

Geometrically true unless collinear, but historically beside the point. This group was distinctive because the stars produced a recognizable small, narrow figure in a stable sky region and were useful for locating faint Aries. The ancient name described a particular established asterism, not a claim of geometric uniqueness.

## 17. Astrology, Freemasonry, and architecture

Later astrological pages claim Triangulum promotes architecture, Freemasonry, law, balance, righteousness, or truth.

**Finding:** Ptolemy supplies only a Mercury-like nature. The occupational and moral elaborations are later interpretive traditions. No classical evidence ties the constellation to Freemasonry, which postdates the ancient sources.

## 18. Hoaxes and unsupported claims found in the contested-material pass

No important organized “Triangulum hoax” was found. Recurrent unsupported or erroneous claims include:

- that M33 is triangular;
- that Triangulum was invented by Ptolemy;
- that the Nile interpretation proves Egyptian origin;
- that Mothallah was always the individual Arabic name of α;
- that Ceres was discovered in Triangulum;
- that “Triangulum Minor” is correct Latin;
- that the Farnese globe clearly shows it;
- that Schiller replaced it with the Trinity rather than Saint Peter’s mitre;
- that Hubble’s 1926 distance matches the modern distance;
- that Triangulum II belongs to M33;
- that generic triangular occult symbols prove an ancient Hermetic cult of this constellation.

## Open questions

1. **Exact Babylonian star membership:** MUL.APIN’s reconstruction remains dependent on rising data, textual interpretation, and comparison with later lists. A new synthesis of cuneiform witnesses and modern precessed simulations could alter individual identifications.

2. **Eratosthenic textual layers:** Which details in the Deltoton chapter descend from the lost Hellenistic original, and which entered through Imperial epitomators or scholia?

3. **Demeter’s request:** The Sicily tradition is secure, but the exact earliest surviving witness for the fully dramatized “Demeter asked Zeus” wording needs a manuscript-level study across the Epitome, *Fragmenta Vaticana*, and Aratean scholia.

4. **Threefold cosmic division:** The attribution of the triangle’s corners to Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades remains poorly sourced in modern retellings.

5. **Kugel Globe:** Whether its small triangular mark is certainly Deltoton requires wider peer-reviewed evaluation.

6. **Arabic indigenous lore:** A complete reading of all Triangulum commentary across al-Ṣūfī manuscripts may disclose regional Arabic names omitted or altered in modern editions.

7. **Hebrew reception:** Medieval Hebrew translations of Arabic star catalogues likely contain translated or transliterated forms, but a specific manuscript survey was beyond the accessible online corpus.

8. **Bode’s exact German prose:** The atlas and companion are digitized, but their scans are not consistently OCR-searchable. A diplomatic transcription of Bode’s entire Triangulum entry would be preferable to relying on catalogue summaries.

9. **“830 stars in 109 groups”:** The dataset or collection meant by this wording remains unidentified; it does not match Bode’s documented catalogue totals.

10. **Recent IAU names:** Because the 2025 additions are new, mirrors and older WGSN tables are inconsistent. The live IAU-CSN should control component spelling and adoption dates.
