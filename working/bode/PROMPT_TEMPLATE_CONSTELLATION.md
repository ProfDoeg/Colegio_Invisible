# The constellation dossier prompt (the Bode collection)

Anthony, 2026-09-12: "I would like a dossier on each of the bode constellations, even the
apocryphal ones." The set is the Bode collection on chain (Uranographia, 1801, 109 groups):
the 88 modern constellations and 19 that the IAU later abolished. The twelve zodiac figures
have their own dossiers in working/zodiac; this prompt covers the rest. `[NAME]` is the only
substitution point; the per-constellation brief follows.

---

[NAME]
Please conduct a comprehensive, source-driven research dossier on this constellation of the
western sky, as it stands on Johann Elert Bode's Uranographia of 1801 and in the tradition
before and after it. I want the fullest reconstructable history of the figure, from its
earliest attestation to the present, chronologically and in detail. Include, wherever available:

- the names: Babylonian and Egyptian antecedents where they exist, Greek, Latin, Arabic
  (al-Sufi's names and the Bedouin asterisms in the same stars), Persian, Hebrew, medieval
  Latin and the vernaculars, Spanish, and Bode's own German and Latin labels; the etymologies;
  the abbreviation and genitive if the IAU kept it
- the stars: which stars make the figure; the brightest with their proper names and the
  derivations (Arabic, Greek, Latin, modern); the Bayer letters and Flamsteed numbers; the
  asterisms inside it; deep-sky objects that carry its name (nebulae, clusters, galaxies, the
  Messier and NGC objects); variable stars, novae and supernovae recorded in it; meteor showers
  radiating from it; the boundary changes from Ptolemy to Bayer, Hevelius, Lacaille, Bode and
  Delporte's 1930 lines; what neighbors it lost stars to or took them from
- THE CLASSICAL MYTHS, EXHAUSTIVELY, where the figure is ancient: every Greek and Roman myth
  attached to it, every catasterism and variant in Eratosthenes' Catasterismi and the Epitome,
  Hyginus' Poetica Astronomica and Fabulae, the scholia to Aratus and Germanicus, Ovid,
  Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, Pausanias and the mythographers; who placed the figure
  in the sky and why; the competing identities; every other classical myth in which the
  figure's person, beast or object acts, each in its own paragraph with the ancient citations
  (author, work, book, section) and the date of first attestation
- THE INVENTION, where the figure is modern: who named it, when, on which globe, chart or
  catalogue (Plancius, Keyser and de Houtman, Bayer 1603, Hevelius 1690, Lacaille 1756, Lalande,
  Bode 1782 and 1801, Poczobut, Hell, Lemonnier and the others); what it honored (a king, a
  patron, an instrument, an animal, a machine, a voyage); Bode's plate and his description in
  the Uranographia's text; how other atlases received it (Flamsteed, Jamieson, Burritt, Argelander,
  Heis); when and why the IAU dropped it (Rome 1922, Delporte 1930) or kept it; where its stars
  live now
- the other traditions in the same stars: Arabic, Chinese equivalents if any, Indian, the
  Christianized sky of Julius Schiller (1627, which saint or object took its place), the Hebrew
  and Kabbalistic mappings, the alchemical and Hermetic readings, the astrological uses of its
  stars (the fixed stars in Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos, the Behenian stars, the lunar mansions
  crossing it)
- iconography: the figure's depiction across manuscripts, globes and atlases with dates and
  places (the Farnese Atlas, al-Sufi's manuscripts, Dürer's 1515 map, Bayer, Hevelius, Bode's
  plate, Jamieson, the Urania's Mirror cards); coins, ceilings, cathedral portals, calendars
- people, events and works attached to it: explorers who steered by its stars, observers who
  discovered its objects, poems, paintings, music and buildings that name it, ships and
  missions
- controversies and disputes: contested star identifications, contested etymologies, disputed
  attributions of invention, misprints that became names, hoaxes and popular errors

Include the good and the bad without judgment, and myth as fully as fact.
For every significant claim distinguish documented fact, scholarly reconstruction, tradition,
disputed claim, unverified claim, legend, myth and modern invention, and say which sources carry
it, when the story first appears, who spread it, and what evidence exists.
Do not use fact-checking summaries as substitutes for the sources; trace to the texts.

Structure the dossier as: Basic identification (names, abbreviation, status: kept or
abolished, stars, boundaries); Stars and objects; Chronology of attestation (dated, placed);
Myths and legends by culture, or Invention and reception for a modern figure; Other traditions
in the same stars; Iconography (dated, placed); People, events and works; Controversies and
disputes; Open questions; Sources (full list of URLs and editions consulted).
Length: as long as the material requires; for the ancient figures a thousand lines is not too
many; for the apocryphal ones, everything that survives about them.
