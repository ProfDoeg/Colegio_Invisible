# Triangulum Minus: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Status — documented fact.** **Triangulum Minus is abolished.** More precisely, it was not included among the 88 constellations adopted by the International Astronomical Union at its first General Assembly in Rome in 1922. The sky was subsequently divided into exclusive constellation areas by Eugène Delporte; his boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930. All three stars of Triangulum Minus consequently lie in the official constellation **Triangulum**.

It is therefore slightly misleading to say that the IAU “abolished Triangulum Minus in 1922” by a specific resolution against it. The documented sequence is:

1. In 1922 the IAU adopted 88 Latin constellation names and abbreviations; Triangulum Minus was omitted.
2. In 1925 Delporte was commissioned to construct northern boundaries.
3. In 1928 his complete system was approved.
4. In 1930 it was published, placing the former Lesser Triangle wholly inside Triangulum.

The IAU’s own historical account documents this sequence: [The Constellations, IAU](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/).

**Official modern destination — documented fact.**

- Constellation: **Triangulum**
- IAU abbreviation: **Tri**
- Genitive: **Trianguli**
- Meaning: “of the Triangle”
- Present area: about 131.85 square degrees
- Former Triangulum Minus stars: **6, 10, and 12 Trianguli**

Triangulum Minus itself has **no IAU abbreviation, official genitive, area, or boundary polygon**. A historically regular Latin genitive would be *Trianguli Minoris*, but that is a grammatical reconstruction, not an IAU designation.

### Name and etymology

**Documented fact.** Hevelius’s name is **Triangulum Minus**, “the Lesser” or “Smaller Triangle”:

- *triangulum*: Latin neuter noun, “triangle,” ultimately from *tri-* (“three”) and *angulus* (“angle”);
- *minus*: neuter comparative of *parvus*, “smaller” or “lesser”;
- contrasting name: **Triangulum Majus**, “the Greater Triangle.”

Because *triangulum* is neuter, the adjective must also be neuter: *minus*, not masculine *minor*. Hence **Triangulum Minor is a grammatical error**, although it became widespread in later popular works and databases.

Documented or straightforward vernacular forms include:

- English: **Lesser Triangle**, **Little Triangle**, **Smaller Triangle**
- German: **Kleines Dreieck**
- French: **Petit Triangle**
- Italian: **Triangolo Minore**
- Spanish: **Triángulo Menor**
- Polish: **Trójkąt Mały** or descriptions equivalent to “small triangle”
- Korean modern rendering: 작은삼각형자리, “Little-Triangle constellation”

No independent Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, or Indian name for **Triangulum Minus as a three-star unit** has been found. Those cultures possessed figures in the larger surrounding region, but the Lesser Triangle itself is an early-modern European construction.

### Bode’s labels

**Documented fact.** On Johann Elert Bode’s 1801 *Uranographia*, the Latin label is **Triangulum Minus**, alongside **Triangulum Majus**. The plate is the northern-sky sheet containing Perseus, Cassiopeia, Andromeda, the Triangles, Honores Friderici, Lacerta, Aries, and Musca Borealis—usually numbered plate IV in modern descriptions of the atlas.

Bode’s German popular works use ordinary German constellation vocabulary, but I could not securely verify an 1801 text passage in which Bode gives the Lesser Triangle a separate formal German heading. “Kleines Dreieck” is the natural and later-attested German translation; it should not be presented as a transcribed Bode catalogue heading without a page citation.

Bode’s accompanying 1801 *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* advertised **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters**. The figure “830 stars in 109 groups” in the brief could not be traced to Bode’s publication, cataloguing system, or surviving plate descriptions. It may describe a modern collection or database “on chain,” not an historical property of *Uranographia*.

## Stars and objects

### The three defining stars

The identification with **6, 10, and 12 Trianguli** is well documented by Hevelius-derived catalogues and later atlases. All are naked-eye stars only under reasonably dark skies.

| Historical role | Modern designations | Approx. visual magnitude | Modern character | Notes |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| Brightest vertex | **6 Trianguli = ι Trianguli = TZ Trianguli = HR 642 = HIP 10280** | about 4.9, combined | Multiple system; active/variable component associated with RS CVn-type behavior | Officially named **Triminus** in 2025 |
| Second vertex | **10 Trianguli = HD 14252 = HR 675** | 5.29 | A2 V star; optical companion reported | Listed as **1 H. Trianguli Minoris** in later Hevelius-derived nomenclature |
| Third vertex | **12 Trianguli** | 5.37 | F-type evolved star, commonly classified F0 III | Flamsteed also marked it with the now-abandoned letter **c Trianguli** |

The exact modern physical parameters vary by catalogue solution, especially for the unresolved or multiple 6 Tri system. Historical claims that all three were simply “fifth-magnitude stars” are reasonable naked-eye classifications, not precise modern photometry.

### Triminus

**Documented modern invention.** On 25 August 2025, the IAU Working Group on Star Names adopted **Triminus** for the principal component of 6 Trianguli, catalogued as HR 642/HIP 10280. The IAU publicly listed it in February 2026.

The name is a modern contraction of **Tri**—the official abbreviation for Triangulum—and **minus**, preserving the memory of Triangulum Minus. The supporting All Skies Encyclopaedia account says that the full historical name was considered too long and liable to confusion with the surviving constellation. The proposal was made in 2023 and accepted in 2025.

This does **not** restore Triangulum Minus as a constellation. It commemorates the lost figure in one star’s proper name.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

**Documented fact with an important qualification.**

- Bayer’s *Uranometria* of 1603 assigned Greek letters only to the principal stars of ancient Triangulum. The Lesser Triangle did not yet exist.
- **ι Trianguli** is a later Bayer-style extension, not one of Bayer’s original five lettered stars.
- The numbers **6, 10, and 12 Trianguli** are Flamsteed numbers.
- Flamsteed’s treatment did not maintain two autonomous constellation catalogue headings: in his reworking of Hevelius he merged Triangulum and Triangulum Minus under the plural **Triangula**, “the Triangles.”
- The designation “1 H. Trianguli Minoris” attached to 10 Tri is a later catalogue reference to the first Hevelius star under that heading, not a Flamsteed number.

### Internal asterism

**Documented fact.** The entire constellation was itself one simple asterism: three stars joined into a small, rather compact triangle south of the ancient, brighter triangle.

There are no securely attested named sub-asterisms inside it. Modern observers occasionally continue to call the pattern the Little or Lesser Triangle, but this is historical revival rather than official nomenclature.

### Variable stars

**Documented fact.** The chief variable associated with the figure is:

- **TZ Trianguli / 6 Tri / ι Tri / Triminus**: a multiple system whose variability is associated with stellar activity in an RS CVn-type system.

No other historically important variable is securely tied specifically to the three-star figure.

### Novae and supernovae

**Negative finding.** No historical nova or supernova is known to have been recorded as occurring “in Triangulum Minus.” Searches for objects assigned to the obsolete constellation did not reveal a securely catalogued nova designation using *Trianguli Minoris*.

Modern transients near these coordinates are catalogued under **Triangulum**, not under the abolished figure.

### Deep-sky objects

**Negative finding.** No Messier object, NGC object, nebula, cluster, or galaxy is known to carry the name **Triangulum Minus** or **Lesser Triangle** as an established historical designation.

The famous objects named for **Triangulum**, particularly:

- **M33**, the Triangulum Galaxy;
- **Triangulum II**, an ultra-faint Milky Way satellite;
- galaxies and variables bearing *Trianguli* designations,

belong to the surviving larger constellation. They should not be retroactively assigned to Triangulum Minus merely because of the shared word *Triangulum*. M33 lies well outside the three-star Lesser Triangle.

### Meteor showers

**Negative finding.** No meteor shower officially bears a name derived from Triangulum Minus. Historical or modern “Triangulid” radiants use the surviving constellation Triangulum. No evidence was found that a recognized radiant was specifically called the *Triangulum Minorids*, *Triangulum Minus meteors*, or equivalent.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the invention

#### Second millennium BCE: Mesopotamia

**Documented tradition, but not Triangulum Minus.** The surrounding ancient Triangulum region participated in Mesopotamian figures connected with the **Plough**, while α Trianguli was associated in *MUL.APIN* with UR.BAR.RA, “the Wolf,” called the “seeder of the Plough.”

None of the evidence recovered identifies 6, 10, and 12 Tri as a separate three-star Babylonian triangle. Applying the Wolf or Plough names to Triangulum Minus is therefore unwarranted.

#### Greek and Roman antiquity

**Documented tradition, but belonging to Triangulum Majus/modern Triangulum.** Greeks knew the principal figure as:

- **Τρίγωνον — Trigōnon**, “Triangle”;
- **Δελτωτόν — Deltōton**, “Delta-shaped.”

Ptolemy catalogued four principal stars: the apex and three along the base. These are not the three stars later removed by Hevelius for Triangulum Minus.

The geometrical and mythological traditions of Hermes, Zeus’s delta, Sicily, Egypt, the Nile, and the tripartite cosmos concern the ancient Triangle—not Hevelius’s Lesser Triangle.

#### ca. 150 CE: Ptolemy

**Documented fact.** In *Almagest* VII–VIII, Ptolemy’s Triangle contains four stars. Modern identifications assign them to α, β, δ, and γ Trianguli. The future 6, 10, and 12 Tri do not form a separate catalogue constellation.

#### ca. 964: al-Ṣūfī

**Documented fact.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābitah* gives the Ptolemaic Triangle as:

- **Ṣūrat al-Muthallath**, “the Figure of the Triangle”;
- **raʾs al-muthallath**, “the head/apex of the triangle,” for α Tri;
- **al-anīsān**, “the two companions/friends,” reported for α and β Tri.

Al-Ṣūfī’s diagram and catalogue preserve the four-star classical figure. No separate Arabic Lesser Triangle appears.

#### 1437: Ulugh Beg

**Documented fact.** Ulugh Beg’s catalogue continues the Ptolemaic/Islamicate Triangle tradition. It offers no independent Triangulum Minus.

#### 1515: Dürer

**Negative finding.** Albrecht Dürer’s printed celestial maps show the traditional constellations known through Ptolemaic and Arabic transmission. They necessarily predate Triangulum Minus by roughly 170 years and cannot depict it.

#### 1603: Bayer

**Documented fact.** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* shows the ancient northern Triangle, also distinguished as **Triangulus Septentrionalis** from the newly mapped southern Triangulum Australe. The Lesser Triangle was not yet a constellation.

#### 1627: Schiller

**Documented fact.** Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaces Triangulum with **Mitræ Pontificalis S. Petri**, the papal mitre or tiara of Saint Peter. This predates Hevelius’s split. Schiller therefore did not Christianize “Triangulum Minus” separately; the future Lesser Triangle’s stars remained merely neighboring background stars.

### Invention and early publication

#### Before 1687: Hevelius’s observations

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Hevelius measured numerous naked-eye stars absent from the standard Ptolemaic–Tychonic figure. The three stars later assigned to Triangulum Minus entered his observational programme as additions in the vicinity between Triangulum and the head of Aries.

Claims that Hevelius was “the first person to see” them are false: they are naked-eye stars. The defensible claim is that he was the first to **catalogue them as a new constellation figure**.

#### 1687

**Documented bibliographical date, with qualification.** The engraved title of Hevelius’s *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* bears **1687**, the year of his death. Some modern accounts consequently say that Triangulum Minus was “introduced in 1687.”

The complete *Prodromus Astronomiae*, including catalogue and atlas, was issued posthumously in Gdańsk in **1690** by Hevelius’s widow and collaborator Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius. Thus:

- “invented or engraved by 1687” is defensible;
- “first published in 1690” is bibliographically safer for the complete work.

#### 1690: first secure publication

**Documented fact.** In Hevelius’s *Catalogus Stellarum Fixarum* and *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia*, the ancient Triangle is divided into:

- **Triangulum Majus**
- **Triangulum Minus**

The plate is titled approximately **Triangulum Majus, Minus et Musca** and shows the figures from the external or globe-view orientation typical of Hevelius’s atlas.

### Hevelius’s reason

**Documented physical rationale; motive partly reconstructed.** The visible reason is taxonomic and geometrical: three newly catalogued nearby stars make another recognizable triangle, so the old figure becomes “Greater” and the new one “Lesser.”

No dedication, commemorative person, voyage, patron, machine, moral emblem, or classical catasterism has been found for it. Unlike Scutum Sobiescianum, it did not honor a king; unlike Sextans Uraniae, it did not memorialize an instrument. The figure appears to have been invented simply because the three stars formed a triangle.

Statements that Hevelius created it to honor the Trinity, Sicily, Egypt, or Zeus conflate it with traditions of the ancient Triangle.

### Eighteenth-century reception

#### 1721–1722: John Senex

**Documented cartographic reception.** Senex’s planispheres show two neighboring triangles. His catalogue basis was connected with the prematurely published 1712 Flamsteed material. The pair is not necessarily maintained as two fully independent catalogue constellations.

#### 1725/1729: Flamsteed

**Documented fact.** In the Hevelius material reprinted in *Historia Coelestis Britannica*, Flamsteed merged the headings into **Triangula**, plural “Triangles.” His 1729 *Atlas Coelestis* similarly treats the area as a compound pair rather than strongly promoting an autonomous Lesser Triangle.

This is an early sign of instability: the shape was accepted visually but its catalogue status was already being blurred.

#### 1742: Doppelmayr

**Documented cartographic reception.** Johann Gabriel Doppelmayr’s *Atlas Coelestis* depicts **Triangulum Majus and Minus**. This confirms that the innovation circulated beyond Hevelius and Flamsteed.

#### ca. 1750: Lotter

**Documented cartographic reception.** Tobias Conrad Lotter’s planisphere shows a pair of triangular figures, in some impressions appearing partially overlapping.

#### 1756–1763: Lacaille

**Negative finding.** Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille’s southern-constellation reforms did not create, rename, or materially modify Triangulum Minus. Lacaille is relevant to Triangulum Australe and the southern instrumental constellations, not to the Lesser Triangle.

#### Lalande, Poczobut, Hell, Lemonnier

**Negative finding.** No evidence was found that Jérôme Lalande, Marcin Poczobut, Maximilian Hell, or Pierre Charles Le Monnier invented or renamed Triangulum Minus. They are associated with other eighteenth-century figures—honorific, instrumental, or zoological. Their inclusion in a general lost-constellation template should not imply participation in this particular invention.

#### 1782: Bode

**Documented reception.** Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln* retained the paired triangles. This popular atlas was based in part on the Flamsteed/Fortin cartographic tradition but corrected and enlarged by Bode.

#### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact.** Bode gives the figure its most elaborate large-format representation. The small geometrical outline appears south of Triangulum Majus and west/northwest of the head of Aries, near Musca Borealis.

Bode did **not** claim invention. He preserved Hevelius’s name and figure among a great number of ancient, early-modern, and newly proposed constellations. His atlas was deliberately inclusive rather than a declaration that every pictured figure had equal standing.

The accompanying German catalogue’s title translates as *General Description and Identification of the Stars, together with a Catalogue of the Right Ascension and Declination of 17,240 Stars, Double Stars, Nebulae and Star Clusters*. I found no extended Bode myth or rationale for the Lesser Triangle: it is essentially a catalogue-and-map inheritance.

### Nineteenth-century decline

#### 1822: Alexander Jamieson

**Documented reception.** Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas*, plate III, labels the region **Triangula**, retaining the two triangles collectively. This follows the compromise already visible in Flamsteed.

#### 1824: *Urania’s Mirror*

**Documented iconographic reception.** Card 5 depicts Gloria Frederici, Andromeda, and **Triangula**. The cards largely redraw Jamieson, with pierced holes marking stars. Again the plural survives more readily than Triangulum Minus as an independent constellation.

#### 1833–1835: Elijah Burritt

**Documented textual survival.** Burritt states that the “upper or Northern Triangle” is ancient and that Hevelius took three stars between it and the head of Aries to form **Triangulum minus**. This is a valuable late popular witness, but Burritt’s wording is historical explanation rather than strong advocacy for a separate modern constellation.

#### 1843: Argelander

**Documented consolidation.** Friedrich Wilhelm Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* is commonly credited with reuniting the two figures under Triangulum. This represents the practical nineteenth-century outcome: precision catalogues increasingly preferred one constellation name.

#### 1872: Heis

**Documented general context; negative specific finding.** Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* belongs to the rationalized naked-eye atlas tradition. I found no evidence that Heis revived Triangulum Minus as an independent constellation; its stars were treated with Triangulum.

#### 1887: Proctor

**Documented late survival.** Richard A. Proctor’s *A New Star Atlas* used **Triangula**, demonstrating that the double-triangle visual arrangement lingered after separate catalogue status had weakened.

#### 1899: Allen

**Documented antiquarian transmission.** Richard Hinckley Allen discussed *Triangulum majus* and the discarded Lesser Triangle in *Star Names and Their Meanings*. Allen helped propagate much of the later English-language lore, but his entry combines primary quotations, antiquarian synthesis, and occasionally speculative etymology. It must not be treated as a primary ancient source.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

#### 1922–1930: non-admission and absorption

**Documented fact.** Triangulum alone entered the IAU list. Delporte’s B1875-aligned boundary polygon placed 6, 10, and 12 Tri inside Triangulum. No neighboring modern constellation received any of the three.

#### Late twentieth century

**Documented antiquarian revival.** Histories of constellation cartography, lists of obsolete constellations, and Ian Ridpath’s *Star Tales* restored awareness of the figure. It survived as an obsolete asterism rather than an astronomical jurisdiction.

#### 2023–2025: Triminus proposal and adoption

**Documented modern invention.** A proposal derived the contracted name **Triminus** from the obsolete constellation. The IAU WGSN adopted it for 6 Tri on 25 August 2025.

#### 2026: official announcement

**Documented fact.** The IAU’s announcement of 12 February 2026 publicly listed Triminus among 59 recently adopted star names, giving HR 642, HIP 10280, 6 Tri, constellation code Tri, and the 2025 adoption date.

## Myths and legends

## Triangulum Minus itself

**Finding: none ancient.** Triangulum Minus was invented in the seventeenth century. It has:

- no Eratosthenic catasterism;
- no entry in the surviving *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- no independent Hyginian myth;
- no separate treatment in the scholia to Aratus or Germanicus;
- no appearance as a constellation in Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, or Pausanias;
- no ancient figure whose adventures can properly be narrated as the mythology of Triangulum Minus.

Any “classical myth of Triangulum Minus” is necessarily a retrospective transfer from the ancient Triangle.

### The Zeus-delta story

**Ancient tradition attached to the parent Triangle.** Hyginus, *De astronomia* 2.19, says that Mercury/Hermes placed the triangular sign above Aries both to indicate the position of the otherwise dim Ram and to make the first Greek letter of the name of Jupiter/Zeus—*Dios*, Διός.

This is myth, not an explanation of Hevelius’s three stars. Hyginus is the earliest surviving explicit carrier located in this research, probably writing in the late first century BCE or early imperial period, although his material draws on older Hellenistic mythography.

### Egypt and the Nile

**Ancient geographical tradition.** Hyginus 2.19 reports that some thought the Triangle represented Egypt; others the region where the Nile divided or bounded Ethiopia and Egypt.

The frequently repeated phrase “Gift of the Nile” ultimately recalls Herodotus’s description of Egypt as the river’s gift, but antiquarian constellation literature confused or played upon Latin forms such as *Nili donum* and *Nili domum*. None of this applies specifically to the Lesser Triangle.

### Sicily

**Ancient tradition.** Hyginus 2.19 reports that some thought Sicily was depicted. Sicily’s triangular form generated the names **Trinacria** and **Trinacris**, “three-cornered.”

Later sources commonly add that Ceres/Demeter requested that her island be placed in heaven. That fuller personalized catasterism is a later elaboration in astronomical mythography; it is not stated in Hyginus’s surviving paragraph. It must not be silently attributed to Eratosthenes or Hyginus.

### The tripartite cosmos

**Ancient mythographic or cosmological tradition.** Hyginus 2.19 reports that the three angles represented the gods’ division of the universe into three portions. The likely background is the division among Zeus/Jupiter, Poseidon/Neptune, and Hades/Pluto.

Hyginus does not tell a full narrative in this passage and does not name all three recipients there. Expanding it into a detailed lot-casting myth uses external traditions such as Homeric and later mythography, not the constellation text itself.

### The delta shape

**Documented ancient description.** Aratus describes the figure below Andromeda as resembling a delta, with two sides equal and the third shorter. Manilius, *Astronomica* 1, likewise calls it **Deltoton** and describes its unequal stellar frame. Ovid’s pseudo-Ovidian *Nux* tradition was later cited for comparison with a delta, but that literary allusion is not a catasterism.

### Christian Trinity

**Early-modern tradition.** Renaissance and early-modern writers sometimes read a celestial triangle as the Christian Trinity. This is Christian allegory, not classical mythology and not a demonstrated reason for Hevelius’s invention.

### Saint Peter’s mitre

**Documented Christianized mapping.** Schiller’s 1627 *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaced ancient Triangulum with **Mitræ Pontificalis S. Petri**, Saint Peter’s papal mitre. This is the closest firmly documented Christian predecessor in the region, but it predates and does not distinguish Triangulum Minus.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Arabic and Persianate astronomy

**Documented for the region; negative for the exact trio.** Al-Ṣūfī’s Arabic Triangle is **al-Muthallath** or *Ṣūrat al-Muthallath*. Its named apex and “two companions” belong to the ancient Triangle.

No al-Ṣūfī name has been traced for 6, 10, or 12 Tri individually. Nor was a Bedouin three-star triangle securely identified in precisely these stars. Modern pages that attach *raʾs al-muthallath* or “Mothallah” to 6 Tri confuse it with **α Tri**, the apex of the greater figure.

Persian astronomical catalogues derived from Ptolemy, al-Ṣūfī, and Ulugh Beg transmitted the Arabic–Greek Triangle. No independent Persian name for Hevelius’s trio was found.

### Chinese astronomy

**Documented overlap.** Stars in this area belonged to **Tiāndàjiāngjūn** (天大將軍), “Heaven’s Great General,” a large Chinese asterism extending across modern Andromeda, Perseus, and Triangulum.

A modern reconstruction of the traditional star chain explicitly runs from **10 Trianguli through ι/6 Trianguli**, then through β Tri and a sequence of Andromeda stars toward φ Persei. Thus at least 10 and 6 Tri securely participate in the Chinese formation.

The inclusion and exact line-position of **12 Tri** varies between modern reconstructions and was not securely established from a primary Chinese star catalogue in this search. It should not be claimed without a chart-specific citation.

Tiāndàjiāngjūn is not a Chinese “Little Triangle.” It is a much larger, culturally independent formation whose lines merely cross the same stars.

### Indian astronomy

**Negative finding.** No traditional nakshatra or named Indian asterism consisting specifically of 6, 10, and 12 Tri was located. The area is near the sidereal Aries–Taurus sector, but proximity to a nakshatra longitude is not evidence that the three stars constituted a traditional figure.

### Hebrew traditions

**Tradition, but not securely ancient.** The form **Hammosciush** appears in early-modern European lists as a supposed Hebrew name for Triangulum, including Sherburne’s seventeenth-century astronomical appendix and later compilations. Its linguistic origin and direct Hebrew textual basis remain uncertain.

No Hebrew or Jewish astronomical source was found that distinguishes the three stars of Triangulum Minus. Modern biblical-astronomy charts that assign theological meanings to the Triangle are confessional reinterpretations, not evidence of ancient Israelite nomenclature.

### Kabbalistic, alchemical, and Hermetic readings

**Negative finding.** Triangles possess extensive symbolic meanings in Kabbalah, alchemy, Hermeticism, and Christian theology. However, no pre-Hevelian text was found that:

1. identifies 6, 10, and 12 Tri as a triangle,
2. calls it a Lesser Triangle,
3. assigns it a specific alchemical element, sephirah, Hermetic deity, or talisman.

General symbolism of an upward or downward triangle should therefore not be presented as historical lore of this constellation.

### Astrology

**Negative finding for the Lesser Triangle.** Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* assigns a Mercurial nature to the stars of the ancient Triangle, but the later Lesser Triangle’s faint stars do not receive an independent set of planetary natures.

None of the three is one of the canonical fifteen **Behenian fixed stars**.

No Arabic lunar mansion is defined by this trio. The nearby lunar mansions of Aries and the Pleiades are separate stellar systems. Likewise, no Indian nakshatra is determined by the Lesser Triangle.

Modern astrologers may apply the general “Mercury nature” of Triangulum to stars now within its boundaries. That is a modern extension of a constellation-wide rule, not an ancient judgment specifically concerning Triangulum Minus.

## Iconography

### Objects that cannot show Triangulum Minus

**Documented chronological exclusion.**

- **Farnese Atlas**, Roman copy of a Hellenistic celestial globe, second century CE: cannot show a seventeenth-century constellation. Its weathered figures include the ancient sky tradition only.
- **Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts**, from the medieval Islamicate tradition: show the classical four-star Triangle, often twice—globe view and sky view—not the Lesser Triangle.
- **Dürer’s 1515 celestial maps**: predate it.
- **Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria***: predates it.
- **Schiller’s 1627 atlas**: predates it, although the region becomes Saint Peter’s mitre.

No ancient coin, classical ceiling, cathedral portal, or medieval calendar has been securely identified as depicting **Triangulum Minus**. A triangular emblem in such art is not sufficient identification.

### Hevelius, 1687/1690

**Documented first iconography.** Hevelius’s plate **Triangulum Majus, Minus et Musca** is the first secure depiction. It shows:

- the larger ancient Triangle;
- a smaller three-star triangle immediately south of it;
- the nearby northern Fly;
- surrounding portions of Aries, Andromeda, Perseus, and the northern Fish.

The constellations are presented as if on a celestial globe, so east and west are reversed relative to a direct sky view. The atlas’s engraved title bears 1687; the complete work was issued at Gdańsk in 1690.

### Doppelmayr, 1742

**Documented fact.** Doppelmayr reproduced the double-triangle arrangement in *Atlas Coelestis*. This was a significant vehicle for dissemination in German-speaking Europe.

### Lotter, ca. 1750

**Documented fact.** Lotter’s planisphere also shows paired triangles, demonstrating broader commercial-map reception.

### Bode, 1782

**Documented fact.** Bode retained the figure in *Vorstellung der Gestirne*, an atlas aimed at educated amateurs who could not conveniently use the enormous professional atlases.

### Bode, 1801

**Documented fact.** Bode’s *Uranographia* plate is the best-known image of the constellation. It renders the geometrical outlines delicately against a dense scientific star field. Unlike mythological human or animal constellations, the pair needs no elaborate personification: they are simply outlined triangles.

The Bode image is preserved in several digital collections, including ETH Zürich, the New York Public Library, the Science Museum Group, and Wikimedia Commons.

### Jamieson, 1822

**Documented fact.** Jamieson’s plate III presents **Triangula** with Andromeda, Perseus, Caput Medusae, and Gloria Frederici. His atlas simplified the sky to naked-eye stars and supplied more naturalistic constellation art.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, 1824

**Documented fact.** The corresponding pierced card—usually card 5—copies Jamieson’s composition. The pair appears collectively as **Triangula**. The cards were initially attributed only to “a lady”; research by Peter Hingley identified the designer as the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam.

### Later scientific atlases

**Documented trend.** Argelander and Heis abandoned the separate pictorial identity in favor of a single Triangulum. Proctor’s 1887 use of *Triangula* was a late survival rather than a lasting reversal.

## People, events, and works

### Johannes and Elisabeth Hevelius

**Documented fact.** Johannes Hevelius conceived and observed the constellation. He died in 1687 before publication of the completed work. Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius supervised or completed the posthumous publication in 1690. The constellation’s first secure public existence is therefore inseparable from both astronomers.

### John Flamsteed

**Documented fact.** Flamsteed transmitted Hevelius’s catalogue material but weakened the new figure’s independence by merging the two triangles as **Triangula**. The familiar numbers 6, 10, and 12 derive from his numbering of Triangulum’s stars.

### Johann Gabriel Doppelmayr and Tobias Lotter

**Documented fact.** Their eighteenth-century maps helped give the Lesser Triangle a wider reception than its minimal invention might suggest.

### Johann Elert Bode

**Documented fact.** Bode was its most prominent late adopter. His 1801 atlas made it visually memorable but did not save it from nineteenth-century consolidation.

### Argelander

**Documented fact.** Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* represents the scientific reunion of the two figures. His practical naked-eye atlas helped establish the form that ultimately passed into standardized astronomy.

### Explorers and navigation

**Negative finding.** No voyage, explorer, or ship is documented as having navigated specifically by Triangulum Minus. Its stars are too faint and the asterism too small to rival Polaris, Ursa Major, Orion, the Southern Cross, or major nautical stars.

### Literature, visual art, and music

**Negative finding.** No securely documented pre-modern poem, painting, musical work, building, or ceiling programme was found that names or represents Triangulum Minus specifically.

References to “the little triangle” in art or poetry cannot be identified with this constellation without an astronomical context.

### Ships, spacecraft, and missions

**Negative finding.** No major ship, naval vessel, spacecraft, or astronomical mission was found whose documented namesake is Triangulum Minus. Uses of **Triangulum** or **Triangulum II** normally refer to the surviving constellation, the M33 galaxy, or unrelated geometrical language.

### Modern commemoration

**Documented fact.** The name **Triminus** for 6 Tri is presently the clearest institutional commemoration of the abolished constellation.

## Controversies and disputes

### “Triangulum Minor” versus “Triangulum Minus”

**Documented error.** *Triangulum Minor* is widely printed but grammatically mismatched. *Triangulum* is neuter; its comparative adjective must be **minus**. The error was spread by popular constellation lists, web pages, and modern telescopic-asterism guides.

This is not evidence for a second historical Latin name. It is best catalogued as a later solecism.

### Was it invented in 1687 or 1690?

**Bibliographical ambiguity, not a substantive dispute.**

- 1687 appears on the engraved atlas title and is Hevelius’s death year.
- 1690 is the publication date of the complete posthumous *Prodromus*, catalogue, and atlas.

Thus “invented by 1687; published in 1690” is the most exact formulation.

### Were its stars first catalogued by Hevelius?

**Qualified fact.** Hevelius first catalogued them **as members of this new constellation** and greatly enlarged the northern naked-eye catalogue. It would be unsafe to claim without qualification that no earlier observer or chart recorded any of the physical stars. Naked-eye visibility makes literal “discovery” impossible to establish.

### Did Hevelius remove them from ancient Triangulum?

**Qualified reconstruction.** Modern histories often say he “excised” or “borrowed” three stars from Triangulum. Ptolemy’s formed Triangle, however, contained four different principal stars; the future Lesser Triangle stars were not part of that canonical four-star skeleton.

The better description is that Hevelius used nearby southern stars in the broader cartographic territory associated with Triangulum and created a second figure. Because pre-1930 constellations lacked universal polygonal borders, “taken from” is a catalogue convention, not a transfer across a legal boundary.

### Did the IAU abolish it in 1922?

**Popular shorthand.** No separate expulsion resolution has been located. The IAU selected 88 names and omitted it. Delporte later assigned the area to Triangulum. “Not retained” is historically more precise than “formally abolished by vote.”

### Did Delporte transfer the stars from another constellation?

**No.** Delporte’s official borders placed all three in Triangulum. They did not pass to Aries, Andromeda, or Perseus. Earlier atlases lacked fixed borders, so maps can make them look close to the head of Aries without implying official membership.

### Why did it disappear?

**Scholarly reconstruction.** No single IAU statement condemning its unimaginative character was found. The evidentiary factors are:

- it duplicated an already existing geometric constellation;
- it contained only three faint stars;
- Flamsteed and others had already treated the pair collectively as *Triangula*;
- Argelander reunited the figures;
- nineteenth-century scientific atlases increasingly minimized superfluous pictorial subdivisions;
- the IAU standardized one continuous sky area as Triangulum.

Descriptions such as “too dull,” “unimaginative,” or “condemned to oblivion” are modern rhetoric, not documented reasons given by Hevelius, Argelander, or the IAU.

### Classical myths attributed to it

**Modern error.** Websites sometimes assign the Sicily, Nile Delta, Trinity, Hermes, or Zeus-delta stories directly to Triangulum Minus. Every traceable ancient source applies those stories to **Deltoton/Triangulum**, the ancient greater figure. No classical author knew the Lesser Triangle.

### Confusion with Triangulum Australe

**Documented risk of confusion.** Triangulum Minus is a northern, obsolete three-star figure next to Triangulum. **Triangulum Australe** is a separate southern constellation introduced through Dutch voyaging and Bayer’s atlas and retained by the IAU as **TrA**. Neither is an alternative name for the other.

### Confusion with M33 and Triangulum II

**Modern naming confusion.**

- “Triangulum Galaxy” means M33.
- “Triangulum II” means the ultra-faint Milky Way satellite.
- Neither name commemorates the Lesser Triangle.
- Searches for “Triangulum minor axis” frequently refer to the minor axis of M33, not the obsolete constellation.

### Contested-material search result

Dedicated searches in English, Spanish, French, German, and Italian for combinations of *Triangulum Minus* with “dispute,” “controversy,” “hoax,” “misidentification,” “error,” “obsolete,” and “abolished” found:

- the *Minus/Minor* grammatical error;
- ambiguity over 1687 versus 1690;
- loose descriptions of IAU “abolition”;
- retrospective transfer of ancient Triangulum myths;
- exaggerated claims that Hevelius “discovered” its stars;
- confusion with Triangulum Australe and objects named after modern Triangulum.

No hoax, forged chart, priority quarrel, patronage scandal, or competing claimant to the constellation’s invention was found.

## Open questions

1. **Hevelius’s exact prose rationale.** The catalogue and atlas prove the construction, but an explicit sentence by Hevelius explaining why he separated these particular stars has not been located. The geometrical motive is obvious but remains partly reconstructed.

2. **Earliest physical engraving date.** Individual sheets may have been printed before Hevelius’s death, while the complete work appeared in 1690. Copy-by-copy paper and plate-state analysis could sharpen the dating.

3. **Bode’s German terminology.** “Kleines Dreieck” is linguistically certain as a translation, but a precise page bearing it as Bode’s own formal heading remains to be established from a searchable transcription of his German catalogues.

4. **Detailed Chinese membership.** Modern reconstructions securely connect 10 and 6 Tri with Tiāndàjiāngjūn; the exact role of 12 Tri should be checked against primary Chinese charts and the Sun–Kistemaker identification tables.

5. **Pre-Hevelian chart appearances of the individual stars.** A systematic comparison of high-resolution Bayer, Brahe-derived, Schiller, Cellarius, and manuscript maps might establish which of the physical stars were plotted before they acquired a constellation identity.

6. **The prompt’s “830 stars in 109 groups.”** This could not be connected with Bode’s original atlas or catalogue. Identifying the named digital “Bode collection on chain” would require its collection title, contract address, catalogue page, or repository.
