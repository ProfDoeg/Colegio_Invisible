# Delphinus: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **Kept.** Delphinus is one of the 88 constellations recognized by the International Astronomical Union. The IAU adopted the name and abbreviation **Del** in 1922; Eugène Delporte’s boundaries, approved in 1928 and published in 1930, remain definitive. The Latin genitive is **Delphini**. No constituent figure was abolished, although modern boundaries replaced the overlapping, non-exclusive outlines used by Ptolemy, Bayer, Hevelius, Flamsteed, and Bode.  
>
> **Evidence labels used below:** **documented fact** = directly supported by a historical text, catalogue, object record, or modern scientific authority; **scholarly reconstruction** = a reasoned identification not explicitly stated by the historical witness; **tradition** = historically transmitted lore whose factual basis is not independently demonstrable; **disputed** = serious alternatives exist; **legend/myth** = supernatural or mythographical narrative; **modern invention** = a demonstrably recent name, story, or reinterpretation.

## Basic identification

### Name, grammar, and present status

**Documented fact.** The official name is **Delphinus**, “the Dolphin”; abbreviation **Del**; genitive **Delphini**. It occupies approximately **188.5 square degrees**, 69th in area among the 88 constellations. Its modern boundaries lie roughly between right ascensions 20h 14m and 21h 08m and declinations +2° and +20°30′, in coordinates referred by Delporte to equator and equinox B1875.0. It borders **Vulpecula, Sagitta, Aquila, Aquarius, Equuleus, and Pegasus**. The entire constellation is visible from terrestrial latitudes north of approximately 69° south. [IAU constellation table](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [IAU style book](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BA678AEF98DF86BF5CAD473B311432E6/S0251107X0001628Xa.pdf/chapter_viii_iau_style_book.pdf); [Delporte catalogue record](https://biblio-n.oca.eu/biblio/pmb3.0/opac_css/index.php?id=12723&lvl=notice_display).

**Documented fact.** The modern Chinese translation is **海豚座 Hǎitún-zuò**, “Dolphin Constellation”; French **le Dauphin**; Italian **il Delfino**; Spanish **el Delfín**; German **der Delphin**. These are translations of, or descendants from, the Greek-Latin animal name, not independent constellations.

### Greek and Latin names

**Documented fact.** Greek authors call the figure **Δελφίς** (*Delphís*, genitive *Delphînos*) or **Δελφίν** (*Delphín*), “dolphin.” Latin transliterated it as **Delphinus**, also **Delphin** and **Delphis**. The English *dolphin*, French *dauphin*, Italian *delfino*, and Spanish *delfín* descend through Latin and medieval Romance forms. Greek *delphís* has often been connected with **δελφύς, delphýs**, “womb,” perhaps because dolphins are viviparous or because of the animal’s rounded form; the semantic path is plausible but **etymologically uncertain**, not a proven derivation. [Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/dolphin); [Allen, *Star Names*, Delphinus](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Delphinus%2A.html).

Later Greek and Latin literary titles include:

- **Curvus**, “the curved one,” in Cicero’s Aratean tradition, referring to the dolphin’s arched back.
- **Musicum signum / Μουσικόν ζῴδιον**, “the musical sign,” from its association with Arion and possibly the nine Muses.
- **Vector Arionis**, “Arion’s bearer.”
- **Persuasor Amphitrites**, “persuader of Amphitrite.”
- **Neptunus** and **Triton**, loose mythographic substitutions rather than stable astronomical names.
- **Sīmon**, “snub-nosed,” reported in early-modern compilations as a mariners’ or “barbarian” name.

**Documented textual error.** Bayer printed **Currus**, “chariot,” while explaining the term from the animal’s curved or humped back. Richard Hinckley Allen correctly recognized it as a likely typographical corruption of Cicero’s **Curvus**. It never represented a genuine “Chariot” constellation.

### Bode’s labels and orientation

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode retained the classical Latin label **DELPHINUS** on plate X of the 1801 *Uranographia*. In German prose his ordinary name was **der Delphin**. The plate is headed “X. Delphinus … Coelum Stellatum” and includes Delphinus with Equuleus and Pegasus. The engraved dolphin is a compact, scaly, heraldic sea animal with a long snout, prominent eye, dorsal contour, paired fins, and curled tail. Unlike Hevelius’s external-globe orientation, Bode normally presented the sky in a direct observational arrangement.

Bode did not invent Delphinus. He inherited the ancient constellation while surrounding it with the much denser stellar content of late eighteenth-century catalogues. His accompanying *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* catalogued **17,240 stars, doubles, nebulae, and clusters** across the atlas. The prompt’s “830 stars in 109 groups” appears to describe a particular collection or digital “Bode collection” chain rather than Bode’s published total; I could not trace it to the original title pages or catalogue and therefore do not treat it as a property of Delphinus. [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [David Rumsey plate X](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~283915~90056550%3AX--Delphinus----Coelum-Stellatum-); [Marseille copy](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035).

## Stars and objects

### The classical and modern figure

The conspicuous modern outline consists of a four-star lozenge plus a tail:

- **α Delphini — Sualocin — Flamsteed 9 Delphini**
- **β Delphini — Rotanev — 6 Delphini**
- **γ Delphini — 12 Delphini**, commonly treated as the dolphin’s snout
- **δ Delphini — 11 Delphini**
- **ε Delphini — Aldulfin — 2 Delphini**, extending the tail

The four-star α–β–γ–δ diamond is the principal naked-eye asterism. In English-speaking popular astronomy it became **Job’s Coffin**; with ε it makes a recognizable dolphin-like outline.

### Ptolemy’s ten stars

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy’s *Almagest* VII.5–VIII.1 catalogues **ten** stars under Delphinus, describing three in the tail, stars on the body and fins, and stars around the head or snout. Modern identifications are reconstructions from the descriptions and coordinates; the principal correspondences are approximately:

1. ε Delphini, the foremost of three tail stars  
2. a faint star in the tail region, usually identified with 4 Delphini or a nearby naked-eye star  
3. another faint tail star, generally 1 or 3 Delphini  
4. β Delphini, on the body  
5. α Delphini, on the body  
6. δ Delphini, on the body or northern side  
7. γ Delphini, at the mouth or snout, with the naked-eye pair sometimes treated as one catalogue object  
8–10. faint stars in or near the fins and southern outline

**Contested identification.** Exact one-to-one equivalences vary between Peters and Knobel, modern Hipparcos-based revisions, and popular tables because Ptolemy’s coordinates contain errors and because naked-eye doubles do not map neatly onto modern components. The secure conclusion is the count and anatomical descriptions, not every modern HD/Bayer assignment. [Toomer’s *Almagest* scan](https://www.mathship.hlevkin.com/900/Astronomy/PtolemyAlmagest.pdf); [Peters and Knobel 1915](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/Montignot%20Knobel%20Ptolemy%27s%20cat%201915.pdf); [Spanish catalogue transcription](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Almagesto%3A_Libro_VII_-_Cap%C3%ADtulo_05).

### Bayer letters and Flamsteed numbers

**Documented fact.** Bayer’s *Uranometria* (Augsburg, 1603) assigned the principal Greek letters **α through ε** and further letters to stars in and around the figure. Flamsteed’s numbered sequence ultimately supplied **1–18 Delphini**; modern lists sometimes show 19 Bayer/Flamsteed-labelled entries because components and historical designations overlap.

The principal traditional figure has:

### Proper names

#### Sualocin

**Documented modern invention.** **Sualocin**, now the IAU-approved proper name for α Delphini Aa, first appeared in Giuseppe Piazzi’s Palermo star catalogue of **1814**. Read backward it gives **Nicolaus**.

#### Rotanev

**Documented modern invention.** **Rotanev**, now official for β Delphini A, appeared in the same catalogue. Backward it gives **Venator**, Latin “hunter.”

Together:

> **SUALOCIN ROTANEV → NICOLAUS VENATOR**

This is the Latinized form of **Niccolò Cacciatore**: Italian *cacciatore* means “hunter.” Cacciatore was Piazzi’s assistant and later his successor as director at Palermo.

**Disputed attribution.** The reversal is certain; who inserted it is not. Cacciatore is the encoded honoree and likely participant, but the surviving catalogue does not state whether Cacciatore, Piazzi, or another Palermo colleague devised or authorized it. Hence “Cacciatore secretly named the stars after himself” is likely but not documented as a confession.

**Correction to a popular superlative.** The claim that these are “the only stars named for a living assistant by a trick” is colorful but not demonstrable. Other covert personal names exist—notably the reversed Apollo navigation names **Dnoces, Navi, Regor**—and commercial or unofficial personal names are innumerable. Sualocin and Rotanev are, however, an exceptional early catalogue case and were made official by the IAU Working Group on Star Names in 2016.

**Discovery of the trick.** Thomas William Webb publicized the reversal around **1859**, roughly 45 years after the catalogue appeared. Earlier writers, including Smyth, had repeated the opaque names without solving them.

**Spurious etymologies.** Frances Rolleston’s *Mazzaroth* derived Sualocin from an alleged Arabic *Scalooin*, “swift,” and Rotanev from alleged Syriac/Chaldean forms meaning “swiftly running.” No credible Arabic or Syriac textual basis has been found; the exact reversal of *Nicolaus Venator* explains both names without residue. Burritt’s **Scalovin** and **Rotanen** are corrupt spellings, not independent ancient names. [IAU star-name list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/naming_stars/); [Allen](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Delphinus%2A.html); [bibliographical discussion of the Palermo catalogue](https://www.abaa.org/assets/newsletter_pdf/Lemley%2C_Samuel_-_Biblioteca_Genealogica_Sicilian_Printing%2C_1704-1893_-_NCBCC2018.pdf).

#### Aldulfin

**Documented Arabic-derived name.** The IAU proper name **Aldulfin** belongs to ε Delphini. It is a contracted European form of Arabic **ذنب الدلفين dhanab al-dulfīn**, “the dolphin’s tail,” also transmitted as **Deneb Dulfim**, **Al Dhanab al Dulfim**, and Latin **Cauda Delphini**.

The word *dulfīn/dālfīn* is itself an Arabic adaptation of Greek *delphín*, not an indigenous Arabian animal-name origin for the Western constellation.

#### Gamma Delphini

γ Delphini has no traditional IAU-approved proper name. It is a celebrated contrasting telescopic pair, γ¹ and γ², often described visually as yellow and orange or gold. The informal name **“Derf”**, used in twentieth-century Hayden Planetarium lectures by Fred Hess—*Fred* reversed—was a joke modeled on Sualocin and Rotanev and never became official.

#### Musica and Arion

**Documented modern invention.** In the IAU’s 2015 NameExoWorlds campaign, the star **18 Delphini** was named **Musica**, reflecting the constellation’s Arion tradition, and its exoplanet **18 Delphini b** was named **Arion**. These are modern commemorative names, not survivals from Greece.

### Variable and unusual stars

- **δ Delphini** is the prototype of the **Delta Scuti**-like historical subgroup once called dwarf Cepheids in older literature; it is a low-amplitude pulsating variable and spectroscopic system.
- **R Delphini** is a long-period Mira-type variable.
- **U Delphini** is a semiregular red variable.
- **EU Delphini** and numerous fainter variables lie within the modern boundary.
- **W Delphini** is an eclipsing binary.
- **V339 Delphini**, Nova Delphini 2013, was discovered by Koichi Itagaki on 14 August 2013 and became visible to the unaided eye at about magnitude 4.3–4.4.
- **HR Delphini**, Nova Delphini 1967, is treated separately below.

A complete modern variable-star inventory runs into hundreds once survey discoveries are counted; the historical dossier should not mistake the best-known named variables for a finite constellation membership list.

### Nova Delphini 1967: HR Delphini

**Documented fact.** British amateur observer **George Eric Deacon Alcock** discovered the nova on **8 July 1967**, near fifth magnitude, after an extensive binocular search. The contemporary RASC announcement cites IAU Circulars 2024–2025 and gives B1950 coordinates RA 20h 40m 04.24s, Dec +18° 58′ 51″. Earlier photographs showed that it was already brightening by late June.

It rose extraordinarily slowly, hovering near fifth magnitude before reaching approximately magnitude **3.5–3.7 in December 1967**. Its time to decline by three magnitudes was about 225–230 days, making it a very slow classical nova. It later displayed a resolved bipolar or prolate shell and remains unusually luminous in ultraviolet observations. [Contemporary RASC announcement](https://www.rasc.ca/670801announcement13); [1970 photometry](https://adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1970PASP...82..626M); [remnant study](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/the-structure-of-the-remnant-of-hr-del/); [long-term study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1007.0932).

**Correction.** The designation **HR Del** does not mean “Harvard Revised star”; here HR is the variable-star designation formed after HQ–HZ Delphini in the GCVS sequence. Confusion with Bright Star Catalogue “HR numbers” is a recurring popular error.

### Supernovae

**Documented absence.** No historically recorded naked-eye Galactic supernova is securely assigned to Delphinus. Supernovae have occurred in galaxies that now lie within its boundaries, but none is a culturally defining “Supernova Delphini” comparable with the historical events in Taurus, Cassiopeia, or Ophiuchus.

### Deep-sky objects

Delphinus contains **no Messier object**.

Its best-known deep-sky objects include:

- **NGC 6934** — globular cluster, discovered by William Herschel on 24 September 1785; about magnitude 8.8–9.8 depending on aperture and integrated photometric source.
- **NGC 7006 / Caldwell 42** — remote Galactic globular cluster, discovered by William Herschel on 21 August 1784; conspicuously distant in the Galactic halo. [NASA Caldwell 42](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubble-caldwell-catalog/caldwell-42/).
- **NGC 6905**, the **Blue Flash Nebula** — planetary nebula near the Delphinus–Sagitta border; discovered by William Herschel in 1784. The nickname is modern observing lore, not Herschel’s.
- **NGC 6891** — compact planetary nebula, discovered by Ralph Copeland in 1884.
- **NGC 6886** — small planetary nebula, discovered by Ralph Copeland in 1884.
- **NGC 6928**, **NGC 6927**, **NGC 6930**, and related systems — galaxies forming an attractive small visual field sometimes called the NGC 6928 group.
- **NGC 6956** — barred spiral galaxy.
- **NGC 7025** — lenticular or early-type spiral galaxy.
- Numerous PGC galaxies, planetary nebulae, open-cluster candidates, and galaxy groups fall inside Delporte’s lines.

“Delphinus Cluster” is not a unique formal deep-sky name: authors and observers sometimes apply it descriptively to NGC 6934 or other fields, so an object number is essential.

### Meteor showers

**Documented fact with nomenclatural caution.** The IAU Meteor Data Center contains several proposed Delphinid radiants, including **γ-Delphinids** and **13-Delphinids**, plus recent candidate streams. Most remain on the **Working List**, not the much smaller Established List. “The Delphinids” therefore does not designate one strong, universally recognized annual shower. [IAU MDC Working List](https://iaumdc.astro.sk/working).

The spectacular 11 June 1930 meteor event sometimes called the **Delphinid meteor storm** has an uncertain radiant and stream identity. Later “July Delphinids” and γ-Delphinids are weak or episodic. Popular tables stating “no meteor shower” mean no major established shower, not literally no reported radiant.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before Greece

**No secure Babylonian antecedent.** Babylonian star catalogues, especially *MUL.APIN* in its first-millennium-BCE form, profoundly influenced Greek uranography, but no securely identified Babylonian “Dolphin” using the compact α–ε Delphini group has been established. Attempts to derive the Greek dolphin from Euphratean sea symbolism are nineteenth-century diffusionist reconstructions, especially in Robert Brown’s writings, not readings compelled by a cuneiform star name.

**Possible Egyptian asterism: disputed reconstruction.** Some modern reconstructions of the Dendera and coffin skies place an Egyptian **dead animal/carcass** or a decanal cluster in stars now assigned to Delphinus. The surviving Egyptian images do not label a dolphin equivalent to Greek Delphinus, and published star identifications disagree. It is therefore improper to call this an Egyptian antecedent of the Dolphin. [Priskin, Egyptian astronomical diagrams](https://www.enim-egyptologie.fr/revue/2019/8/Priskin_ENiM12_p137-180.pdf); [RASC World Asterisms handbook](https://www.rasc.ca/sites/default/files/World%20Asterisms%20Project%20Handbook%20V%202024.4.pdf).

### Fifth century BCE: the Arion story

**Earliest surviving dolphin narrative relevant to the constellation, but not yet a catasterism.** Herodotus, writing around 430 BCE, records at *Histories* **1.23–24** what “the Corinthians say, and the Lesbians agree”: Arion of Methymna, threatened by sailors seeking his wealth, put on his ceremonial costume, sang to his cithara, leapt into the sea, and was carried to Taenarum by a dolphin. Herodotus says a small bronze dedication of a man riding a dolphin stood at Taenarum.

Herodotus does **not** say in this passage that the dolphin became the constellation. That celestial step is a later mythographic attachment. His careful attribution makes the episode documented as a fifth-century tradition, not as a zoological event. [Herodotus 1.23–24](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0126%3Abook%3D1&force=y).

### Fourth century BCE: Eudoxan sky

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Delphinus was probably present in Eudoxus of Cnidus’s lost *Phaenomena* and *Enoptron*, the principal prose sources behind Aratus. Since the works survive only through later witnesses, the exact wording and any Eudoxan myth cannot be recovered.

### Early third century BCE: Aratus

**Documented fact.** Aratus’s *Phaenomena*, approximately 275 BCE, describes a small Dolphin below or near the Eagle, “small to sight,” marked by few stars. Aratus gives no origin myth. This is the earliest surviving unmistakable literary attestation of Delphinus as a constellation.

The familiar claim “Aratus said it was Arion’s dolphin” is false: that belongs to scholia and later mythographers, not the poem itself.

### Third–second century BCE: Eratosthenian tradition

**Myth/catasterism.** *Catasterismi* 31, transmitted under Eratosthenes’ name but surviving in later epitomes and fragments, says Poseidon wished to marry Amphitrite, who fled and hid, commonly at Atlas’s domain. Searchers failed until a dolphin found her, persuaded her to accept Poseidon, and conducted the marriage. In gratitude Poseidon placed the dolphin among the stars.

The chapter’s star count is commonly **nine**, arranged in the creature. Hyginus explicitly attributes this account to “Eratosthenes and others.”

**Textual caution.** The extant *Catasterismi* is not Eratosthenes’ autograph. The fuller recension, Epitome, and *Fragmenta Vaticana* differ in details. “Eratosthenes 31” therefore means the transmitted Eratosthenian corpus, not a securely verbatim third-century-BCE text. Modern critical editions are Olivieri (1897), Pàmias (2004), and Pàmias–Geus (2007).

### Second century BCE: Hipparchus

**Documented fact.** Hipparchus discussed Delphinus in his commentary on Aratus and Eudoxus, criticizing or clarifying stellar positions. Later writers sometimes suggest that he carved Equuleus out of stars previously belonging to a larger Delphinus.

**Disputed reconstruction.** Ptolemy catalogued Equuleus separately, but no surviving statement proves that Hipparchus literally “took” its stars from Delphinus. Allen’s suggestion rests on spatial proximity and catalogue history.

### First century BCE–first century CE: Roman poetry

**Documented fact.** Cicero’s fragmentary Latin Aratea calls the creature curved, the source of **Curvus**.

**Documented fact.** Germanicus’s *Aratea*, composed around the turn of the era, describes the small stellar dolphin and alludes to the Amphitrite episode. The associated scholia expand the catasterism from Eratosthenian material. It is unsafe to attribute every scholion to Germanicus himself.

**Documented fact.** Manilius, *Astronomica* **1.346–348** approximately, includes Delphinus in the constellation survey, raised from the sea to become “the glory of the flood and of the stars.” In book **5**, his paranatellonta astrology gives those born under the Dolphin bodily speed and affinity with maritime or aquatic pursuits. Manilius does not independently narrate the full Arion rescue.

### Ovid

**Myth and astronomical calendar.** Ovid, *Fasti* **2.79–118**, makes Delphinus set in early February and offers two causes: it was either the fortunate intermediary in a love affair—Amphitrite—or the bearer of “the Lesbian lyre and its master,” Arion. He narrates Arion’s Sicilian and Italian success, the sailors’ plot, his final song, leap, rescue on the dolphin’s curved back, arrival before the ship, and exposure of the criminals. Jupiter gives the dolphin nine stars.

This is the earliest surviving full, explicit conjunction of the Arion story with Delphinus’s catasterism. [Ovid, *Fasti* 2.79–118](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti2.html); [French text and commentary](https://bcs.fltr.ucl.ac.be/fastam/F2-001-242.html).

### Hyginus

**Myth/catasterism.** Pseudo-Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* **2.17**, preserves several alternatives:

1. **Amphitrite:** Neptune desires to marry her; she flees to Atlas; Delphinus finds and persuades her, manages the wedding, and is placed among the stars. Statues put a dolphin in Neptune’s hand or beneath his foot in commemoration.
2. **Arion:** the musician is threatened by sailors; dolphins gather at his music; he leaps overboard and one carries him to shore. The celestial dolphin commemorates the rescue.
3. **Dionysus and the pirates:** Bacchus, captured by Tyrrhenian pirates, transforms them into dolphins; one version associates the constellation with the pilot or compassionate figure who resisted the crime.

Hyginus’s *Fabulae* **134** narrates the Tyrrhenian pirates and Dionysus, while **194** treats Arion. The precise chapter numbering can vary among editions.

**Correction.** Hyginus is not securely “a Roman poet” and is probably not Gaius Julius Hyginus, Augustus’s librarian. Both the authorship and date of the surviving astronomical handbook are uncertain, commonly assigned between the first and second centuries CE.

### Ptolemy, about 150 CE

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogues Delphinus as northern constellation XVII with **ten stars**. It is therefore one of the conventional “48 Ptolemaic constellations,” although Ptolemy did not invent it.

**Astrology.** In *Tetrabiblos* **2.7**, Ptolemy groups sea-associated configurations—including Cancer, Capricorn, and Delphinus—with events involving the sea and the departure or movement of fleets. He does not assign Delphinus’s individual stars a detailed planetary temperament comparable with prominent zodiacal stars. Later lists that claim a universal Saturn/Mars nature for all Delphinus stars are post-Ptolemaic generalizations.

### Late antiquity

**Documented tradition.** Avienus translated and expanded Aratus; scholia to Aratus and Germanicus circulated the Amphitrite and Arion explanations. Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* **45**, elaborates Dionysus’s sea adventures and dolphin transformations, but does not securely establish a new Delphinus catasterism. The marine animal’s pervasive Dionysian, Apollonian, and Poseidonian symbolism encouraged later mythographers to merge otherwise separate dolphin stories.

### Tenth century: al-Ṣūfī

**Documented fact.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita*, completed about **964**, calls it **Ṣūrat al-Dālfīn**, “the Figure of the Dolphin.” *Dālfīn/dulfīn* is the Arabicized Greek name. He preserved Ptolemy’s ten-star organization, corrected magnitudes and descriptions from observation, and illustrated each constellation twice: as seen in the sky and as seen on a globe.

Al-Ṣūfī distinguished this imported Ptolemaic Dolphin from indigenous Arabian star lore. [Al-Ṣūfī constellation index](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_constellations.htm); [BnF manuscript display](https://expositions.bnf.fr/monde-en-spheres/grand/arabe_5036_088v-089.php).

### Medieval Latin transmission

**Documented fact.** Gerard of Cremona’s twelfth-century Latin translation of the *Almagest* transmitted the catalogue through Arabic, producing such forms as **Delphin**, **Delphinus**, and Arabic-influenced stellar descriptions. The *Ṣūfī Latinus* and Alfonsine traditions corrected longitudes for precession while retaining the animal.

Vernacular forms include Chaucer’s **Delphyn** and later English **Dolphyne/Dolphin**. The constellation is “Delphienus” in a poem attributed to James VI and I. These are linguistic developments, not different sky figures. [University of Michigan Gerard page](https://www.early-astronomy-um.org/ancient_ptolemy2.php).

### 1515: Dürer, Stabius, and Heinfogel

**Documented fact.** The northern celestial hemisphere woodcut designed by Johann Stabius, with stars placed by Conrad Heinfogel and figures drawn by Albrecht Dürer, includes the traditional dolphin. It is among the first printed European star maps. Its external-globe convention reverses the sky as an observer sees it.

### 1603: Bayer

**Documented fact.** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* gave Delphinus a dedicated artistic figure and Greek-letter designations. It helped stabilize α–ε Delphini in European practice. Bayer did not invent either the constellation or the letters’ modern brightness ordering: within many constellations his sequence follows figure position, catalogue order, or magnitude classes rather than exact brightness.

### 1627: Schiller

**Documented Christian reinvention.** Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Augsburg 1627, attempted to erase pagan figures and replace them with biblical subjects. In the Delphinus region he placed part of the **Hydriae Canae**, the **Water Jars of Cana**, associated in the atlas’s paired northern plates with Marian imagery, including the Rose of Mary. The primary reference is John **2:1–11**, the wedding at Cana.

The concise statement “Delphinus became the Water Jar of Cana” is substantially correct, although Schiller’s compositions can span more than one pagan constellation and the plates’ titles should be read collectively rather than forced into a one-for-one substitution. Schiller’s system was admired as art but not adopted for ordinary astronomy. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbc0001.2017rosen1317/?sp=1&st=gallery); [ETH record](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720).

### 1690: Hevelius

**Documented fact.** Johannes Hevelius’s *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*, published posthumously at Danzig by Elisabeth Hevelius, portrays **Sagitta, Delphinus et Equuleus** together. It uses the external-globe view and a richly modeled sea creature. His catalogue contains 1,564 entries overall. [Hevelius plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AJohannes_Hevelius_-_Prodromus_Astronomia_-_Volume_III_%22Firmamentum_Sobiescianum%2C_sive_uranographia%22_-_Tavola_S_-_Sagitta_Delphinus_et_Equuleus.jpg); [Hevelius catalogue study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.3841).

### Flamsteed, 1729

**Documented fact.** John Flamsteed’s posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* preserved Delphinus and supplied the catalogue ordering from which the later Flamsteed numbers were extracted. Contrary to a common shorthand, Flamsteed himself did not consistently print today’s numbers beside every star; the numbered system was regularized through later editions of his catalogue.

### Bode, 1782 and 1801

Bode’s **1782** *Vorstellung der Gestirne* adapted the reduced French Flamsteed atlas issued by Fortin, corrected positions, and made constellation imagery available to educated amateurs.

His **1801 Uranographia**, published at Berlin, placed Delphinus on plate X. It was one of the last monumental atlases to combine scientific star plotting with full pictorial figures. Bode outlined constellation territories, but these were cartographic conventions, not yet internationally binding borders. [Heidelberg account](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/einl.html).

### 1814: Palermo names

Piazzi’s 1814 Palermo catalogue introduced **Sualocin** and **Rotanev**, encoding Cacciatore. Their continued use is a rare case of a catalogue joke becoming official nomenclature.

### 1822–1825: Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* (London, 1822) depicted Delphinus in a softened neoclassical style. The anonymous cards of *Urania’s Mirror*, issued in 1824/1825 and engraved by Sidney Hall, copied Jamieson closely. Card **13** combines **Delphinus, Sagitta, Aquila, and Antinous**, with star holes intended to shine when held before light.

The cards’ authorship was advertised as “by a lady.” Peter Hingley’s 1994 archival study identified the likely designer as the Reverend **Richard Rouse Bloxam**. The disguise and the close copying from Jamieson are part of the work’s own controversy. [Library of Congress card set](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2013645050/); [RAS account](https://www.women.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror).

### Nineteenth-century catalogues

Burritt counted **18**, Argelander **20**, and Heis **31** stars under Delphinus, reflecting progressively deeper naked-eye catalogues and different membership rules, not the physical acquisition of new stars. Argelander and Heis stripped atlas figures toward scientific charting; the dolphin survived as a constellation name while its anatomical outline lost official function.

### 1922–1930: IAU settlement

**Documented fact.** At its first General Assembly in Rome in 1922, the IAU standardized the list of 88 constellations and their three-letter abbreviations. Delphinus was retained as **Del**.

Delporte subsequently drew complete, non-overlapping boundaries along B1875 right-ascension and declination lines. The boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* in 1930.

**Boundary effect.** Earlier figures could overlap or leave unassigned spaces; after Delporte, every coordinate belonged to exactly one constellation. Delphinus’s historic core remained intact. Marginal stars and deep-sky objects were legally allocated among Delphinus, Pegasus, Equuleus, Aquarius, Aquila, Sagitta, and Vulpecula. No famous principal dolphin star was lost.

## Classical myths and related dolphin legends

### 1. The dolphin that found Amphitrite

**Myth; first surviving celestial form in the Eratosthenian corpus, probably Hellenistic.** Poseidon sought Amphitrite, but she fled his marriage proposal and hid—Hyginus says with Atlas. Poseidon sent many searchers. A dolphin found her among the islands, persuaded her to consent, and escorted or organized the marriage. Poseidon rewarded the animal by placing it among the stars.

Sources: Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 31; Pseudo-Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.17; allusion in Ovid, *Fasti* 2.81; scholia to Aratus and Germanicus. The named individual **Delphin/Delphinus** is sometimes treated as a sea-daemon rather than an ordinary animal.

This is the most explicit etiological explanation of why Poseidon’s art regularly includes a dolphin underfoot or in hand. Hyginus makes that iconographic connection directly. [Hyginus translation and source extracts](https://www.theoi.com/Ther/Delphin.html).

### 2. Arion of Methymna

**Legend, later catasterism.** Herodotus’s fifth-century-BCE version is the earliest. Arion, a famous citharode and alleged inventor or organizer of the dithyramb, became wealthy in Italy and Sicily. Returning from Tarentum on a Corinthian ship, he learned that the crew intended to kill him and take his money. Allowed a last performance, he dressed in ceremonial costume, sang, and leapt into the sea. A dolphin carried him to Taenarum. He reached Corinth before the ship; Periander detained the sailors until Arion confronted them.

Herodotus’s reported statue at Taenarum functions as material authentication inside his narrative, but not modern proof that the rescue happened. The story belongs to widespread folklore about dolphins rescuing humans.

The celestial sequel appears explicitly in Ovid: Jupiter placed the dolphin among the stars and gave it nine stars. Hyginus also transmits the astronomical connection. Later names **Vector Arionis**, “Arion’s carrier,” and **Musicum signum** preserve it.

### 3. Dionysus and the Tyrrhenian pirates

**Myth; early poetic form in the Homeric Hymn to Dionysus, later connected to Delphinus.** Pirates seize Dionysus, mistaking him for a prince worth ransom. Miracles reveal the god: vines overgrow the ship, wine flows, and Dionysus assumes a terrifying form. The sailors leap into the sea and become dolphins. The helmsman—often Acoetes in Latin tradition—recognizes the god or refuses to join the violence and is spared.

Sources include *Homeric Hymn to Dionysus* 7; Euripidean and Hellenistic allusions; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* **3.582–691**; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 134 and *Astronomica* 2.17; Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*.

**Contested catasterism.** The transformation myth is old, but identifying one of these dolphins with the constellation is a later mythographical graft. Hyginus’s compressed alternatives enabled early-modern writers to list **Acoetes/Acetes** as a name of Delphinus, although Acoetes himself was the spared helmsman, not normally the transformed dolphin.

### 4. Apollo Delphinios and the Cretan sailors

**Myth with cultic evidence; astronomical connection late and speculative.** In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* **388–544**, Apollo takes dolphin form, leaps aboard a Cretan ship, and guides its sailors to Crisa, where they become his priests at Delphi. This explains Apollo **Delphinios** within the hymn’s wordplay.

Early-modern compilers sometimes said Delphinus represented Apollo returning to Crisa or carrying the Cretan leader Castalius. No early source securely says this dolphin was placed among the stars. The association is therefore a legitimate classical dolphin myth but a **late proposed identity** for the constellation.

### 5. Palaemon/Melicertes

**Myth, no early secure catasterism.** Ino leaps into the sea with Melicertes; sea gods transform or receive them as Leucothea and Palaemon. A dolphin carries the child’s body to the Isthmus, where Sisyphus establishes the Isthmian games. Sources include Pausanias **1.44.7–8; 2.1.3**, Ovid, *Metamorphoses* **4.416–542**, and mythographic summaries.

Dolphins bearing Palaemon appear on ancient coins and art. Nineteenth-century authors sometimes invoked this story in general Delphinus lore, but no surviving early catasterism identifies the constellation as Palaemon’s carrier.

### 6. Taras and Phalanthus

**Legend and civic iconography.** Tarentine foundation tradition shows the hero **Taras**, son of Poseidon, riding a dolphin; another version has a dolphin rescue the Spartan founder Phalanthus after shipwreck. Tarentum/Taras placed the dolphin rider on its coins from the classical period. Pausanias **10.13.10** associates Tarentine dedications with the colonial story.

This imagery provides a striking parallel to Arion’s ride from the Tarentine region but is not an ancient explanation of the constellation. Conflating the coin type with Arion is an iconographic error.

### 7. Enalus and other dolphin rescues

Plutarch’s *Banquet of the Seven Sages* and related Lesbian traditions tell of **Enalus**, who follows a maiden cast into the sea and is saved or aided by dolphins. Pliny, *Natural History* **9.8–11**, Aelian, *On Animals*, and Oppian, *Halieutica*, collect stories of dolphins loving music, escorting ships, mourning companions, and rescuing boys.

These accounts explain why Arion’s carrier was culturally plausible and why the dolphin became a beneficent sea animal. None securely supplies an additional ancient Delphinus catasterism.

### 8. Poseidon, Aphrodite, and general dolphin symbolism

Dolphins accompany Poseidon/Neptune, Amphitrite, Tritons, Nereids, Aphrodite’s sea birth, and Eros in Greek and Roman art. They signify the sea, safe passage, speed, erotic attraction, and sometimes passage of souls.

**Interpretive caution.** These meanings belong to the animal’s iconography generally. Claims that Delphinus itself was a universal funerary “soul carrier,” an Atlantean emblem, or an encoded mystery symbol are modern Hermetic extrapolations unless tied to a specific ancient text or monument.

### Authors requested but yielding no independent Delphinus catasterism

- **Pseudo-Apollodorus:** preserves Poseidon, Amphitrite, Dionysus, and related genealogies but no independent constellation chapter for Delphinus.
- **Pausanias:** supplies dolphin-rider monuments and local traditions, especially Palaemon and Taras, but not a securely separate Delphinus origin.
- **Nonnus:** elaborates Dionysian dolphin transformations, not a demonstrably new constellation identity.
- **Manilius:** supplies poetic and astrological interpretation, not a full competing catasterism.
- **Aratus:** names and places the Dolphin without myth.
- **Germanicus:** alludes to or receives the Amphitrite tradition; the fuller narratives belong largely to scholia.
- **Hyginus’s *Fabulae*:** supplies parallel myth narratives; *Astronomica* 2.17 is the central constellation witness.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Indigenous Arabian sky

Al-Ṣūfī records the imported constellation as **al-Dālfīn**, but the same stars participated in older Arabian patterns.

**Tradition, recorded in the medieval Arabic astronomical literature.** The four bright stars corresponding broadly to the Delphinus diamond were likened to a **camel**, commonly rendered “the Riding Camel.” Sources and later translations differ over whether the pattern was a she-camel, a camel bearing a rider, or part of a broader camel group. The camel did not arise by translating Greek “dolphin”; it represents an independent reading of the same stars.

The Arabic tail description **dhanab al-dulfīn** belongs to the Ptolemaic figure and produced Aldulfin. It must not be confused with the indigenous camel asterism.

**Persian.** Al-Ṣūfī was a Persian astronomer writing in Arabic at the Buyid court. Persian manuscripts of his work reproduce **دلفین delfīn**, a loan ultimately from Greek, alongside Arabic astronomical terminology. I found no securely documented pre-Islamic Persian dolphin constellation in these stars.

### Chinese astronomy

The modern Delphinus region belongs to the **Black Tortoise of the North** and is divided principally between two asterisms in the **Girl (Nǚ)** lunar mansion:

- **瓠瓜 Hùguā**, “Good Gourd”: α Del, γ² Del, δ Del, β Del, and ζ Del, with later added stars.
- **敗瓜 Bàiguā**, “Rotten Gourd”: ε, η, θ, ι, and κ Del, plus added stars.

Part of the southern or eastern modern constellation also touches the area of **虛 Xū**, “Emptiness,” with **司非 Sīfēi**, “Official/Judge of Right and Wrong”; 18 Delphini is catalogued as an added star to Sīfēi in later Chinese lists.

**Documented difference.** There was no traditional Chinese dolphin here. **海豚座** is the modern translation of the Western IAU constellation. [Chinese star mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphinus_in_Chinese_astronomy).

### Indian traditions

**Disputed equivalence.** Sanskrit **Śiśumāra** means a dolphin, porpoise, or aquatic creature, and Vedic-Purāṇic texts describe the heavens or the rotating celestial order as a śiśumāra. However, the best-attested **Śiśumāra-cakra** is a large circumpolar configuration centered on Dhruva, often identified broadly with Draco or the northern sky—not the small Greek Delphinus.

Nineteenth-century writers who say “the Hindus called Delphinus Śiśumāra” collapse these distinct traditions. Some later Indian star charts translated the Western constellation as a dolphin; that does not prove the Greek figure was borrowed from India or vice versa. A recent comparative study explicitly warns that the Vedic celestial dolphin is not necessarily Greek Delphinus. [*Dolphin Deified*](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/journals/ejvs/article/download/10613/10447/23247).

Delphinus lies well north of the ecliptic. No principal Vedic **nakṣatra** is formed by its α–ε diamond, though longitude-based sectors projected pole-to-pole can “cross” it in modern astrological software. Such projection is not the same as a traditional nakṣatra identification.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

**Absence of secure ancient evidence.** No biblical Hebrew constellation can be reliably identified with the Delphinus diamond. Biblical *tannīn*, Leviathan, and Jonah’s “great fish” are not Delphinus.

Medieval and early-modern Christian Hebraists attempted to map biblical figures onto every Greco-Roman constellation. Delphinus was sometimes moralized as Jonah’s fish, a fish of salvation, or a funerary emblem. These are Christianized mappings, not demonstrated Jewish antiquity.

**Kabbalistic claims.** Modern occult charts occasionally associate Delphinus with Hebrew letters, tribal emblems, paths of the Tree of Life, or the watery zodiacal signs. I found no stable mapping in the *Sefer Yetzirah*, *Zohar*, or classical Jewish lunar-mansion literature. Such assignments should be labelled modern esoteric systems unless their specific author and edition are given.

### Job’s Coffin

**English-language tradition, origin unknown.** The α–β–γ–δ diamond is widely called **Job’s Coffin**. It is not in Job, Greek myth, medieval Arabic catalogues, Bayer, Hevelius, or Bode.

Elijah Burritt’s *Geography of the Heavens* in the **1830s** already says it was known “to many” but admits he could not discover by whom or from what fancy it arose. Later Garrett Serviss and William Tyler Olcott repeated the uncertainty. Allen likewise could not identify its inventor.

The form is visually coffin-like, but why it is Job’s rather than another biblical figure’s remains unknown. The suggestion that it is a corruption of **“Jonah’s Coffin”**, linking Jonah’s three days in a great fish to entombment, is a **recent conjecture**, not supported by an early printed “Jonah’s Coffin” form. The safest first-attestation statement is: widespread vernacular use by the early nineteenth century; inventor and precise origin untraced. [Allen](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Delphinus%2A.html); [1911 *Star Lore of All Ages*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/17/Star_Lore_Of_All_Ages%2C_1911.pdf).

### Christian sky

Schiller’s **Water Jars of Cana** are the only well-documented systematic Christian replacement in the requested tradition. Later devotional atlases occasionally made the Dolphin Jonah’s fish or a baptismal symbol, but no replacement displaced Delphinus in scientific use.

### Astrology, magic, and lunar mansions

Ptolemy’s secure statement is mundane: watery configurations affect maritime affairs and fleets (*Tetrabiblos* 2.7).

Manilius’s book 5 associates the rising Dolphin with swift movement, swimming, sea work, and dolphin-like physical capacity. This is poetic astral determinism, not modern empirical science.

Delphinus contains **none of the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars**. Claims that Sualocin or Rotanev are Behenian are erroneous.

Because Delphinus lies about 30° north of the ecliptic, no classical Babylonian, Arabic, Indian, or Chinese lunar mansion is centered upon its diamond. Chinese Hùguā and Bàiguā are assigned within the broad celestial sector of the Girl mansion, but they are not themselves mansion determinators. Modern astrologers sometimes interpret Sualocin and Rotanev by projected ecliptic longitude; these doctrines postdate the Palermo names and cannot be ancient.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

Dolphins occur in emblem books as symbols of speed moderated by restraint—especially through the **festina lente** device of dolphin and anchor—and as agents of salvation, music, or mercurial mediation. Early-modern writers could apply those motifs to Delphinus.

I found no canonical alchemical text that assigns a unique operation, metal, or stage of the Great Work specifically to the constellation Delphinus. Websites presenting a fixed Delphinus–Mercury, Delphinus–silver, or Delphinus–Atlantis correspondence are modern syntheses unless they cite a particular historical author.

## Boundary and membership history

### Ptolemy

Ptolemy supplied a list of ten stars “in the figure.” Constellations were catalogue groupings and images, not exhaustive polygons. Stars outside figures could be entered as **amorphōtoi**, “unformed” stars, and a physical point in the sky did not have the modern legal membership concept.

### Bayer

Bayer expanded the labelled star field and imposed Greek letters. His plate’s pictorial body—not a mathematical boundary—determined visual membership. The lettering established the modern core but not an exact perimeter.

### Hevelius

Hevelius catalogued fainter stars and drew Delphinus beside Sagitta and Equuleus. His reversed external-globe view sometimes makes comparisons with Bayer or Bode appear as though stars shifted sides; much of that difference is projection, not reassignment.

### Flamsteed

Flamsteed’s observations expanded the numbered population. Later editors derived 1–18 Delphini from catalogue order. Numbers do not trace the dolphin’s anatomy as neatly as Bayer letters.

### Lacaille

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille invented many southern constellations but **did not alter or invent Delphinus**. It lies outside his principal southern reform. Lists that place Delphinus among Lacaille’s creations confuse the ancient figure with his instrument constellations.

### Bode

Bode showed outlined constellation territories, dense faint-star populations, and the traditional animal. His boundaries were influential graphic guides but lacked international authority. Plate edges and colored outlines should not be interpreted as Delporte borders.

### Neighboring and obsolete figures

- **Equuleus:** Allen proposed that Hipparchus separated some stars of an older, larger Dolphin to form the Little Horse. This remains reconstruction, not explicit ancient testimony.
- **Anguilla, the Eel:** John Hill’s short-lived 1754 figure used ε and κ Delphini along with stars from Equuleus, Aquila, Sagittarius, and Scutum. Delphinus therefore temporarily “lost” stars on Hill’s private chart, but Anguilla was never broadly accepted.
- **Antinous:** south of Aquila, sometimes sharing atlas plates with Delphinus, but it did not take the Dolphin’s core stars.
- **Sagitta, Vulpecula, Pegasus, Equuleus, Aquarius, and Aquila:** Delporte’s lines assigned marginal telescopic stars and objects definitively, but the α–ε figure remained wholly Delphinus.

### Delporte

The 1930 polygon has fourteen boundary segments in B1875 coordinates. Precession makes these edges appear slightly slanted in J2000 chart projections even though they were defined on B1875 meridians and parallels. This is not a drafting error.

## Iconography

### Classical monuments

**Farnese Atlas, marble, Roman Imperial period, now Naples.** The globe carried by Atlas contains a small dolphin among the constellations. The sculpture is usually dated to the second century CE and probably copies an earlier Hellenistic model. Claims that its exact star placements reproduce Hipparchus’s lost catalogue are **disputed**; Bradley Schaefer argued for a Hipparchan epoch, while critics question the precision and selection of the sculpted circles. [Schaefer study](https://www.phys.lsu.edu/farnese/JHAFarneseProofs.htm).

### Coins

Classical silver coins of **Taras/Tarentum** depict a youth riding a dolphin. The rider may be Taras or Phalanthus depending on issue and interpretation. These are important dolphin-rider parallels but are not labelled constellation images.

Roman coins and mosaics show Neptune, Amphitrite, Erotes, or Palaemon with dolphins. Their relevance is cultural and iconographic, not proof of a Delphinus star map.

### Aratea manuscripts

Late-antique and Carolingian Aratea manuscripts illustrate Delphinus as a compact arched fish or dolphin. The Leiden Aratea, produced around 816 at Aachen, is a major witness to the classical figure tradition. Individual manuscripts vary between profile and near-frontal presentation.

Recent multispectral work on the **Codex Climaci Rescriptus** recovered an early illustrated Aratean layer with a dolphin figure resembling later Greek manuscript types. Its relationship to Hipparchus and the *Catasterismi* remains under active scholarly study. [Cambridge study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/classical-quarterly/article/newly-discovered-illustrated-texts-of-aratus-and-eratosthenes-within-codex-climaci-rescriptus/74BAAA7FE7278A10E23A2F0FB86989FF).

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

From medieval Islamic copies onward, Delphinus appears twice per constellation, sky-view and globe-view. Manuscript painters gradually transformed classical profile prototypes: some show a short-snouted cetacean; others make it more fish-like or frontal. A 1125 Süleymaniye manuscript has been identified as transitional between profile and bilateral types. [Iconographic study](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/Zagrebelsky/Tesi%20inglese%20al%20sufi%201367524_1.pdf).

### Dürer, 1515

Dürer’s northern map renders the constellation in Renaissance classicizing form. The colophon distinguishes roles: Stabius organized, Heinfogel placed stars, Dürer surrounded them with images. Calling it simply “Dürer’s astronomical observations” is inaccurate.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer’s dolphin is energetic and anatomically imaginative, with the labelled stars embedded in its body and tail. Bayer’s atlas became the chief source for later scientific and artistic star maps.

### Schiller, 1627

The dolphin disappears beneath Christian imagery connected with the Cana water vessels. Lucas Kilian engraved the plates from designs associated with Johann Matthias Kager; Bayer assisted with the improved uranometry before his death. The work was printed by Andreas Aperger at Augsburg.

### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius’s plate S shows a graceful, curling dolphin with Sagitta and Equuleus, viewed from outside the celestial sphere. Elisabeth Hevelius supervised the posthumous publication.

### Flamsteed, 1729

Flamsteed’s *Atlas Coelestis* uses Baroque figure drawing over improved telescopic positions. Delphinus remains recognizable but the scientific grid increasingly dominates.

### Bode, 1801

Plate X is the culmination of the engraved-figure tradition: an anatomically stylized dolphin overlays a very dense field, with explicit region outlines and coordinate grids. Bode’s title describes twenty copper plates based on the newest and most complete observations. The original was published at Berlin “apud autorem,” at the author’s expense.

### Jamieson, 1822

Jamieson simplifies the great folio tradition for instruction while retaining the dolphin. His atlas was the direct visual source for *Urania’s Mirror*.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, 1824/1825

Card 13 puts the Dolphin below the Eagle and Arrow. Pierced star holes convert the printed picture into a domestic viewing device. The anonymous “lady” attribution is now considered a concealment; Bloxam’s authorship remains highly persuasive rather than proved by a signed manuscript.

### Argelander and Heis

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) and Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* (1872) emphasized naked-eye star accuracy and reduced pictorial ornament. Their respective counts of approximately 20 and 31 Delphinus stars show differing visibility thresholds.

### Architectural contexts

Dolphins abound on ancient temples, Roman mosaics, Renaissance ceilings, baptismal decoration, and cathedral sculpture. However, identifying any isolated dolphin as **the constellation Delphinus** requires stars, a zodiacal/celestial program, or an inscription. I found no securely documented cathedral portal where Delphinus, as distinct from a generic dolphin or zodiacal fish, has a canonical place.

Likewise, celestial ceilings may contain Delphinus—for example Renaissance and Baroque mythological sky programs—but individual identifications must be made building by building. General web claims that “Delphinus appears on medieval cathedral portals” were not traceable to a specific labelled monument.

## People, events, and works

### Arion

Arion is the central literary person attached to the constellation. Herodotus calls him a pre-eminent citharode and credits him with composing, naming, and teaching the dithyramb at Corinth. Whether he literally invented the genre is an ancient cultural claim, not independently verifiable biography.

The image of Arion riding a dolphin passed through Ovid, Renaissance emblem books, opera, painting, poetry, and sculpture. Authors frequently use it as an emblem of art or music overcoming violence.

### Niccolò Cacciatore

Cacciatore joined the Palermo Observatory under Giuseppe Piazzi, helped with catalogue work, succeeded him, and became secretly encoded in α and β Delphini. His surname means “hunter”; **Nicolaus Venator** is a translation and Latinization, not his baptismal form.

### William Herschel and Ralph Copeland

Herschel discovered the two principal globular clusters, NGC 6934 and NGC 7006, as well as NGC 6905. Copeland discovered the compact planetary nebulae NGC 6891 and NGC 6886. The modern observing reputation of Delphinus owes much to these telescopic objects, despite the absence of a Messier entry.

### George Alcock

Alcock’s 1967 discovery of HR Del made Delphinus a landmark in twentieth-century amateur nova hunting. The nova’s prolonged naked-eye and binocular visibility produced exceptionally extensive visual and photoelectric coverage.

### Navigation

Delphinus’s brightest stars are only fourth magnitude and the constellation lies away from the celestial pole. It was recognizable to mariners but was not a first-rank positional-navigation constellation comparable with Ursa Minor, Crux, Orion, or the principal nautical stars.

Claims that unnamed ancient explorers “steered by Delphinus” are plausible in a general orientation sense but remained untraceable to voyage logs or navigation manuals. Its literary association with ships should not be converted into evidence of routine navigational use.

### Ships

**USS Delphinus (AF-24)** was built as *San Mateo* in Belfast in 1915, acquired by the United States Navy in 1942, renamed for the constellation on 22 August 1942, and used as a refrigerated stores ship in the Pacific. It was decommissioned in 1946. [U.S. Naval History and Heritage Command](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/d/delphinus.html).

A planned hydrofoil initially called **Delphinus** was renamed **USS Pegasus**, lead ship of the Pegasus class. Popular lists that count it as an operational USS *Delphinus* should note that the name was changed before service.

### Spaceflight and planets

The acronym **DELPHINUS** has been proposed for lunar-impact and dust-detection instrumentation associated with the EQUULEUS CubeSat program. This is an engineered acronym inspired by the constellation/animal, not a mission devoted to the constellation.

The planetary system of **18 Delphini** carries the IAU names **Musica** and **Arion**, making the ancient music legend part of official exoplanet nomenclature.

### Literature and music

- Herodotus, *Histories* 1.23–24: foundational Arion narrative.
- Ovid, *Fasti* 2.79–118: principal Latin catasterism.
- Manilius, *Astronomica*: poetic and astrological Dolphin.
- Oppian, *Halieutica*: dolphin behavior and human affinity.
- Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*: Dionysian dolphin transformation.
- Renaissance and Baroque “Arion” poems, operas, and paintings commonly show the dolphin ride, although many do not explicitly show the constellation.
- Henry Kirke White’s line about “Arion’s star,” quoted by Allen, demonstrates the constellation’s survival in English poetic diction.
- Frances Rolleston’s *Mazzaroth* spread spurious Semitic etymologies for Sualocin and Rotanev.

A title containing *Delphinus* may instead refer to the biological genus of common dolphins; attribution to the constellation requires contextual evidence.

## Controversies and disputes

### Was Delphinus Babylonian?

**Disputed.** Greek constellations contain substantial Mesopotamian inheritance, but no cuneiform Dolphin securely maps onto α–ε Delphini. Robert Brown and other pan-Babylonian writers argued for a Euphratean or Phoenician solar dolphin; their comparisons are suggestive mythology, not demonstrated stellar continuity.

### Was it Indian Śiśumāra?

**Disputed and commonly overstated.** Śiśumāra is a real Sanskrit celestial dolphin/crocodilian image, but normally a vast northern or circumpolar configuration. The identification with little Delphinus is late and geographically inconsistent. Neither Greek borrowing from India nor Indian borrowing from Greece is proved for this particular asterism.

### Did Hipparchus cut Equuleus from Delphinus?

**Unverified reconstruction.** Spatial proximity and the late prominence of Equuleus encouraged the theory. No surviving Hipparchan sentence documents the transfer.

### Which myth is “the real” origin?

There is no recoverable single answer. Aratus supplies no myth; the Eratosthenian corpus favors Amphitrite; Herodotus supplies the older non-celestial Arion legend; Ovid gives both; Hyginus transmits both and associates a Dionysian alternative. Ancient mythography was additive rather than canonical.

### Who put it in the sky?

- Amphitrite tradition: **Poseidon/Neptune**.
- Arion in Ovid: **Jupiter**.
- Some modern retellings: **Apollo**, because Arion was a musician or because Apollo appeared as a dolphin.

The Apollo version is a modern conflation unless a particular late source is named.

### Sualocin and Rotanev: joke, fraud, or honor?

The encoding is factual; calling it a “hoax” overstates the evidence. It was an unexplained catalogue conceit that successfully entered usage. Whether Piazzi knowingly approved Cacciatore’s self-memorial, devised it for him, or overlooked it is unknown.

### Were they the only stars named for a living person?

No defensible universal claim can be made. Formal astronomical nomenclature has often avoided living persons, but unofficial and covert exceptions exist. The Palermo pair remains unusually early, durable, and ingenious.

### Spurious Arabic and Syriac derivations

Rolleston’s alleged “swift-flowing” explanations have no demonstrated lexicographical or manuscript basis and are rendered unnecessary by the exact backward Latin name. They illustrate how nineteenth-century biblical astronomy manufactured Semitic antiquity for opaque European names.

### Job’s Coffin

The origin is genuinely unknown. It is not a classical, Arabic, Hebrew, or biblical constellation. The “Jonah’s Coffin” theory is ingenious modern speculation. Burritt’s early admission of ignorance is better evidence than later confident explanations.

### Bayer’s Currus

It is almost certainly a misprint for **Curvus**, not evidence that the constellation was formerly a chariot.

### Number of stars

Counts of nine in mythography, ten in Ptolemy, eighteen in Burritt, twenty in Argelander, thirty-one in Heis, or hundreds in modern surveys are not contradictions. They count different things: mythic star-points, catalogue members to different magnitude limits, or every object within mathematical boundaries.

### Does the figure really look like a dolphin?

This is subjective. The five-star modern line figure can resemble a leaping dolphin, but ancient artists did not derive anatomy mechanically from connecting dots. The artistic animal and catalogue descriptions shaped how the stars were read.

### “Blue Flash” location and classification

NGC 6905 lies close to the modern Sagitta boundary and is sometimes loosely associated with Sagitta in observing discussions. Delporte’s coordinates place it in **Delphinus**. Its nickname is informal; NGC 6905 is the stable identifier.

### HR Del designation

As noted, “HR” is a variable-star letter designation, not a Bright Star Catalogue number. The nova is also **Nova Delphini 1967**; V339 Del is the distinct nova of 2013.

### Meteor-shower claims

A radiant named for Delphinus need not lie permanently inside today’s boundary at all epochs, and Working List status does not make a shower established. Popular sources range from “no showers” to lists of many Delphinids because they use different evidentiary thresholds.

### Bode’s boundaries

Descriptions of Bode as the inventor of official constellation boundaries are incorrect. He drew practical regions; the globally exhaustive mathematical system is Delporte’s.

### Atlas dates

- Bayer: 1603.
- Schiller: 1627.
- Hevelius: 1690 posthumous publication.
- Flamsteed’s atlas: 1729, posthumous.
- Bode’s popular atlas: 1782; monumental *Uranographia*: 1801.
- Jamieson: 1822.
- *Urania’s Mirror*: advertised in late 1824 and commonly dated 1825.

The paired dates arise from publication and advertisement history, not necessarily cataloguing mistakes.

## Open questions

1. **When and where did “Job’s Coffin” first appear in print?** Burritt proves circulation by the 1830s but says it was already popular. Earlier newspapers, sailors’ manuals, schoolbooks, or oral-history collections may contain it.

2. **Who inserted Sualocin and Rotanev into the 1814 Palermo catalogue?** The catalogue proves the names and the reversal proves the honoree; surviving correspondence or printer’s copy would be needed to distinguish Cacciatore from Piazzi as inventor.

3. **What precise stars made every indigenous Arabian camel form?** Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts and later philological interpretations differ; the entire camel complex should not automatically be reduced to the four-star diamond.

4. **Can an Egyptian Dendera group be securely fixed to Delphinus?** Current reconstructions conflict, and Egyptian astronomical figures often lack coordinates precise enough for a unique solution.

5. **What was in Eratosthenes’ original text?** The *Catasterismi* survives through epitomes, fragments, and revised manuscripts. Differences between the Amphitrite and Arion traditions may reflect successive editorial layers.

6. **Did Hipparchus materially redefine the border with Equuleus?** His lost catalogue and fragmentary commentary prevent a final answer.

7. **Which Delphinid meteor candidates will become established?** The IAU Meteor Data Center changes as orbital evidence accumulates; Working List status should be checked afresh in future research.

8. **What exactly is meant by the “Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)”?** I found no such enumeration in Bode’s original title, plate, or accompanying global catalogue. It may be metadata for a modern linked-data collection and requires its repository record to interpret.
