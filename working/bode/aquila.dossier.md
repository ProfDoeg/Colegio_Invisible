# Aquila: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **Kept.** Aquila is one of the 88 constellations recognized by the International Astronomical Union. Its official abbreviation is **Aql**, its genitive is **Aquilae**, and its present boundaries are those prepared by Eugène Delporte, approved by the IAU in 1928, and published in 1930. The adjoining figure **Antinous** was not retained as an independent constellation; its stars are now principally in Aquila.  
> **Historical status:** One of Ptolemy’s 48 constellations, although an eagle in substantially the same part of the sky is documented much earlier in Mesopotamia.  
> **Bode:** Johann Elert Bode’s *Uranographia* (Berlin, 1801) pictures and catalogues **Aquila together with Antinous**. The plate’s classical Latin identity is *Aquila*; in Bode’s German explanatory tradition it is **der Adler**, with **Antinous** beneath or in the eagle’s grasp.  
> **Evidence labels used below:** **Documented fact**, **scholarly reconstruction**, **tradition**, **disputed**, **unverified claim**, **legend**, **myth**, and **modern invention**.

## Basic identification

### Official identity

**Documented fact.** The IAU form is:

| Field | Official form |

| Genitive | **Aquilae** |
| Abbreviation | **Aql** |

| Status | Retained |
| Modern area | About 652 square degrees; 22nd largest |

The familiar statement that the IAU “fixed the constellations in 1922” needs qualification. At Rome in 1922 the Union standardized the accepted constellation list and three-letter abbreviations. Delporte’s coordinate boundaries were proposed in 1925, approved at Leiden in 1928, and printed in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* in 1930. They were drawn in right ascension and declination for epoch B1875.0, with an effort to preserve existing Bayer, Flamsteed, and variable-star affiliations. [IAU constellation history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [Delporte’s publication record](https://books.google.com/books?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ).

### Position and neighbors

**Documented fact.** Aquila straddles the celestial equator in the Milky Way. Under the Delporte system it borders:

- Sagitta
- Hercules
- Ophiuchus
- Serpens Cauda
- Scutum
- Sagittarius
- Capricornus
- Aquarius
- Delphinus

No ancient or early-modern atlas had boundaries in the modern area-filling sense. Ptolemy, Bayer, Tycho, Hevelius, Flamsteed, and Bode assigned stars to figures or cataloguing groups; blank or overlapping spaces remained possible.

### What makes the visual figure?

There is no official IAU stick figure. The common modern outline usually includes:

- α Aql, Altair
- β Aql, Alshain
- γ Aql, Tarazed
- δ Aql
- ζ Aql, Okab
- η Aql
- θ Aql
- λ Aql
- sometimes ε, ι, κ, μ, ν, and ξ Aql

The most conspicuous internal asterism is the straight row **γ–α–β Aquilae**: Tarazed–Altair–Alshain. Arabic tradition treated the three collectively as *al-nasr al-ṭāʾir*, “the flying eagle.” Modern English sources sometimes call them the “Family of Aquila” or “Shaft of Aquila”; those expressions are recent popular asterism-names, not ancient Western catalogue titles.

Altair is also the southern vertex of the **Summer Triangle**, with Vega in Lyra and Deneb in Cygnus. **Modern invention:** the stars are ancient, but “Summer Triangle” is a twentieth-century popular name, commonly associated with Oswald Thomas and later popularized by Patrick Moore.

### Names and etymologies

#### Mesopotamian

**Documented fact with identification reconstructed.** Babylonian astronomical sources include **MUL.TI₈.MUŠEN**, “the Eagle,” in this sector. *MUL* is the star determinative and *MUŠEN* the bird determinative. The exact reading of the logogram TI₈ and the extent of the constellation have varied in Assyriological literature.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The continuity from the Babylonian Eagle to Greek *Aetos* is plausible because both occupy the same conspicuous three-star region near the celestial river. It is not proof that the entire Greek picture and mythology were borrowed unchanged. Claims that it was specifically a vulture, or the “Living Eye of Zamama,” depend on older translations and proposed identifications and should not be repeated as settled fact.

#### Egyptian

**Disputed reconstruction.** Modern popular accounts identify Aquila or Altair with Horus’s falcon. Egypt certainly possessed falcon celestial imagery, and Graeco-Roman Egyptian zodiacs contain raptorial birds, but no securely continuous pharaonic catalogue identifies the modern α–β–γ Aql group as “Horus/Aquila.” The proposed match in the Daressy zodiac and modern reconstructions such as *The Celestial River* remain hypotheses, not an Egyptian antecedent comparable in evidentiary strength to MUL.TI₈.MUŠEN.

#### Greek

- **Ἀετός, Aetós** — “eagle.”
- **Ἀετὸς Διός, Aetòs Diós** — “Eagle of Zeus”; a mythological description rather than a universal catalogue title.
- Ptolemy’s heading is conventionally translated “the stars in Aquila/the Eagle.”
- Ptolemy separately identifies nearby unformed stars as those “upon which [is] Antinous”: **οἱ περὶ τὸν ἀετόν, ἐφ’ ὧν ὁ Ἀντίνοος**.

Greek words rendered “eagle,” “vulture,” or “great bird of prey” were not always taxonomically identical to modern ornithological categories. That ambiguity helps explain later *Aquila/Vultur* double naming.

#### Latin

- **Aquila** — Latin “eagle”; probably related to *aquilus*, “dark-colored/dusky,” although the deeper Indo-European derivation is uncertain.
- **Vultur volans** — “flying vulture,” especially in medieval and early-modern star nomenclature.
- Literary circumlocutions include **Jovis ales** (“Jove’s bird”), **Jovis armiger** (“Jove’s armor/thunderbolt-bearer”), and **Ganymedis raptrix** (“ravisher of Ganymede”). These are poetic epithets, not separate securely defined constellations.
- Medieval English descendants include **the Eagle**, **Flying Eagle**, **Flying Vulture**, and **Flying Grype/Griffin**.

#### Arabic

Two traditions have to be separated:

1. **The translated Greek figure:** **al-ʿuqāb**, “the eagle,” used for Ptolemy’s Aquila in the Arabic scientific tradition.
2. **The indigenous Arabic asterism:** **al-nasr al-ṭāʾir** or assimilated **an-nasr aṭ-ṭāʾir**, “the flying eagle/vulture,” applied to α, β, and γ Aql.

*Nasr* denotes a large raptorial/scavenging bird and is often translated “eagle” or “vulture.” The contrast was with **al-nasr al-wāqiʿ**, the “falling/alighting eagle,” centered on Vega. Western **Altair** is a contraction of *al-ṭāʾir*, “the flying [one],” not simply the Arabic word for eagle.

In al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* (964 CE), the Greek constellation is preserved and compared with Arabic usage. **Documented tradition:** α–β–γ are the flying eagle; al-Ṣūfī also transmits indigenous names for other stars rather than pretending that the Greek bird and Bedouin sky were identical systems.

#### Persian

The Western names **Alshain** and **Tarazed** descend through medieval astronomical transmission from a Persian expression commonly reconstructed as **šāhīn-i tarāzū** or **šāhīn tarāzū**, associated with the beam or pointer of a balance.

**Disputed etymology.** Persian *šāhīn* also means “royal falcon,” encouraging attractive “falcon” translations. Historical star-name scholarship usually treats the expression as a balance-beam term. The division of the phrase between β and γ Aql produced forms that later appeared Arabic even though their immediate ancestry is Persian:

- β Aql: **Alshain**
- γ Aql: **Tarazed**

These are good examples of star names reshaped by multilingual copying rather than transparent ancient personal names.

#### Hebrew

- **נשר, nesher** — eagle, vulture, or great bird of prey.

**Tradition, not demonstrated ancient constellation equivalence.** Early-modern Christian Hebraists associated *Nesher* with Aquila and sometimes with the tribal standard of Dan. The biblical and targumic eagle imagery is genuine; the assertion that biblical Israel had exactly the Ptolemaic Aquila is not demonstrated. Dan was more often mapped to Scorpio in later zodiacal tribal schemes.

#### Medieval and vernacular forms

Forms include Latin *Aquila*, *Vultur volans*, German *Adler*, French *Aigle*, Spanish *Águila*, Italian *Aquila*, Portuguese *Águia*, Dutch *Arend*, and older English *Eagle* or *Flying Grype*. Spanish **águila** and French **aigle** descend from Latin *aquila* through their respective Romance developments.

#### Bode’s labels

**Documented fact.** Bode’s 1782 *Vorstellung der Gestirne* presents **Adler** and **Antinous**. His 1801 *Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio* uses the international Latin constellation nomenclature, **Aquila** and **Antinous**, while the associated German text uses **der Adler**. Bode did not invent either figure.

The supplied “830 stars in 109 groups” description could not be reconciled with the original *Uranographia* title and catalogue. Bode’s accompanying 1801 catalogue advertises **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters**, not 830. The smaller numbers may describe a particular collection, exhibition chain, subset, or cataloguing interface rather than Bode’s whole publication.

The IAU Working Group on Star Names standardized **Altair** in 2016 and maintains an evolving catalogue of stellar proper names. A proper name officially belongs to a specific stellar component, not indiscriminately to every member of a multiple system. [IAU Working Group on Star Names](https://iau.org/WG280/WG280/Home.aspx).

### Altair

**Documented fact.** Altair is α Aql, 53 Aql, about 16.7 light-years away. It is a rapidly rotating A-type star, visibly oblate in interferometric observations, and a low-amplitude δ Scuti variable. It is the twelfth-brightest night-sky star by ordinary visual ranking.

**Name correction.** “Altair means eagle in Arabic” is a popular error. It represents the final part of *al-nasr al-ṭāʾir*: “the flying [eagle].”

### Tarazed and Alshain

Both flank Altair and helped give the indigenous Arabic asterism its winged appearance. Tarazed is an orange bright giant; Alshain is a multiple system whose principal visible member is a yellow subgiant.

**Contested nomenclature.** Sources often call both names Arabic because they entered Europe through Arabic astronomical manuscripts. Their lexical ancestry is generally considered Persian. “Tarazed means falcon” is a folk simplification created by reading Persian *šāhīn* in isolation.

### Okab and Deneb el Okab

**Documented transmission; disputed allocation.** Arabic *dhanab al-ʿuqāb*, “tail of the eagle,” was assigned inconsistently in European sources to ε and ζ Aql. Modern IAU usage fixes **Okab** to ζ Aql, but historical tables must be read in their own coordinate context. Treating every occurrence of “Deneb el Okab” as ζ Aql creates false identifications.

### Bayer and Flamsteed

Bayer’s *Uranometria* (1603) assigned Greek letters to Aquila’s principal stars. The letters are not a perfectly descending brightness sequence—Bayer’s systems also reflected position and practical grouping.

Flamsteed numbers were published from observations arranged by right ascension within constellations. Consequently α Aql is 53 Aql, γ is 50, and β is 60. Numbers and letters preserve historical affiliation and do not define modern boundaries.

### Antinous’s stars

In the early-modern reconstruction, Antinous’s head and body were variously drawn through η, θ, ι, κ, λ, ν, σ, and δ Aql. Catalogues disagree because Ptolemy supplied star descriptions, not an engraved human outline.

- Tycho tabulated twelve Aquila stars and seven Antinous stars.
- Hevelius tabulated 23 in Aquila and 19 in Antinous.
- Flamsteed used compound headings such as *Aquila vel Antinous* and *Aquila cum Antinoo*.
- Bode retained both figures pictorially and catalogically.
- Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) dropped Antinous as a title while leaving the stars in Aquila.

### Variable and eruptive stars

#### η Aquilae

**Documented fact.** η Aql is one of the brightest classical Cepheid variables, changing roughly from magnitude 3.5 to 4.4 in about 7.18 days. Edward Pigott discovered its variability in 1784. Together with δ Cephei, it became historically important in the study of Cepheid pulsation.

#### R Aquilae

A long-period Mira variable whose period has measurably shortened over historical observations. It is important in studies of thermal changes in evolved stars.

#### V Aquilae

A semiregular carbon star, notable for its deep red color and proximity on the sky to NGC 6751.

#### V603 Aquilae — Nova Aquilae 1918

**Documented fact.** Discovered in eruption in June 1918, V603 Aql reached approximately magnitude −1 to −0.5, rivaling or surpassing every star except Sirius. It is among the brightest novae of the telescopic era and remains a cataclysmic binary. Modern work finds a long post-eruption luminosity evolution. [SIMBAD record](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-basic?Ident=V603+Aquilae); [long-term study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.6802).

#### V605 Aquilae

**Documented fact; classification historically contested.** V605 Aql flared around 1919 and is the central star of planetary nebula Abell 58. Initially described in nova-like language, it is now usually interpreted as a “born-again” post-AGB star that underwent a final helium-shell flash. The object demonstrates why the historical word *nova* does not always imply the modern thermonuclear-nova mechanism. [V605 Aql study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1009.3138).

#### Other novae

Aquila’s Milky Way star fields have produced many novae, including Nova Aql 1899, 1936, 1943, 1945, 1993, 1995, and 1999, with catalogue designations changing as identifications were refined. The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* necessarily calls the 1899 event “Nova Aquilae”; it predates the famous 1918 nova and should not be quoted as though it meant V603 Aql.

### The alleged nova of 389 CE

**Tradition/disputed astronomical identification.** Early-modern chronicler Johannes Cuspinianus reported that in 389 a star near Aquila shone like Venus and disappeared after about three weeks. This has repeatedly been listed as a possible nova or supernova.

Problems:

- The report is transmitted through a much later source.
- Its exact position is not securely defined.
- A comet is not excluded.
- No securely matched remnant has been demonstrated.
- The claimed duration and brightness alone do not distinguish nova, supernova, or comet.

It should therefore be called an **unconfirmed transient report**, not “Nova Aquilae 389” as a fact. Similar claims for an event about sixty years earlier are even less securely localized.

### Supernova remnants, compact objects, and high-energy sources

- **W49B:** an unusually asymmetric Galactic supernova remnant, perhaps about a millennium old as seen from Earth. Its compact remnant is uncertain; absence of a detected neutron star has supported, but not proved, a black-hole interpretation. [NASA/Chandra](https://www.nasa.gov/universe/supernova-remnant-w49b/).
- **W50 and SS 433/V1343 Aql:** W50 is a supernova remnant distorted by the relativistic jets of the extraordinary microquasar SS 433.
- **SGR 1900+14:** a magnetar/soft gamma repeater in Aquila.
- The **“first pulsar” was not in Aquila**. CP 1919, the first recognized pulsar, is in Vulpecula. Any Aquila attribution is an error, perhaps encouraged by the proximity of the two constellations and the Milky Way.
- The **Hercules–Aquila Cloud** is a Galactic stellar-halo overdensity, not a traditional constellation object.
- The **Aquila Rift** is a complex of dark molecular clouds across Aquila and neighboring constellations; it is a physical Galactic structure, not a deep-sky object named from the myth.

#### NGC 6863

**Documented controversy resolved observationally.** NGC 6863 was long catalogued as an open cluster. Photometric and kinematic study found no coeval, gravitationally related population: it is an **asterism**, a chance visual grouping. [Study](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.0653).

### Meteor showers

The name “Aquilid” indicates a radiant in or historically assigned to Aquila; it does not mean that meteors come from the constellation’s stars.

Reported or catalogued streams include:

- June Aquilid complexes
- Northern and Southern June Aquilids
- ε-Aquilids
- γ-Aquilids
- August Aquilids and several weak video/radar streams

**Documented uncertainty.** Many are weak, historically inconsistently defined, or remain on the IAU Meteor Data Center’s working list rather than the established list. Radiants also move with solar longitude and can cross modern constellation boundaries. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century claims of one broad “Aquilid” shower from June to August should not be treated as a single securely established modern stream. [IAU MDC working list](https://iaumdc.astro.sk/working); [γ-Aquilid analysis](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2017/02/aa29100-16.pdf).

## Chronology of attestation

### Second millennium BCE: Mesopotamia

**Documented text; reconstructed sky match.** MUL.TI₈.MUŠEN, the Eagle, appears in the Mesopotamian uranographic tradition. By the first millennium BCE the canonical *MUL.APIN* compendium organized inherited constellations, calendrical risings, and stellar paths. The α–β–γ Aql region is the strongest candidate for this Eagle.

**Caution.** Statements that “Sumerians called Altair the Eagle Star” often compress sources from different periods and languages. The surviving documentation is mainly Babylonian scholarly tradition using Sumerograms; it does not by itself prove an unchanged Sumerian constellation myth.

### c. 700–600 BCE: early Greek epic

Homer knows Ganymede as the beautiful Trojan youth taken by the gods to be Zeus’s cupbearer, but does **not** specify an eagle in the earliest version: *Iliad* 5.265–267; 20.231–235. The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 202–217 likewise tells of Zeus carrying off Ganymede without making the constellation the agent.

This matters because the eagle-abduction icon and catasterism are later developments, not explicit in the earliest Ganymede texts.

### c. 700 BCE: Prometheus

Hesiod, *Theogony* 521–525, gives the earliest surviving literary account of Zeus binding Prometheus and sending a long-winged eagle to eat his immortal liver, which regrows. Heracles later kills the bird with Zeus’s consent (*Theogony* 526–534).

**Documented myth; not initially a catasterism.** Hesiod does not identify the bird with the constellation.

### Fifth century BCE

Aeschylus or the author of *Prometheus Bound* elaborates the liver-eating “winged hound of Zeus” (*Prometheus Bound* 1021–1025). Vase painting also associates eagles with Zeus and with Ganymede.

Euripides, *Trojan Women* 820–839, invokes Ganymede amid Trojan suffering; later visual and literary traditions make the eagle explicit.

### Eudoxus, fourth century BCE

**Documented indirectly.** Eudoxus’s lost *Phaenomena* described the constellation; its contents survive partly through Aratus and Hipparchus’s commentary. Aquila therefore predates Ptolemy by centuries in Greek scientific uranography.

### Aratus, c. 275 BCE

Aratus, *Phaenomena* 312–315, places the small but storm-associated Eagle near the celestial Bird/Swan. His poem describes the figure and weather signs but supplies no Ganymede catasterism.

### Hellenistic catasterism tradition

The work transmitted as Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* is based on a Hellenistic compilation but survives in epitomized and reworked forms. Its attribution and exact original text are therefore **textually qualified**.

#### Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 30

**Documented mythological explanation.** The Eagle is the bird that carried Ganymede to Zeus. Zeus honored it among the stars because it is king of birds and can face the sun. The chapter also records stellar anatomy.

This is the earliest surviving explicit constellation-Ganymede synthesis in a dedicated catasterism text, though the material may be older.

### First century BCE–first century CE

- Cicero’s fragmentary Latin *Aratea* renders the Greek astronomical poem and uses Latin eagle vocabulary.
- Hyginus’s source Aglaosthenes, author of a lost *Naxica*, supplied the Naxian Zeus-and-eagle omen story; the original date is uncertain but probably Hellenistic or earlier.
- Manilius, *Astronomica* 1.343–345 and 5.486–537, treats Aquila as a constellation and assigns astrological effects to its rising.
- Germanicus’s *Aratea* translates and embellishes Aratus; its scholia become major carriers of catasterism variants.

### Ovid, c. 8 CE

Ovid supplies several separate eagle myths:

- *Metamorphoses* 10.155–161: Jupiter becomes or deploys the eagle to abduct Ganymede; the god considers no bird except the thunderbolt-bearer worthy.
- *Metamorphoses* 6.108: Arachne’s tapestry depicts Jupiter as an eagle assaulting Asterie.
- *Metamorphoses* 7.399–400: Jupiter transforms righteous Periphas into an eagle.
- *Fasti* and the exilic poems use the eagle as Jupiter’s emblem without necessarily identifying Aquila.

Ovid does not consistently say that each of these eagles is the constellation. Later mythographers sometimes collapse independent transformations into Aquila’s identity.

### Hadrian and Antinous, 130–c. 150 CE

Antinous drowned in the Nile in October 130 CE. Hadrian founded Antinoopolis and promoted his cult.

Cassius Dio 69.11.2–4, writing in the early third century, says Antinous died either accidentally or as a voluntary sacrificial victim and that Hadrian declared he had seen a new star that was Antinous. The popular exact date “132” for creation of the constellation is conventional and not securely supplied by Dio.

Ptolemy’s *Almagest*, c. 150 CE, catalogues Aquila and then a group of adjacent stars **“on which is Antinous.”** This is decisive evidence that the celestial Antinous existed within a generation of the youth’s death.

**Correction:** Ptolemy does not make Antinous one of his 48 independent constellation headings. It is an asterism/subfigure among Aquila’s associated “unformed” stars.

### Ptolemy, c. 150 CE

Ptolemy lists Aquila’s figured stars and adjacent stars associated with Antinous, nineteen in the combined traditional count. He locates Altair as the bright star around/between the shoulders.

The catalogue defines positions and stellar anatomy, not polygonal borders. [Toomer translation scan](https://www.mathship.hlevkin.com/900/Astronomy/PtolemyAlmagest.pdf); [Greek textual record](https://ptolemaeus.badw.de/work/19).

### Second century CE: Hyginus

Pseudo-Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.16, preserves the richest set of Aquila catasterisms:

1. The eagle abducted Ganymede and delivered him to Jupiter.
2. Jupiter chose the eagle as his bird; it alone flies toward the rising sun.
3. **Merops of Cos:** Hera transformed the grieving king into an eagle and placed him among the stars after his wife Ethemea was taken alive to the underworld.
4. **Aglaosthenes’ Naxian version:** young Zeus, about to fight the Titans, saw an eagle while sacrificing; taking it as a favorable omen, he placed it in heaven.
5. **Hermes and Aphrodite:** an eagle stole Aphrodite’s sandal while she bathed in the Achelous and carried it to Hermes in Egypt, enabling their union; Hermes rewarded the eagle with a celestial place.
6. Other ancient sources linked the bird with nectar brought to the infant Zeus.

The Latin text is preserved online at [Packard Humanities Institute](https://latin.packhum.org/loc/899/1/25/114-117%2C1390-1393).

### Late antiquity

Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 10.258–321 and related passages, elaborates the Ganymede story in late-antique epic style and repeatedly associates the youth with Zeus’s eagle and cup.

The scholia to Aratus and Germanicus preserve or cross-reference variants about Ganymede, Zeus’s war, Merops, the eagle’s solar sight, and the bird’s service to the god. Since scholia accumulated over centuries, “the scholiast” is not a single datable author.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: Arabic reception

Arabic translations of Ptolemy rendered the Greek figure as an eagle while observational astronomers preserved indigenous Arabic asterisms. Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* (964) famously supplies two images of each constellation, as seen on a globe and as seen in the sky, and compares Ptolemaic stellar descriptions with Arabic names.

### Medieval Latin reception

Gerard of Cremona’s twelfth-century Arabic-to-Latin *Almagest* helped establish *Aquila*, *Vultur volans*, and Arabic-derived star names in Europe. Astrolabes and computistical manuscripts often pictured Altair and Vega as paired flying/falling birds.

### 1515: Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s printed northern celestial map, produced with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel at Nuremberg, includes Aquila within the Ptolemaic figure cycle. It presents the heavens globe-wise, so orientation differs from direct-sky maps.

### 1536 and 1551: Antinous re-emerges

**Documented correction to older attribution.** Caspar Vopel’s 1536 celestial globe contains Antinous as a separately pictured constellation. Gerard Mercator’s 1551 globe also shows it.

Therefore Tycho Brahe did **not** invent Antinous. His importance was to give it an independent catalogue table.

### 1603: Bayer

Bayer’s *Uranometria* depicts the eagle grasping a male youth and calls the youth **Ganymedes**, effectively substituting the older divine cupbearer for Hadrian’s Antinous. Bayer did not provide a fully separate Antinous star list. [Bayer plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aquila_Uranometria.jpg).

### 1606–1627: Kepler and Tycho

Kepler’s *De Stella Nova* (1606) printed a chart treating Antinous separately. The *Rudolphine Tables* (1627), incorporating Tycho’s observations, reinforced the separate catalogue group.

### 1627: Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, published at Augsburg with assistance from Bayer and engravings by Lucas Kilian, replaced Aquila with **Saint Catherine the Virgin and Martyr — Sancta Catharina Virgo et Martyr**. Schiller’s chart is reversed in globe orientation.

This Christianized system was reproduced by Cellarius but never displaced classical nomenclature. [Complete 1627 facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720).

### 1640–1770

- Longomontanus used Antinous in *Astronomica Danica* (1640).
- Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum/Uranographia* (1690) distinguished Aquila and Antinous, counted 23 and 19 stars respectively, and associated bow and arrow imagery with the region.
- Flamsteed used *Aquila Antinous*, *Aquila vel Antinous*, and *Aquila cum Antinoo*.
- Maximilian Hell used Antinous in his 1769–1770 ephemerides.

Lacaille did not invent or substantially redraw Aquila. His major work lay in southern constellations. Lists that place Aquila among “Lacaille constellations” are erroneous.

### 1782 and 1801: Bode

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* (1782) shows **Adler** above **Antinous**. In the monumental *Uranographia* of 1801 he retained both the ancient Eagle and Hadrianic youth, over a dense field reaching far below naked-eye visibility.

**Documented fact.** Bode’s atlas was intended to consolidate the expanded late-eighteenth-century sky. Its associated *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* catalogued 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. [Digital *Uranographia*](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/download/pdf/3341734).

The engraved figure is iconographic, not a boundary. Bode’s attempted division of surrounding stars relied heavily on existing Flamsteed-style affiliations.

### Nineteenth century: decline of Antinous

- Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* (1822) retained the pictorial Aquila–Antinous complex.
- *Urania’s Mirror* (London, 1824/25), derived closely from Jamieson and engraved by Sidney Hall, shows Aquila bearing Antinous.
- Elijah Burritt’s *Atlas Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens* (1833) used “Aquila et Antinous.”
- Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) omitted Antinous as an independent name.
- Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* (1872) retained the German cultural memory of **der Adler mit dem Antinoüs** but treated the region observationally as Aquila.

Antinous did not perish in a single decree; it gradually ceased to function as a useful catalogue division.

### 1922–1930: IAU settlement

Aquila was retained. Antinous was not placed among the 88 official constellations. Delporte allocated its stellar territory chiefly to Aquila.

The formal boundary process did not literally “abolish the picture”: the IAU standardizes scientific regions and names, not artistic or mythological use.

### 1992: ρ Aquilae crosses into Delphinus

**Documented curiosity.** Because of its proper motion, ρ Aql crossed the fixed Delporte boundary into Delphinus around 1992. Its historical Bayer designation remains ρ Aquilae. This is not a boundary revision and Aquila did not formally “lose” territory.

## Classical myths and legends

### The eagle as Zeus’s thunderbolt-bearer

**Myth; classical tradition.** Zeus’s eagle is his royal and martial bird, carrying or attending the thunderbolt. This identification is explicit in Roman poetic epithets and in Ovid’s Ganymede passage, where Jupiter chooses the bird capable of carrying his lightning.

The catasterism’s earliest surviving explicit combination with Ganymede is Pseudo-Eratosthenes 30; the thunderbolt-bearer emphasis is especially strong in Latin poetry and later mythography.

### Ganymede’s abduction

**Myth, layered development.**

The earliest surviving Ganymede accounts—Homer, *Iliad* 5.265–267 and 20.231–235—say the gods took the youth because of his beauty so that he would pour wine for Zeus. No eagle appears.

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 202–217 says Zeus carried Ganymede away and compensated his father Tros with divine horses.

By the classical and Hellenistic periods, art and literature made Zeus’s eagle, or Zeus transformed into an eagle, the abductor. Pseudo-Eratosthenes 30 makes that bird Aquila. Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.16, says the eagle seized Ganymede and delivered him to Jupiter. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 10.155–161, says Jupiter assumed a false bird-shape and chose the eagle that bears his thunderbolts.

**Variant:** Sometimes Zeus himself becomes the eagle; sometimes he sends his eagle. Ancient authors do not enforce a single version.

**Celestial geometry:** Aquarius was sometimes identified as Ganymede, while Aquila flies above him. Antinous later occupied the stars beneath the eagle, allowing Hadrian’s favorite to be assimilated visually to Ganymede.

### The Titanomachy omen

**Myth preserved by Hyginus from Aglaosthenes.** Zeus was secretly moved from Crete to Naxos and raised there. Before attacking the Titans he sacrificed; an eagle appeared as a favorable omen. After victory he placed it among the stars: Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.16.

A related version, known through late testimony attributed to Anacreon, makes the eagle the origin of the golden eagle on Zeus’s standards. The survival chain is late, so the precise archaic form cannot be verified.

### The eagle that nourished Zeus

**Myth/tradition.** Some mythographers said an eagle brought nectar to the infant Zeus. Zeus rewarded it with catasterism. The story parallels the better-known nourishment by the goat Amalthea and the bees. It is ancient but less securely localized to an early extant author than the Ganymede and Naxian versions.

### Merops of Cos

**Myth preserved in Hyginus.** Merops, king of Cos, married the nymph Ethemea. When she ceased honoring Artemis, the goddess shot her and Persephone took her alive below. Merops attempted suicide from grief. Hera transformed him into an eagle and placed him among the stars so that, no longer in human form, he would not remain consumed by his wife’s memory: Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.16.

Later summaries sometimes say “Ovid made Aquila Merops,” but Ovid’s securely surviving eagle-transformation in *Metamorphoses* 7 concerns **Periphas**, not Merops. The attribution is therefore frequently confused.

### Hermes, Aphrodite, and the stolen sandal

**Myth preserved in Hyginus.** Hermes—or in a textual variant, Anaplas—desired Aphrodite but was rejected. While she bathed in the Achelous, Zeus sent an eagle to steal her sandal and carry it to Hermes in Egyptian Amythaonia. Aphrodite followed it; Hermes attained his desire and set the helpful eagle among the stars: *Astronomica* 2.16.

This story is absent from the principal Pseudo-Eratosthenic epitome and survives chiefly through Hyginus’s compilation.

### Zeus, Nemesis, the eagle, and the swan

**Mythological constellation linkage.** In variants of Helen’s conception, Zeus pursues Nemesis. She becomes a goose or swan; Zeus becomes, or stages a pursuit involving, an eagle and swan. Hyginus discusses the constellation implications principally under Cygnus, *Astronomica* 2.8, and related accounts occur in the Cyprian epic tradition.

This is sometimes offered as Aquila’s origin, but the ancient evidence more directly explains Cygnus and the egg of Helen. Calling it a primary Aquila catasterism overstates the source.

### Prometheus’s torment

**Myth; later constellation association.**

Hesiod, *Theogony* 521–534, tells how Zeus’s eagle consumes Prometheus’s regenerating liver until Heracles kills it. Aeschylus, *Prometheus Bound* 1021–1025, elaborates the torment. Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Library* 2.5.11, and Hyginus, *Fabulae* 31 and 54, incorporate the episode into Heracles’ labors and Prometheus’s punishment.

Ancient and medieval mythographers readily identified Zeus’s eagle with Aquila, but the earliest Prometheus texts do not state that the slain bird was catasterized. The astronomical identification is therefore **tradition**, not a securely independent early catasterism.

Sagitta, the Arrow, nearby in the sky, was sometimes called Heracles’ missile against the eagle. This produces an attractive Aquila–Sagitta–Prometheus tableau, but sources vary over whether Sagitta is this arrow or belongs to another myth.

### Periphas

**Myth, not originally a secure Aquila catasterism.** Periphas was a righteous autochthonous king and priest of Apollo whom the Athenians honored like Zeus. Zeus intended to destroy him but, at Apollo’s intercession, transformed him into an eagle and made him king of birds. Antoninus Liberalis 6 preserves the full metamorphosis; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 7.399–400, briefly alludes to Periphas and his eagle form.

Later handbooks equate him with Aquila. Neither surviving passage unambiguously says that he became the constellation, so that step should be labeled a later identification.

### Aetos, the beautiful companion of young Zeus

**Late myth/tradition.** Antoninus Liberalis and related late sources preserve stories of **Aetos**, a handsome earth-born companion of Zeus in Crete. Hera, jealous of the youth, transformed him into an eagle. This explains the bird’s intimacy with Zeus by name-play: Greek *aetos*, eagle.

The equation with the constellation is later and not prominent in Pseudo-Eratosthenes or Hyginus.

### The eagle and the swan as rival birds

**Scholarly/antiquarian interpretation.** Nineteenth-century writers suggested that the story of an eagle attacking a swan but being defeated encoded the relative rising and setting of Aquila and Cygnus. This is a speculative astronomical allegory, not an established ancient explanation.

### The Eagle in Aristomenes’ escape

Pausanias 4.18.5 tells how the Messenian hero Aristomenes, thrown into the Ceadas, followed a fox or was aided amid divine signs; an eagle episode occurs in traditions of his rescue. It belongs to the terrestrial sacred biography of the hero, not Aquila’s catasterism.

### Zeus and Asterie

Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 6.108, has Arachne depict Jupiter in eagle form pursuing Asterie. This is another example of Zeus’s eagle metamorphosis, but no ancient source securely makes this particular eagle Aquila.

### Nonnus and the expanded Ganymede cycle

Nonnus’s fifth-century *Dionysiaca* repeatedly describes Ganymede as Zeus’s Trojan wine-pourer and evokes the eagle-abduction in a luxuriant late-antique synthesis. Nonnus is evidence for the story’s endurance, not its origin.

## Antinous: Hadrian’s boy beneath the Eagle

### Historical core

**Documented fact.** Antinous was a young Bithynian favorite and lover of Emperor Hadrian. He drowned in the Nile in 130 CE. Hadrian founded Antinoopolis, sponsored cult and games, and promoted images of the divinized youth.

Cassius Dio 69.11 reports competing explanations: accident, magical/sacrificial death, and Hadrian’s claim to have seen Antinous as a new star.

### Sacrifice story

**Ancient allegation/tradition, not established fact.** Dio reports that Antinous may have volunteered to die for Hadrian, because occult rites supposedly required a substitute life. Later biographies embellished this into an oracle declaring that the object Hadrian loved most must die. The exact oracle narrative is not securely contemporary and should not be presented as proven motive.

### The celestial figure

Ptolemy’s catalogue proves that stars near Aquila were associated with Antinous by c. 150. It does not prove who chose the exact stars. Hadrian’s court is likely, but “Hadrian personally drew the constellation in 132” is stronger than the evidence.

### Why the eagle carries him

The iconography deliberately assimilated Antinous to Ganymede: beautiful imperial beloved, carried heavenward by the divine eagle. This gave a new Roman cult a classical celestial grammar.

### Suppression and survival

Antinous was not officially “abolished in 1930” by a special vote. It was absent from the IAU’s accepted 88 and disappeared within Aquila’s region. Its image nevertheless survives in historical atlases and modern reconstructions.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

MUL.TI₈.MUŠEN is the most persuasive antecedent. Neighboring Mesopotamian constellations differed from the Greek arrangement, so one should compare star groups rather than overlay entire mythic maps.

### Arabic and Bedouin

The main indigenous pattern is:

- α, β, γ Aql: *al-nasr al-ṭāʾir*, the flying eagle
- Vega and neighbors: *al-nasr al-wāqiʿ*, the falling or alighting eagle
- The two form a culturally meaningful opposition across the Milky Way.

The Greek Aquila translated as *al-ʿuqāb* coexisted with this system in Arabic scientific books. Confusing the two produces erroneous statements that al-Ṣūfī merely translated every Western name.

### Chinese

Chinese astronomy distributed modern Aquila among several asterisms rather than recognizing one eagle:

- **河鼓 Hé Gǔ**, “River Drum”: α, β, γ Aql.
- Altair specifically: **河鼓二 Hé Gǔ èr**, “Second Star of the River Drum.”
- **牵牛 Qiānniú**, “Leading/Oxherd,” and **牛郎星 Niúláng xīng**, “Cowherd Star,” became cultural names for Altair.
- Other Aquila stars belonged to asterisms including **右旗 Yòu Qí**, the Right Flag; **天桴 Tiān Fú**, the Heavenly Raft; and stars near the Heavenly Market Enclosure.

#### Cowherd and Weaver Girl

**Chinese legend with early poetic roots and later narrative development.** The *Shijing/Book of Odes* mentions the Draught Ox and Weaver as celestial figures, but the fully developed romantic story is later.

Niulang/Altair and Zhinu/Vega are separated by the Heavenly River, the Milky Way. They meet on the seventh night of the seventh lunar month when magpies form a bridge. β and γ Aql are often interpreted as their children.

**Dating caution.** The early poems establish the celestial occupational figures, not every element of the modern Qixi romance. The marriage, punishment, children, and magpie bridge accumulated over Han and later literature.

### Japan

The Chinese legend became **Tanabata**. Vega is Orihime and Altair Hikoboshi. The seventh-night festival is historically documented in Japan from the Nara period, combining imported Chinese ritual with Japanese weaving observances.

### Korea and Vietnam

Related Chilseok and Thất Tịch traditions identify Altair and Vega with separated lovers. Details differ; the generic label “the Asian Vega–Altair legend” hides distinct textual and ritual histories.

### India

The α–β–γ Aql group is commonly identified with **Śravaṇa**, the twenty-second *nakṣatra*, “hearing” or “the ear,” associated with Viṣṇu. Its three stars are interpreted as Vishnu’s three strides or footprints.

**Documented Indian astronomical equivalence.** Sanskrit astronomical tables use α Aql as the principal reference star of Śravaṇa.

**Tradition/modern synthesis.** Calling the whole Western Aquila “Garuda” is common in modern Indian popular astronomy and is mythologically apt, but the classical *nakṣatra* is Śravaṇa, not simply a translated eagle constellation. Garuda stories—the eagle-like vehicle of Vishnu, enemy of snakes, and seeker of amṛta—are independent mythological material later mapped onto the Western figure.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

Biblical *nesher* imagery includes the swift, high-flying eagle and the four-faced living creature of Ezekiel. Revelation’s eagle also inspired Christian astronomical allegory.

**Early-modern tradition.** Christian Kabbalists and antiquarians mapped zodiacal figures to the twelve tribes and sometimes associated an eagle with Dan or with a transformed Scorpio. Aquila is not zodiacal, making direct Dan–Aquila claims structurally problematic.

**Finding:** No primary Jewish astronomical or Kabbalistic text was located that securely establishes the modern Aquila star group as a canonical Hebrew constellation. Later “Hebrew zodiac” charts should be identified by their early-modern authors, not projected into biblical antiquity.

### Christianized sky

Schiller’s 1627 replacement was **Saint Catherine of Alexandria, Virgin and Martyr**. The eagle’s territory became the saint’s figure, while Schiller replaced the pagan northern constellations generally with New Testament saints and Christian subjects.

Caesius and other Protestant constellation interpreters also proposed:

- the eagle of Saint John
- the Roman imperial eagle
- biblical eagles in Exodus, Ezekiel, or Revelation

These were exegetical overlays, not observational discoveries.

### Alchemical and Hermetic interpretation

**Tradition with weak Aquila-specific evidence.** The eagle is a major alchemical symbol for sublimation, volatility, spirit, and mercurial ascent. Hermetic emblem books pair eagles with lions, serpents, suns, and celestial ascent.

**Finding:** No authoritative ancient Hermetic text was found that assigns those operations specifically to the astronomical constellation Aquila. Modern esoteric works often move freely from symbolic eagle to constellation without a historical textual bridge. Those readings are legitimate as later reception but not evidence for ancient Aquila lore.

### Astrology

Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* 1.9, assigns the stars of Aquila a nature like **Mars and Jupiter**. This is technical ancient astrology, not the constellation myth.

Manilius, *Astronomica* 5, associates the Eagle’s rising with martial, predatory, or ambitious dispositions and professions involving seizure or spoils.

Later fixed-star astrology applies Mars–Jupiter qualities to Altair: boldness, command, military fortune, sudden advancement, and danger. Much of the detailed wording in modern astrology comes from nineteenth- and twentieth-century compendia rather than directly from Ptolemy.

#### Behenian stars

**Correction.** Altair is not normally one of the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars of medieval ritual magic. Vega is. Lists that include Altair generally represent expanded modern compilations.

#### Lunar mansions

- Indian Śravaṇa directly uses the Altair group.
- In Arabic lunar-mansion astronomy, the ecliptic mansions do not center on Altair because Aquila lies appreciably north of the ecliptic.
- Chinese lunar mansions intersect the broader sky region through a different equatorial division, but Hé Gǔ itself is an asterism rather than one of the twenty-eight mansion determinatives.

## Boundary history and exchange of stars

### Ptolemy

Ptolemy described nine figured Aquila stars plus stars “around Aquila” associated with Antinous, conventionally totaling nineteen in the combined discussion. There were no lines enclosing territory.

### Bayer

Bayer formalized letter designations and pictured a larger iconic Eagle carrying Ganymede. Stars remained capable of appearing in adjoining cataloguing traditions; figure edges did not settle ownership.

### Tycho and Kepler

Tycho’s separate Antinous table removed seven stars from ordinary Aquila cataloguing for practical purposes. Kepler’s publication consolidated that division.

### Hevelius

Hevelius enlarged the catalogued populations to 23 Aquila and 19 Antinous stars. Some apparent “growth” reflects deeper naked-eye limiting magnitude and catalogue practice, not conquest from neighboring figures.

### Lacaille

No evidence supports a significant Lacaille redrawing of Aquila. His new southern figures lay far away. Mentioning him in Aquila’s boundary history mainly demonstrates that not every famous uranographer altered every constellation.

### Bode

Bode’s much denser star field and attempted divisions made Aquila and Antinous visually distinct but contiguous. His plate also preserves the overlapping relation with Sagitta and the crowded Milky Way.

### Delporte

The modern polygon absorbed Antinous into Aquila and set exact borders against nine neighbors. Most pre-existing Bayer and Flamsteed designations were preserved.

**Loss/gain summary:**

- Aquila effectively **reabsorbed Antinous**.
- Scutum, introduced by Hevelius, claimed a southern Milky Way field that earlier loose Aquila/Antinous drawings could visually approach.
- Delphinus received ρ Aql physically through the star’s later proper-motion crossing, not through a boundary alteration.
- Some historical stars retain designations from a constellation different from their present position; nomenclature is archival, boundaries geometric.

## Iconography

### Mesopotamian imagery

Birds of prey occur on Mesopotamian seals and boundary stones, but identifying any isolated eagle image as MUL.TI₈.MUŠEN requires accompanying astronomical context. Older star-lore books often made overly confident matches from a bird motif alone.

### Farnese Atlas, Rome, second century CE

The marble Farnese Atlas, now in the Museo Archeologico Nazionale, Naples, includes Aquila among the classical constellation reliefs. It is the oldest surviving monumental representation of much of the Greco-Roman celestial sphere.

**Dispute.** Bradley Schaefer argued that the positions encode Hipparchus’s lost catalogue and an epoch near 125 BCE. Other historians have challenged the precision and inference. The sculpture securely documents the ancient figure; its exact scientific prototype remains disputed. [Schaefer study](https://www.phys.lsu.edu/farnese/JHAFarneseProofs.pdf).

### Aratean manuscripts, ninth–fifteenth centuries

Carolingian and later manuscripts of Germanicus’s *Aratea* show Aquila as a spread-winged bird, often isolated, sometimes with constellation stars marked on the body. Major witnesses include:

- Leiden, Universiteitsbibliotheek, Voss. Lat. Q.79, ninth century
- Boulogne-sur-Mer, Bibliothèque municipale, MS 188
- Vatican and Madrid Aratean manuscripts
- the *Handbook of 809* tradition

Images vary because copyists transmitted both text and pictorial cycles; star dots frequently drift away from the anatomical positions stated in the catalogue.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

From the eleventh century onward, manuscripts of al-Ṣūfī show two Eagles: mirror images for the external globe-view and direct sky-view. Surviving copies are often centuries later than al-Ṣūfī himself, so an illustration’s manuscript date must not be mistaken for the work’s composition date, 964.

### Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

Dürer’s northern hemisphere print renders Aquila within a learned Ptolemaic-Aratean sky. Its orientation is the external globe convention.

### Celestial globes

- Caspar Vopel, Cologne, 1536: early separate Antinous.
- Gerard Mercator, Louvain, 1551: Aquila and Antinous/Ganymede region.
- Willem Janszoon Blaeu and later Dutch globemakers: elaborate Baroque eagle imagery.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer shows a muscular eagle clutching a naked youth labeled or catalogically identified as Ganymede. Stars are placed against anatomy and supplied Greek letters. [High-resolution plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aquila_Uranometria.jpg).

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

Aquila’s stars become Saint Catherine. Engravings are by Lucas Kilian after designs associated with Johann Matthias Kager. [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720).

### Cellarius, Amsterdam, 1660/61

Andreas Cellarius’s *Harmonia Macrocosmica* reproduces both pagan and Schillerian Christian heavens, making the atlas a major witness to competing iconographic systems.

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Hevelius pictures the constellations in globe orientation. Aquila and Antinous are treated distinctly; comparison with Bayer therefore requires reversing left and right.

### Bode, Berlin, 1782 and 1801

Bode’s eagle spreads above Antinous amid many more stars than earlier atlases. The *Uranographia* combines scientific density with late-Baroque neoclassical figure engraving. It is one of the fullest visual statements of the Aquila–Antinous synthesis before pictorial figures disappeared from professional charts.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* closely follows Bode’s visual tradition while simplifying it for a British audience.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, c. 1824–1825

The pierced, hand-colored card “Aquila and Antinous” was based substantially on Jamieson. Light behind the card made major stars shine through. The set was advertised as designed “by a lady,” but that authorship remains unidentified; Sidney Hall engraved the plates. [Collection description](https://dorok.framer.website/collection/urania-s-mirror).

### Burritt, United States, 1833 onward

Burritt’s educational atlas presents “Aquila et Antinous,” transmitting the combined figure into American school astronomy. [Smithsonian catalogue](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1823231).

### Coins and state imagery

Eagles appear abundantly on Greek, Ptolemaic, Roman, Byzantine, and modern coins as emblems of Zeus, Jupiter, empire, and sovereignty. Antinous also appears on provincial Roman coinage.

**Caution:** An eagle on a coin is not automatically the constellation Aquila. A celestial identification requires stars, zodiacal context, a globe, or an inscription.

### Ceilings, portals, calendars

Aquila occurs in medieval zodiacal and Aratean cycles, manuscript calendars, globes, and astronomical ceilings. Christian tetramorph eagles on cathedral portals normally signify Saint John, not the constellation. Some Renaissance ceilings deliberately mix planetary, zodiacal, and non-zodiacal figures; each must be assessed individually.

## People, navigation, events, and works

### Navigation

Altair’s brightness and proximity to the celestial equator made it useful for orientation, timekeeping, latitude estimation, and seasonal reckoning across Eurasia and the Pacific.

- Arab and Persian astronomers used it in star tables and astrolabes.
- Indian astronomers used α Aql as Śravaṇa’s reference.
- Chinese and Japanese calendars used the seasonal visibility of Altair and Vega in seventh-night traditions.
- Bugis navigators call Altair an eastern star in traditional navigation.
- Pacific traditions preserve separate names for Altair, but those should not be collapsed into the Western eagle.

Claims that a named European explorer “steered by Aquila” require logbook evidence. General navigational usefulness is documented; particular unsourced anecdotes were not traceable.

### Scientific observers

- Ptolemy catalogued the classical star group.
- ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī revised magnitudes and recorded Arabic lore.
- Tycho Brahe separately tabulated Aquila and Antinous.
- Bayer established the enduring Greek-letter designations.
- Hevelius expanded their naked-eye catalogues.
- Pigott discovered η Aql’s variability.
- Numerous independent observers noticed Nova Aquilae 1918; it became a defining naked-eye nova.
- Modern interferometry made Altair one of the first ordinary main-sequence stars whose oblate surface was spatially resolved.

### Literature

- Dante, *Purgatorio* 9.19–30, dreams of a golden eagle carrying him aloft “where Ganymede left his companions.” It is a literary transformation of the Ganymede ascent, not a star catalogue.
- Tennyson’s *In Memoriam A.H.H.* invokes “the young Antinous” and celestial commemoration in the broader Victorian reception of the youth.
- Numerous modern poems titled “Altair” or “Aquila” depend on the Summer Triangle and separated-lovers traditions.
- Ovid’s Ganymede passage became the principal Latin literary source for Renaissance and neoclassical eagle-and-youth art.

### Painting and sculpture

Ganymede and the Eagle was treated by:

- ancient Greek vase painters and Hellenistic jewelers
- Roman sculptors
- Michelangelo in a celebrated drawing for Tommaso de’ Cavalieri
- Correggio, *The Abduction of Ganymede*, c. 1531–1532
- Rubens and Rembrandt

These images illustrate the mythic identity later attached to Aquila, although most are not astronomical charts.

### Music

Ganymede and the eagle appear in operatic, cantata, and ballet subjects. “Altair” is used widely as a title in contemporary electronic, orchestral, and popular music, but no single canonical composition defines the constellation’s reception.

### Ships, aircraft, and spaceflight

**Documented modern naming, sometimes only indirectly astronomical:**

- Numerous naval vessels have borne Latin **Aquila**, including the unfinished Italian aircraft carrier *Aquila*. Most invoke the eagle emblem rather than explicitly the constellation.
- **Altair** was NASA’s proposed lunar-surface access module for the Constellation program, named after α Aql. It was cancelled with that program.
- Apollo 11’s lunar module was **Eagle**, producing modern popular associations with Aquila, although its official name referred directly to the American national emblem.
- The Japanese **HITOMI/ASTRO-H** X-ray observatory was initially called ASTRO-H, not Aquila; similarly named projects should not be conflated.
- “Aquila” is used for sounding rockets, aircraft, drones, observatories, software, schools, and buildings. In many cases the immediate referent is simply Latin “eagle.”

## Controversies and disputes

### Was Aquila Greek, Babylonian, or Egyptian?

**Best assessment:** Mesopotamia offers the earliest securely named Eagle in the corresponding region. Greek astronomy inherited or paralleled parts of Mesopotamian uranography but supplied its own Zeus-centered myths. Egyptian-Horus equivalence is possible but unproved.

“Greek Aquila is merely the Babylonian bird renamed” is too strong; “the two are unrelated” ignores the positional and historical evidence.

### Eagle or vulture?

Arabic *nasr* and some Greek/Latin bird words do not align neatly with modern species. “Flying Vulture” is historically legitimate, but the IAU English gloss is Eagle. The choice does not identify a zoological species.

### Did Altair’s name belong only to α?

Originally the Arabic phrase referred to the α–β–γ asterism. Medieval and early-modern transmission contracted it into the proper name of α. Both statements are true at different dates.

### Are Alshain and Tarazed Arabic?

They entered Western astronomy through Arabic-language scholarship but are generally Persian-derived. Calling them unqualifiedly “Arabic words meaning falcon” obscures their transmission and the balance-beam interpretation.

### Which star is Deneb el Okab?

Historical sources divide the “eagle’s tail” name between ε and ζ Aql. The modern IAU name **Okab** belongs to ζ Aql. Older records must be identified by coordinates, not name alone.

### Did Tycho invent Antinous?

No. This is a documented older error. Ptolemy mentions Antinous c. 150 CE; Vopel depicts it in 1536 before Tycho. Tycho’s contribution was a separate catalogue group.

### Did Hadrian create it in 132?

**Plausible tradition, imprecisely dated.** The celestial cult began after Antinous’s death in 130 and existed by Ptolemy. No surviving contemporary decree says “Hadrian created this constellation in 132.”

### Was Antinous one of Ptolemy’s 48?

No, not as an independent top-level heading. It is a named subfigure among stars around Aquila. Saying “Ptolemy’s constellation Antinous” is understandable shorthand but taxonomically misleading.

### Did the IAU abolish Antinous at Rome in 1922?

Not by a discrete documented condemnation. The IAU standardized 88 constellation names and abbreviations; Antinous was not included. Delporte’s 1930 system then placed the territory within official regions, chiefly Aquila.

### Is the 389 object a supernova?

Unproved. The report is late, poorly localized, and compatible with a nova or comet. No remnant identification closes the case.

### Are Aquilids one meteor shower?

No. “Aquilid” has been used for multiple weak radiants. Some are established or provisionally catalogued; others were historical statistical groupings not recovered by modern surveys.

### Is NGC 6863 a cluster?

Modern photometry and kinematics say no: it is an asterism.

### Is W49B the youngest Galactic black hole?

It is a candidate. The distorted remnant and absence of a detected neutron star support the hypothesis, but absence of detection is not proof.

### Is Altair part of the Behenian fifteen?

Not in the standard medieval list. Its inclusion is a modern expansion or confusion with Vega.

### Is Aquila Horus?

Unverified reconstruction. Falcon symbolism alone is insufficient.

### Is the Summer Triangle ancient?

Its component stars were culturally linked in several traditions, especially the Chinese river legend, but the English geometric name “Summer Triangle” is modern.

### Did Aquila contain the first pulsar?

No. CP 1919 is in Vulpecula.

### Hoaxes and unsupported claims

The dedicated search did not uncover a major historical “Aquila hoax” comparable to famous lunar or Martian hoaxes. It did reveal recurrent unsupported claims:

- that Altair means simply “eagle”;
- that Tycho invented Antinous;
- that Antinous was created precisely in 132 by a documented decree;
- that the 389 transient was certainly a supernova;
- that Horus was definitively the Egyptian Aquila;
- that every eagle in coinage or alchemy denotes the constellation;
- that Aquila hosted the first pulsar;
- that NGC 6863 is a physical cluster;
- that one continuous Aquilid shower lasts most of summer.

## Open questions

1. **Exact Babylonian extent.** Which modern stars beyond α–β–γ belonged to MUL.TI₈.MUŠEN in each textual period?
2. **Transmission mechanism.** Was Greek Aquila directly borrowed from Mesopotamian scholarly astronomy, inherited through intermediary traditions, or independently elaborated around a borrowed core?
3. **Egyptian identification.** Can a securely dated Egyptian star list, rather than an iconographic resemblance, tie Altair to Horus or a named falcon constellation?
4. **Original *Catasterismi*.** How much of chapter 30 belongs to Eratosthenes’ third-century BCE compilation and how much to later epitomators?
5. **Antinous’s foundation.** Did Hadrian select the stars personally, or did Alexandrian court astronomers formalize an imperial cult claim?
6. **Ptolemy’s wording.** Does “upon which is Antinous” imply a recognized drawn figure, a courtly nickname, or a catalogue annotation?
7. **389 transient.** Is there an East Asian, Syriac, Armenian, or physical-remnant counterpart that can be securely matched?
8. **Bode collection count.** What is the source of the supplied “830 stars in 109 groups” metadata? It does not describe Bode’s complete 1801 catalogue.
9. **Historical boundaries.** Exact star-by-star comparison among Bayer, Hevelius, Flamsteed, and Bode remains complicated by copying, coordinate precession, duplicated names, and inconsistent Antinous anatomy.
10. **Hebrew/Kabbalistic mapping.** A primary Jewish source explicitly identifying the α–β–γ Aql group as *Nesher* remains to be demonstrated.
11. **Aquilid streams.** Improved orbital datasets may merge, divide, confirm, or delete several weak working-list showers.
