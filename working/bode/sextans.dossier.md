# Sextans: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **Kept by the International Astronomical Union.**  
> **Official name:** *Sextans*  
> **Abbreviation:** **Sex**  
> **Genitive:** **Sextantis**  
> **Meaning:** the Sextant  
> **Modern boundaries:** fixed under Eugène Delporte’s system, approved by the IAU in 1928 and published in 1930.  
> **Present neighbors:** Leo, Hydra, and Crater.  
> **Origin:** a modern constellation introduced by Johannes Hevelius as *Sextans Uraniae*, “Urania’s Sextant.” It is not a classical Greek, Roman, Babylonian, or Egyptian constellation.

## Method and evidentiary labels

The following labels are used throughout:

- **Documented fact:** stated in a contemporary primary source or an official astronomical record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion reached by comparing historical catalogues, coordinates, or iconography.
- **Tradition:** a story or identification repeatedly transmitted, but not necessarily contemporary with the event.
- **Disputed claim:** an attribution or interpretation for which credible alternatives exist.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in modern literature but not located in an adequate primary source.
- **Legend:** narrative elaboration not securely supported by contemporary evidence.
- **Myth:** religious or literary narrative not intended here as empirical history.
- **Modern invention:** a post-antique constellation, name, interpretation, or cultural association.

## Basic identification

### Official identity

**Documented fact.** The IAU recognizes Sextans as one of the 88 constellations. Its official three-letter abbreviation is **Sex**, and the possessive Latin form used in stellar designations is **Sextantis**. The IAU gives the English meaning “the Sextant.” Its present area is approximately **314 square degrees**, making it about the 47th-largest constellation.

The official abbreviation descends from the standardized list proposed by Henry Norris Russell in 1922. The constellation itself was retained because it had become an established part of European stellar catalogues and atlases after Hevelius. It was not one of the many eighteenth-century inventions discarded during standardization.

**Documented fact.** Delporte’s boundaries were approved at the IAU General Assembly at Leiden in 1928 and published in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* in 1930. The boundaries were drawn along hour circles and parallels for epoch B1875.0, later transformed computationally to other epochs. The IAU required known variable stars to remain, as far as practicable, in the constellations whose designations they already bore. [IAU constellation history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### Position and boundaries

Sextans lies astride the celestial equator, principally south of Leo and north of the long body of Hydra. Its official border also meets Crater. In approximate J2000 terms it spans right ascensions around 9h 41m–10h 51m and declinations from roughly +6.4° to −11.7°. Because precession alters coordinate grids, these modern-coordinate figures are not the literal numbers printed by Delporte for epoch B1875.

The Moon and planets can occasionally enter its northeastern part because that corner approaches the ecliptic. Sextans is nevertheless not a zodiacal constellation.

### What the figure comprises

There is no universally canonical line figure. Contemporary educational charts commonly reduce it to a sparse angular line involving α, β, and sometimes γ Sextantis. Hevelius’s pictorial figure was much fuller: an open-framed astronomical sextant laid across Hydra’s back.

**Documented fact.** Hevelius described his original group as stars previously unclaimed between Leo and Hydra. Later summaries give **twelve original stars**. The phrase “the twelve unclaimed stars” appears in nineteenth-century constellation literature and in R. H. Allen’s synthesis; the surviving Hevelian catalogue and plate should be preferred to later counts because historical writers did not always count accessory or unformed stars consistently. [Allen, *Star Names*, “Sextans”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Sextans%2A.html)

### Names and etymology

#### Latin

- **Sextans Uraniae** — “Urania’s Sextant.”
- **Sextans** — “sextant,” literally an instrument whose graduated arc originally represented a sixth of a circle.
- **Genitive:** *Sextantis*.
- *Uraniae* is the genitive of *Urania*, the Muse of astronomy.

Latin *sextans* originally meant “a sixth part,” from *sex*, “six.” It was also the name of a Roman coin and weight equal to one-sixth of an *as*. The instrument acquired the name because its arc was normally 60 degrees, one sixth of a circle. The etymological relationship is secure; arguments over whether the fractional noun was internally formed in Latin or influenced by Greek ἑξᾶς concern the older common noun, not Hevelius’s constellation name.

#### Hevelius’s form

**Documented fact.** The atlas plate is headed **SEXTANS URANIAE**. Hevelius’s accompanying Latin repeatedly speaks of *Uraniae Sextans*, “Urania’s sextant.” The Muse’s possessive identifies the instrument as astronomical, not specifically as a mariner’s reflecting sextant.

#### Bode’s labels

**Documented fact.** Bode retained the Latin form **Sextans Uraniae** in the *Uranographia* tradition. In German explanatory use the object is **der Sextant**. Bode’s 1782 *Vorstellung der Gestirne* and his 1801 atlas continued to depict an open-sight astronomical instrument rather than the compact double-reflection marine sextant now familiar to navigators.

The 1801 companion volume was bilingual German and French:

- German: **Sextant**
- French: **Sextant**
- Latin atlas label: **Sextans Uraniae**

Bode’s atlas contains more than 17,200 plotted stellar and nebulous entries across more than one hundred traditional and proposed constellations. [Bode, *Uranographia*, ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [Bode, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

#### Vernacular names

These are translations or adaptations of the modern Latin figure, not independent ancient names:

- English: **Sextant**, formerly **Urania’s Sextant**
- German: **Sextant**
- French: **Sextant**
- Spanish: **Sextante**
- Italian: **Sestante**
- Portuguese: **Sextante**
- Dutch: **Sextant**
- Polish: **Sekstant**
- Modern Chinese: **六分仪座 / 六分儀座**, *Liùfēnyí Zuò*, “constellation of the sextant/sixth-part instrument”
- Modern Japanese: **ろくぶんぎ座**, *Rokubungi-za*, “sextant constellation”

No securely documented medieval vernacular name exists because the constellation had not yet been created.

#### Greek

There is no ancient Greek constellation name. A descriptive modern Greek translation is possible, but it has no historical standing comparable with the ancient names of Leo or Hydra. *Urania* derives from Greek Οὐρανία, “heavenly,” from οὐρανός, “sky/heaven.”

#### Arabic and Persian

There is no name for Sextans as a constellation in al-Ṣūfī’s tenth-century *Book of the Fixed Stars*, because al-Ṣūfī followed the 48 Ptolemaic figures and lived seven centuries before Hevelius. Modern Arabic normally translates or transliterates the European constellation as the sextant or “instrument of the sixth”; modern Persian likewise uses a modern equivalent or transliteration of “sextant.” These should not be represented as traditional Arabic or Persian star figures.

#### Hebrew

No securely attested ancient or medieval Hebrew constellation corresponding to Sextans was found. Modern Hebrew nomenclature translates the instrument. Attempts to assign it to biblical tribal, zodiacal, or Kabbalistic schemes are modern overlays.

### No Babylonian or Egyptian Sextans

**Documented absence.** No Babylonian constellation, Egyptian decan, or Greco-Egyptian figure equivalent to Hevelius’s sextant has been identified in the same small patch of sky.

That does not mean its individual stars were necessarily unnoticed. It means that available Babylonian and Egyptian records do not preserve a matching instrument-shaped group. Claims that “Sextans” is ancient because surveying instruments existed in Egypt confuse the history of instruments with the history of the constellation.

## Stars and objects

## Principal stars

Sextans is exceptionally faint. Only α Sextantis is brighter than magnitude 5.0, and none of its stars has an IAU-approved traditional proper name. Thus “Alpha Sextantis” is a Bayer-style designation, not a proper name comparable with Sirius or Regulus.

| Star | Flamsteed number | Approx. visual magnitude | Character |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| α Sextantis | 15 Sextantis | 4.48–4.49 | A-type giant; brightest star |
| γ Sextantis | 8 Sextantis | about 5.07 | A-type multiple/double system |
| β Sextantis | 30 Sextantis | about 5.08 | late-B star; chemically peculiar/variable classifications reported |
| δ Sextantis | 29 Sextantis | about 5.19 | late-B main-sequence star |
| ε Sextantis | 22 Sextantis | about 5.25 | F-type evolved star |
| 18 Sextantis | 18 | about 5.64 | K giant |
| 19 Sextantis | 19 | about 5.77 | K giant |
| 25 Sextantis / SS Sex | 25 | about 5.94–5.98 | magnetic chemically peculiar α² CVn variable |
| 35 Sextantis | 35 | about 6 | hierarchical multiple system |

Current distances vary between catalogues because Hipparcos and Gaia astrometry do not always yield the same inferred distances, especially for bright or multiple stars. For α Sextantis, older references commonly give about 280 light-years, while Gaia-based solutions have sometimes implied a larger distance. This is a catalogue-model issue, not evidence that the star itself changed location.

### Bayer letters and their late origin

**Important correction.** Johann Bayer could not have assigned Greek letters to Sextans in the 1603 *Uranometria*, because the constellation did not yet exist.

John Flamsteed subsequently catalogued and numbered stars under Sextans; historical accounts give 41 Flamsteed-numbered entries. In the nineteenth century Francis Baily considered lettering its stars but left the faint constellation without Greek designations. Benjamin Apthorp Gould eventually assigned **α through ε** in the *Uranometria Argentina* of 1877. Consequently the familiar “Bayer letters” in Sextans are Gould’s later extensions of Bayer’s system.

### Proper names

**Documented absence.** The IAU Working Group on Star Names has not approved an ancient proper name for α, β, γ, δ, or ε Sextantis. Popular pages sometimes call α Sextantis simply “Sextans,” “the Sextant,” or “the equator star”; none is an official stellar proper name.

### Multiple and variable stars

- **γ Sextantis:** a visual/multiple system and one of the more accessible telescopic stellar targets.
- **35 Sextantis:** a hierarchical triple. The wide pair consists of evolved stars, while one component is itself spectroscopic.
- **β Sextantis:** reported as a low-amplitude magnetic or α² Canum Venaticorum-type variable in modern catalogues.
- **23 Sextantis = RS Sextantis:** a B-type pulsating-variable candidate with β Cephei/slowly pulsating B-star characteristics.
- **25 Sextantis = SS Sextantis:** a chemically peculiar late-B star and α² CVn variable.
- **Y Sextantis:** a W Ursae Majoris contact eclipsing binary; variability was announced by Cuno Hoffmeister in 1934.
- **RW Sextantis:** a nova-like cataclysmic variable with a luminous accretion disk and wind. “Nova-like” is a physical class; it is not evidence for a historically observed naked-eye nova.
- **SW Sextantis:** prototype of the important **SW Sex** class of nova-like cataclysmic variables. The class name now applies to systems in many constellations.
- **LHS 292:** a nearby, very faint M-dwarf flare star, roughly 15 light-years away.
- **UY Sextantis:** a pulsating hot subdwarf.
- **UZ Sextantis:** a compact binary/white-dwarf system in variable-star catalogues.

No securely documented historical guest star, naked-eye nova, or Galactic supernova is known as “Nova Sextantis” in the manner of Nova Persei 1901. Searches for that wording frequently return nova-like variables or extragalactic supernovae instead.

### Exoplanet systems

The official territory contains several planetary systems, including:

- **24 Sextantis**, an evolved star with interacting giant planets;
- **HD 86081**, with a close-orbiting giant planet;
- **BD−08°2823**, with at least two reported planets;
- **WASP-43**, host of the intensely studied hot Jupiter WASP-43 b;
- **WASP-127**, host of the inflated planet WASP-127 b;
- **TOI-561**, an old, metal-poor multi-planet system including an ultra-short-period rocky planet.

These systems carry the constellation genitive only where traditional stellar nomenclature does; modern survey identifiers do not become mythological star names.

## Deep-sky objects

### Messier objects

**Documented fact:** Sextans contains **no Messier object**.

### NGC 3115, the Spindle Galaxy

**Documented fact.** William Herschel discovered NGC 3115 on **22 February 1787**, cataloguing it as H I.163. Dreyer later described it as very bright, large, and extremely elongated, with a bright middle and extended nucleus. It is now classed as an edge-on lenticular galaxy and catalogued as **Caldwell 53**.

“Spindle Galaxy” is a descriptive modern nickname. It is not unique: NGC 5866 in Draco is also frequently called the Spindle Galaxy, producing a persistent popular identification ambiguity.

NGC 3115 contains a massive central black hole and an old stellar population. It hosted the poorly characterized transient **SN 1935B**, whose type is uncertain in retrospective catalogues.

[Historical identification of NGC 3115](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/ngc31.htm)

### NGC 3166, NGC 3169, and NGC 3165

NGC 3166 and NGC 3169 are a conspicuous interacting pair, with smaller NGC 3165 nearby. Their gravitational encounter has distorted their disks. NGC 3169 contains an active nucleus. Several supernovae have been reported in galaxies of this group, but transient lists vary with boundary and host assignments; individual claims should be checked against the Transient Name Server rather than treated as a single “Sextans supernova” tradition.

The phrase “NGC objects of Sextans” therefore refers to modern boundary membership, not to objects known to Hevelius or inherent in his drawing. [NGC/IC object list](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/sextans-deepsky-objects)

### Sextans A

Sextans A is a low-metallicity dwarf irregular galaxy near the outskirts of the Local Group. Its designation is positional: it was one of the conspicuous galaxies distinguished in the Sextans field. It has become important as a nearby laboratory for massive-star formation and chemically primitive interstellar material.

### Sextans B

Sextans B is another dwarf irregular near the Local Group’s edge. A 1954 paper by Edison Pettit referred to it as the “Wilson Dwarf,” an early published identification frequently cited in later histories. Cepheids discovered and studied in Sextans A and B became distance indicators.

### Sextans dwarf spheroidal galaxy

This is distinct from Sextans A and Sextans B.

**Documented fact.** Mike Irwin, Peter Bunclark, Mick Bridgeland, and Richard McMahon discovered the extremely diffuse **Sextans dwarf spheroidal galaxy** in photographic survey material, publishing the discovery in 1990. It was the eighth of the “classical” Milky Way dwarf spheroidals recognized. Its extremely low surface brightness explains why it escaped visual discovery.

Its stellar population is predominantly ancient and metal-poor. Studies have identified RR Lyrae variables, anomalous Cepheids, and Mira variables; possible spatial substructure and a relic cluster have been debated. [MNRAS history and stellar-population study](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/476/1/71/4828392)

### COSMOS Redshift 7, CR7

**Documented fact.** CR7 was announced in 2015 as an exceptionally luminous Lyman-α-emitting galaxy at redshift approximately **z = 6.6**, in the COSMOS survey field, which lies in Sextans.

Its formal name means **COSMOS Redshift 7**. The discoverers also enjoyed the coincidence with Portuguese footballer Cristiano Ronaldo’s familiar “CR7” nickname; contemporary ESO publicity acknowledged the allusion.

**Contested interpretation.** The original paper suggested that part of the system might contain very metal-poor or Population III-like stars, or alternatively a direct-collapse black hole. Later observations and modeling weakened the strongest “first-generation stars discovered” headlines. CR7 remains an important high-redshift galaxy, but the existence of a pristine Population III population there was never established as settled fact.

The popular claim that “a galaxy was officially named after Cristiano Ronaldo” is therefore misleading. The survey/redshift expansion is genuine; the football reference was an intentional secondary joke, not the sole scientific derivation.

## Meteor showers

### Daytime Sextantids

**Documented fact.** The **Daytime Sextantids**, IAU shower number **221**, code **DSX**, are an established shower whose radiant lies near Sextans. The shower is active approximately in September and early October, peaking near the end of September or beginning of October depending on dataset and solar-longitude convention. Its geocentric speed is about 31–32 km/s.

Because the radiant is close to the Sun, the shower is primarily detected by radar or radio rather than normal visual observing. Research associates it dynamically with the broader Phaethon–Geminid stream complex. [IAU Meteor Data Center](https://iaumeteordatacenter.org/); [NASA compilation of officially named showers](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/580931main_NASA_CP_2011_216469.pdf)

### Provisional or weaker Sextantid radiants

The IAU working list has included **β-Sextantids** and **α-Sextantids** as proposed or working-list showers. Presence in the working list does not by itself establish physical reality: the IAU Meteor Data Center expressly distinguishes registration and nomenclature from proof that a detected stream is real, unique, or correctly determined.

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity to the Middle Ages: no Sextans constellation

#### Babylonian period

**Documented absence.** No surviving MUL.APIN-style constellation or zodiacal figure corresponding to Sextans is known. Its modern stars lie south of the zodiacal Lion region, but any proposed equation with a Babylonian figure would require star-by-star positional evidence that has not been produced.

#### Egyptian astronomy

**Documented absence.** No securely identified Egyptian decan or temple-ceiling figure corresponds to the sextant. A sextant is also chronologically inappropriate as an ancient Egyptian optical measuring instrument.

#### Greek and Roman astronomy

**Documented absence.** Sextans does not occur in:

- Aratus, *Phaenomena*
- Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* or its Epitome
- Hipparchus’s surviving commentary
- Ptolemy, *Almagest* VII–VIII
- Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica*
- Manilius, *Astronomica*
- Germanicus’s *Aratea*
- Avienius’s *Aratea*
- the ancient scholia to Aratus or Germanicus

The stars later assigned to it were too faint to form an independent Ptolemaic constellation. They occupied the broad uncatalogued or “unformed” territory between Leo and Hydra, or were treated as external stars of those figures depending on catalogue and reconstruction.

#### Islamic astronomy

**Documented absence.** Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita*, composed around 964, contains the Ptolemaic Leo and Hydra but no Sextans. Accordingly there is no “al-Ṣūfī name for Sextans.”

Individual faint stars may belong to Arabic descriptive star lists, weather lore, or *anwāʾ* systems, but no securely demonstrated Bedouin sextant-shaped asterism was found.

#### Medieval Latin astronomy

Medieval Latin catalogues inherited Ptolemy’s 48 constellations. The relevant stars remained unformed or attached loosely to Leo/Hydra. No *Sextans Uraniae* appears before the seventeenth century.

### 1515: Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s northern and southern celestial maps of 1515 reproduce the classical sky. **Sextans is absent.** This absence is decisive evidence against claims that the figure belonged to the standard Renaissance constellation tradition before Hevelius.

### 1603: Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* likewise predates Sextans. It gives no constellation of that name and no Bayer letters in it. Modern references to the “Bayer stars of Sextans” mean later Greek-letter designations using Bayer’s convention.

### 1627: Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* was printed at Augsburg in 1627 with Johann Bayer’s collaboration. It replaced Leo with **St Thomas the Apostle** and Hydra with a Christianized river/Jordan-related figure, depending on the atlas section and interpretive level.

**Documented absence.** Schiller could not assign a saint to Sextans because Sextans did not yet exist.

### 1643: Schyrleus de Rheita and the Sudarium

**Documented but limited claim.** Antonius Maria Schyrleus de Rheita published *Oculus Enoch et Eliae* in 1645, reporting telescopic observations made in April 1643. He believed that stars in or near Leo formed the **Sudarium Veronicae**, the veil or cloth bearing Christ’s face.

Later writers connect this proposed Christian asterism with stars subsequently placed in Sextans. It is therefore a genuine pre-Hevelian attempt to impose a figure on at least part of the same sky.

**Caution:** modern summaries often state too strongly that Rheita “invented Sextans first.” He did not devise a sextant, did not use Hevelius’s boundary, and described his object relative to Leo. The overlap needs star-by-star reconstruction from Rheita’s plate or coordinates. It is an antecedent in the same region, not an earlier name for Sextans.

### 1658–1679: Hevelius’s sextant in use

**Documented fact.** Hevelius employed large open-sighted angular-measuring instruments at his observatory in Danzig/Gdańsk. His principal brass sextant had a radius of about six feet. One observer controlled a fixed sight while another directed the movable sight at the comparison star; Elisabeth Hevelius and assistants participated in observations.

The instrument was not a later compact nautical reflecting sextant. It was a large astronomical arc for measuring angular separations.

### 1670s: the telescopic-sight controversy

Robert Hooke, John Flamsteed, and others argued that telescopic sights and micrometers yielded superior stellar positions. Hevelius continued to trust carefully made open sights, seeing himself as Tycho Brahe’s successor.

It is an exaggeration to say Hevelius “refused telescopes.” He used very long telescopes for lunar, planetary, cometary, and stellar-diameter work. What he resisted was attaching telescopic sights to his principal positional instruments.

### 1679: Halley’s visit

**Documented fact.** Edmond Halley arrived in Danzig on 26 May 1679 on behalf of the Royal Society and made comparative observations with Hevelius. Contemporary and later accounts agree that Hevelius’s naked-eye/open-sight measurements performed impressively.

**Nuance and dispute.** The popular version—“Halley proved Hevelius was exactly as accurate as telescopic instruments”—is too simple. Halley left a favorable testimonial, but private correspondence afterward was more critical, calling Hevelius obstinate and suggesting improvements remained possible. The episode vindicated Hevelius’s personal skill more than it settled the general instrumental question. Telescopic sights soon became standard.

[Scholarly study of Hevelius and Halley](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160932799012636)

### 16/26 September 1679: the Danzig fire

**Documented fact.** On 16 September Old Style, 26 September New Style, a fire destroyed Hevelius’s houses, brewery, observatory, presses, books, and instruments. A contemporary report by D. Capellus to Peter Wyche states that the fire began in the stable after a servant or coachman left a burning candle. The report also records suspicions of deliberate wrongdoing, though intention was not proved.

Hevelius and Elisabeth were away at a garden outside the city. Stories that he foresaw the disaster derive from the contemporary account’s language about foreboding, but later retellings often dramatize it into supernatural premonition.

The great metal sextant and other major instruments were destroyed. Important catalogue manuscripts survived because they had been removed or rescued.

[Contemporary fire report in translation](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Hevelius_fire/)

### 1680–1685: recovery and memorial formulation

Hevelius resumed observing, including the great comet of 1680, and published *Annus Climactericus* in 1685. The title treated 1679 as his climacteric forty-ninth year of observing—seven times seven—as well as the year of Halley’s visit and the fire.

### 1687/1690: creation and publication of Sextans Uraniae

Hevelius died in 1687. His *Prodromus Astronomiae*, catalogue, and *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* were completed and published posthumously in 1690 through Elisabeth Hevelius’s work. Some plates or front matter carry 1687, explaining why sources alternate between “introduced in 1687” and “published in 1690.”

**Best formulation:** Sextans was devised and engraved by Hevelius by 1687; the completed atlas normally cited as its first publication appeared at Danzig in 1690.

The primary Latin explanation is unusually explicit. Hevelius says, in substance, that the sextant had served Urania until an immense fire snatched it away. He then explains that he placed it between Leo Major and Hydra:

> *non ex eo … quod situs sit convenientissimus, novis Stellis refertus; sed … quod Leo & Hydra animalia sint ferè ignea … hic Sextans … igneæ … Constellationis.*

That is: not because the position was especially convenient or rich in new stars, but because Leo and Hydra were considered almost fiery animals and the Sextant would therefore seem an entirely fiery constellation.

This is the documentary basis for the later phrase **“Vulcan overcame Urania.”** The exact polished English wording belongs to later retelling; Hevelius’s own Latin supplies the underlying fire-versus-astronomy conceit. [Hevelius, *Prodromus Astronomiae*, searchable facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/download/pdf/207915.pdf)

### 1690 plate

The *Firmamentum* plate depicts a large open-framed sextant placed on Hydra beneath Leo. Like Hevelius’s other constellation plates, it uses the external or globe-maker’s view, reversing the sky relative to an observer looking outward from Earth.

The engraved frame includes the graduated arc, sights, braces, pivots, and supporting structure. It is therefore an image of Hevelius’s astronomical practice, not a generic navigation emblem.

[Hevelius plate, *Sextans Uraniae*](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Johannes_Hevelius_-_Prodromus_Astronomia_-_Volume_III_%22Firmamentum_Sobiescianum%2C_sive_uranographia%22_-_Tavola_VV_-_Sextans_Uraniae.jpg)

### 1725–1729: Flamsteed

Flamsteed accepted Sextans into the British catalogue-and-atlas tradition. The posthumous *Historia Coelestis Britannica* and *Atlas Coelestis* numbered stars within it and reproduced the instrument near Leo and Hydra. Later compilations give 41 Flamsteed-numbered Sextans stars.

Its acceptance by Flamsteed was crucial: several of Hevelius’s other compounds or accessory figures later changed status, but Sextans acquired an independent catalogue identity.

### 1750s: Lacaille

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille did not invent Sextans. His major constellation inventions concerned the far southern sky observed from the Cape. European maps and catalogues of his period generally accepted the Hevelian figure, while Lacaille’s own instrumental constellations made Sextans look stylistically at home among modern scientific apparatus.

No evidence was found that Lacaille substantially redrew Sextans’s northern limits.

### 1770s–1782: Bode’s earlier atlases

Bode incorporated Sextans in his popular German works and in the 1782 *Vorstellung der Gestirne*, derived partly from the reduced French Flamsteed atlas of Jean-Baptiste Fortin. The image still resembles an open-sighted astronomical sextant.

### 1787: Herschel discovers the Spindle Galaxy

William Herschel discovered what became NGC 3115. Bode’s later catalogues could therefore populate the Sextans region not only with stars but with the new class of “nebulae” then being rapidly enlarged by Herschel.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact.** Bode published the monumental *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* at Berlin in 1801. Its twenty large copperplate charts plot more than 17,200 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters and display over one hundred accepted and proposed constellations.

Sextans appears between Leo and Hydra under the traditional Latin label **Sextans Uraniae**. Bode preserved Hevelius’s instrument figure while surrounding it with substantially denser stellar plotting and newly catalogued nebulous objects.

The bilingual companion, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne / Description et connoissance générale des constellations*, describes constellation positions and provides coordinates. Bode admits that combining different observers produced uncertainties and contradictions, which he intended to correct in later ephemerides.

**Unverified collection claim.** The phrase supplied in the brief, “the Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups),” could not be traced to Bode’s 1801 atlas, companion catalogue, a recognized library record, or standard astronomical bibliography. Its numbers conflict with Bode’s documented **17,240** catalogue entries and “more than one hundred” constellation figures. It may describe a modern digital, blockchain, NFT, or database collection derived from Bode, but without a title or URL it cannot be treated as historical evidence.

### 1822: Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* received Sextans as an established constellation. Its illustration places the instrument with Hydra and neighboring modern figures. Jamieson’s atlas strongly influenced popular British and American constellation art.

### 1824: *Urania’s Mirror*

Card 32 of *Urania’s Mirror; or, a View of the Heavens* includes **Sextans Uraniæ**, Hydra, Crater, Corvus, Felis, Antlia Pneumatica, Pyxis, Argo Navis, and other southern figures. The cards were first advertised in 1824 as the invention of “a Lady,” and pierced star holes allowed them to be held to the light.

The designs closely follow Jamieson, sometimes so closely that “adapted” and “plagiarized” have both been used. [Smithsonian record](https://www.si.edu/object/uranias-mirror%3Anmah_1460911)

### 1833–1835: Burritt

Elijah H. Burritt’s American *Atlas, Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens* includes Sextans in the seasonal map for April–June and in its planisphere. The 1835 plate shows the instrument above Argo and on Hydra’s region. Burritt’s school atlas popularized the constellation in the United States. [American Museum of Natural History copy](https://digitalcollections.amnh.org/archive/Celestial-map-from-Burritt-s-Atlas-designed-to-illustrate-the-geography-of-the-heavens-2URM1T14S658.html)

### 1843: Argelander

Friedrich Wilhelm August Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* represented the transition toward scientific naked-eye charts in which accurate magnitudes mattered more than allegorical engraving. Argelander counted about **17 naked-eye stars** in Sextans according to later constellation histories.

### 1872: Heis

Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus*, based partly on Argelander, retained Sextans. The atlas tradition of Argelander and Heis contributed to the practical northern-constellation repertoire inherited by twentieth-century standardizers.

### 1877: Gould assigns Greek letters

Benjamin Apthorp Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* supplied α, β, γ, δ, and ε. This late date explains why none of Sextans’s Greek letters is genuinely Bayerian.

### 1887: Proctor

Richard A. Proctor listed “Sextans, the Sextant” among constellations recognized in the British Association catalogue, evidence that it was fully institutionalized before the IAU.

### 1922: IAU retention and abbreviation

At the first IAU General Assembly in Rome, the Union adopted the modern constellation list and Russell’s three-letter abbreviations. Sextans was retained as **Sex**.

The IAU did not “invent” the 88 at a single stroke in 1922; it standardized a historically evolved working set. Nor were the final boundary polygons yet settled.

### 1928–1930: Delporte

Delporte’s boundaries converted Sextans from a pictorial instrument and catalogue grouping into an exact area of sky. Stars and galaxies inside that polygon are now “in Sextans” whether or not Hevelius drew them on the instrument.

The result retained territory between Leo, Hydra, and Crater. It regularized earlier overlaps and gaps rather than following the edge of Hevelius’s engraving.

### 1990: Sextans dwarf spheroidal

Irwin and collaborators discovered the Milky Way satellite now called the Sextans dwarf spheroidal.

### 2015: CR7

Sobral and collaborators announced COSMOS Redshift 7 and proposed extreme early-universe interpretations, including a possible Population III component.

### Present

Sextans remains an official IAU constellation. Modern scientific importance rests far more on its galaxies, transients, exoplanet systems, cataclysmic variables, and survey fields than on its faint naked-eye pattern.

## Invention and reception

## What Sextans honored

**Documented fact.** It honored Hevelius’s astronomical sextant, his long program of stellar measurement, and the instrument’s destruction in the 1679 fire.

It did not honor:

- a king or patron;
- a voyage;
- a marine navigator;
- a newly invented reflecting sextant;
- an ancient god’s weapon.

The possessive “Urania’s” made it an emblem of astronomy. The nearby Leo and Hydra supplied the fire symbolism. In Hevelius’s frontispiece, Urania presides over astronomical tradition while Hevelius presents his new constellations, reinforcing the constellation’s autobiographical character.

## Who invented it

The invention is securely attributable to **Johannes Hevelius**. Elisabeth Hevelius was essential to the observation program and the posthumous publication, but no source found credits her with independently designing or naming Sextans.

A careful attribution is therefore:

> Conceived and drawn under Johannes Hevelius; based on an instrument used by Johannes, Elisabeth, and their assistants; published posthumously through Elisabeth Hevelius in 1690.

## Why it survived

Sextans’s survival probably resulted from several reinforcing circumstances:

1. Hevelius’s prestige;
2. a clear, compact region not already dominated by a bright classical figure;
3. acceptance by Flamsteed and subsequent cataloguers;
4. a useful independent designation for otherwise unformed faint stars;
5. incorporation into Argelander–Heis professional practice before IAU standardization.

This is a scholarly reconstruction, not a recorded IAU statement that Sextans was retained specifically as a memorial.

## Boundary transfers

Before Hevelius, the region belonged loosely to the space between **Leo** and **Hydra**, with some stars treated as external or unformed stars. The Sudarium proposed by Rheita was placed relative to Leo. Hevelius effectively took faint stars from this inter-constellation reserve.

Flamsteed greatly expanded the numbered stellar content assigned to Sextans. Delporte then removed ambiguity by giving the constellation an exact quadrilateral-like area bounded by Leo, Hydra, and Crater.

Because historical figures did not possess legal borders, it is misleading to say Ptolemy “lost” a fixed block of Leo or Hydra in the modern territorial sense. What changed was catalogue membership first and geometric jurisdiction only in 1930.

## Myths and legends

## Classical myths: the decisive negative finding

Sextans is modern. No ancient Greek or Roman catasterism explains it. There is consequently no exhaustive chain of competing classical identities such as exists for Orion, Corona Borealis, or Ara.

### Eratosthenes

**Documented absence.** Neither the surviving *Catasterismi* nor its Epitome contains a sextant, Urania’s measuring instrument, or a figure occupying modern Sextans.

### Hyginus

**Documented absence.** Hyginus’s *Poetica Astronomica* does not include Sextans. The *Fabulae* contain no catasterism of Hevelius’s object.

### Aratus and the scholia

**Documented absence.** Aratus does not name it. The scholia to Aratus and Germanicus attach stories to Leo, Hydra, Crater, Corvus, and neighboring ancient figures, but not to a sextant between Leo and Hydra.

### Ovid

**Documented absence.** Ovid supplies myths and transformations for many constellated beings but no celestial sextant. His stories concerning the Muses or Vulcan do not constitute a Sextans catasterism.

### Manilius

**Documented absence.** *Astronomica* treats the zodiac and other ancient constellations astrologically; it has no Sextans.

### Nonnus

**Documented absence.** Nonnus’s *Dionysiaca* contains elaborate stellar and divine imagery but no catasterism of Urania’s sextant.

### Pseudo-Apollodorus

**Documented absence.** The *Bibliotheca* contains myths of Heracles, the Nemean lion, Hydra, Apollo’s raven, and other neighboring figures, but no sextant constellation.

### Pausanias

**Documented absence.** Pausanias records cults, artworks, and local myths of Urania and the Muses, but no celestial sextant.

### Farnese Atlas

The Farnese Atlas preserves a Greco-Roman sculptural celestial globe with the ancient constellations. Sextans is absent. Its stars fall in an undecorated or neighboring area; the sculpture cannot be evidence for a pre-Hevelian sextant.

## Urania

### The ancient Muse

**Myth.** Urania is one of the nine Muses. Hesiod, *Theogony* 75–79, names the daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne, including Οὐρανία. Later ancient art and literature increasingly associate her with astronomy, a celestial globe, and a pointing rod or compass.

No ancient text says she possessed Hevelius’s sextant. **“Urania’s Sextant” is Hevelius’s learned modern personification.**

### Who placed it in the sky?

In literal historical terms, Johannes Hevelius placed it there. In his allegory, Urania possessed the instrument until fire took it. There is no ancient god who catasterized it.

## Vulcan overcoming Urania

**Modern allegorical myth.** Later writers summarize the fire as “Vulcan overcame Urania.” Vulcan is the Roman god of destructive and controlled fire; Urania personifies astronomy. The opposition poetically transforms a documented workshop catastrophe into a divine contest.

The underlying idea is Hevelian and seventeenth-century. It should not be presented as a recovered Roman myth.

## Leo and Hydra as fiery animals

**Documented Hevelian astrological conceit.** Hevelius says he placed the sextant between Leo and Hydra because astrologers treated them as fiery. Leo is conventionally a fire sign. Calling Hydra fiery is less obvious in modern elemental astrology, and later popular writers sometimes “correct” or rationalize it.

The proper historical method is to report Hevelius’s statement without forcing it into a modern zodiacal-element chart. He may have meant their violent, destructive mythic natures or relied on an astrological authority not yet securely identified.

## Saint Veronica’s veil

**Christian tradition.** Veronica’s veil or sudarium is the cloth with which a woman wiped Christ’s face on the way to Calvary, receiving an image of his countenance. The canonical Gospels do not tell this story; it developed in late antique and medieval traditions combining the woman healed by Christ, the *vera icon*, and Passion devotion.

**Astronomical modern invention.** Rheita’s 1643 observation projected this sacred cloth into stars near Leo, some later associated with Sextans. It was not accepted into mainstream catalogues. Later writers preserve it chiefly as an intriguing failed constellation.

The statement that Rheita “saw the face of Christ in Sextans” is anachronistic: Sextans did not yet exist, and Rheita described the region through Leo.

## Hevelius’s premonition

**Contemporary report, later legend.** Capellus’s letter says Hevelius felt unusual distress and warned that care be taken against fire before leaving for his garden. This may record a real warning caused by ordinary concern. Later biographies transformed it into a supernatural premonition. The evidence supports foreboding and warning, not clairvoyance.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Arabic-Islamic sky

The correct comparison is with the faint stars south of Leo and north of Hydra, not with a nonexistent Arabic “Sextant.”

Al-Ṣūfī retained Ptolemy’s Leo and Hydra. Arabic stellar tradition distinguishes:

- translated Ptolemaic positional descriptions;
- traditional indigenous Arabic names and *anwāʾ*;
- later Islamic technical terminology.

No coherent Bedouin asterism using precisely α–ε Sextantis was located in the consulted scholarship. Because the stars are faint, the absence is unsurprising. Statements that al-Ṣūfī named Sextans are false.

## Chinese astronomy

The modern Sextans area lay within the broad southern-sky sector symbolized by the **Vermilion Bird**, but that does not mean Sextans itself was “the Vermilion Bird.” The Bird is a much larger directional complex.

Specific modern Sextans stars are mapped to traditional or later Chinese catalogue extensions:

- **36 Sextantis** has been identified as an added star of **Língtái**, 靈台, the Astronomical Observatory.
- **32 Sextantis** has been identified as an added star of **Chángyuán**, 長垣, the Long Wall.

The word **增**, “added,” in such Chinese stellar names often reflects stars inserted into later expanded catalogues, especially the Qing-era synthesis influenced by Jesuit astronomy. It should not automatically be read as evidence that the exact identification is ancient.

R. H. Allen, following nineteenth-century sinological sources such as John Reeves, reports a faint star in Sextans as **Tien Seang**, “Heavenly Minister of State.” The identification is uncertain because Allen’s “q, Bode 2306” uses obsolete lettering and numbering. It should be checked against the original Reeves chart before being equated with a modern Bayer or Flamsteed star.

[Hong Kong Space Museum glossary and background](https://hk.space.museum/en/web/spm/resources/teachers-corner/constellations-and-myths/glossary-of-chinese-star-regions-asterisms-and-star-names.html)

## Indian astronomy

The ecliptic and lunar mansions are the governing structures of classical Indian stellar astronomy. Most of Sextans lies away from the traditional defining stars of the *nakṣatras*. No Indian sextant figure was identified.

Because the modern northeastern boundary approaches the ecliptic, planets or the Moon may cross the IAU territory. That geometrical fact does not make Sextans an Indian lunar mansion.

## Hebrew and biblical skies

No biblical passage or securely early Jewish astronomical text names this star group. Christian and Jewish constellation harmonizations often assign the zodiac to tribes, apostles, or biblical events, but they work with Leo/Hydra or the zodiac as a whole, not Hevelius’s later Sextans.

A specific Kabbalistic assignment for Sextans could not be traced earlier than modern esoteric correspondence tables.

## Schiller’s Christianized sky

Schiller published in 1627, sixty years before Hevelius’s atlas. Therefore there is no Christian replacement for Sextans in his system. The same region belonged pictorially to Schiller’s replacements for Leo and Hydra—especially St Thomas for Leo and the Christianized Hydra/Jordan complex.

Modern assertions that Schiller assigned “St Veronica” to Sextans confuse Schiller with Rheita.

## Astrological reception

### Ptolemaic fixed-star astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* could not assign planetary natures to Sextans because the constellation did not exist. No star now called α Sextantis appears as a named Ptolemaic fixed star with its own standard influence.

### Later constellation astrology

Modern astrological writers sometimes assign Sextans qualities such as:

- mechanical ingenuity;
- mathematical ability;
- precision;
- astronomy and navigation;
- measuring, surveying, or technical skill.

These are **modern sympathetic interpretations** derived from the instrument’s function, not ancient doctrine.

### Behenian stars

None of the fifteen medieval Behenian fixed stars lies in Sextans. Claims that Sextans is a Behenian constellation are unfounded.

### Lunar mansions

No standard Arabic, Indian, or medieval European lunar mansion is defined by a principal Sextans star. The region lies below the Leo-sector mansions and is at most crossed marginally by modern geometrical extensions.

## Hermetic and alchemical readings

No pre-Hevelian Hermetic or alchemical source could identify the constellation. Later esoteric writers can read the sextant as:

- measure and proportion;
- the sixth part of a circle;
- mediation between heaven and earth;
- Urania opposed by Vulcan;
- transformation through fire.

These are legitimate modern symbolic constructions but were not found in Hevelius’s technical text as a developed alchemical doctrine. The “Vulcan versus Urania” phrase is allegorical, not evidence that Hevelius encoded an alchemical initiation.

## Iconography

### Ancient objects

- **Farnese Atlas, Roman Imperial copy of an earlier Hellenistic conception:** no Sextans.
- **Dendera and other Egyptian astronomical ceilings:** no identifiable sextant constellation.
- **Classical coins:** Roman *sextans* coins exist, named for a one-sixth denomination, but they do not depict the modern constellation. Their occurrence is etymological, not iconographic continuity.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

Illustrated manuscripts of al-Ṣūfī depict Leo and Hydra in internal and external views but not Sextans. Empty or unfigured stars in the later Sextans region should not be mistaken for a lost plate.

### Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

Dürer’s woodcut star maps show the classical figures and named astronomical authorities in the corners. No sextant appears.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

The area is absorbed into Christianized Leo/Hydra imagery. No instrument corresponding to Sextans is present.

### Hevelius, Danzig, engraved by 1687; published 1690

This is the foundational image. The instrument is large, skeletal, and technically rendered, resting across Hydra. It is shown from outside the celestial sphere, so comparisons with modern maps require horizontal reversal.

The plate title **Sextans Uraniae** and its mechanical detail make it both a constellation chart and a memorial portrait of lost instrumentation.

### Flamsteed, London, 1729

Flamsteed received the figure into the great British atlas tradition. The constellation appears beside Leo and Hydra with star positions and numbers adapted to Flamsteed’s catalogue.

### Bode, Berlin, 1782

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* depicts the simpler open-frame sextant. This popular-scale chart mediated between Flamsteed’s atlas and Bode’s later monumental work.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s *Uranographia* preserves the instrument in a densely plotted scientific-artistic field. It belongs to the last great European atlas in which elaborate allegorical figures and serious catalogue content coexist on equal terms.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson renders the figure in a cleaner pedagogical style, with Hydra and the neighboring apparatus constellations.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824

Card 32 presents *Sextans Uraniæ* as a colored instrument among Hydra, Crater, Corvus, Felis, Antlia, Argo, and Pyxis. Pricked star holes make it part image, part optical teaching device.

### Burritt, United States, 1833–1835

Burritt’s seasonal plates reproduce Sextans for mass education. Its position above Argo can visually suggest a navigational instrument, though the historical object was Hevelius’s astronomical sextant.

### Argelander and Heis, Bonn/Cologne, 1843 and 1872

The figure is progressively subordinated to points, magnitudes, and coordinate utility. These atlases mark the disappearance of full mythological and mechanical bodies from professional star charts.

### Modern charts

IAU and educational charts usually omit the pictorial sextant or reduce it to a small line. The transformation is conceptually important: the constellation is now legally an area, not a drawing.

### Architecture, ceilings, portals, and calendars

No medieval cathedral portal or ancient painted ceiling can contain Sextans as such. Apparent examples would have to be later restorations or generic instruments.

Astronomical interiors and calendars produced after 1690 may include it, but no major named ceiling cycle centered specifically on Sextans was securely located. Claims of a “medieval Sextans” in architectural zodiac cycles should be rejected unless the instrument can be dated independently of later restoration.

## People, events, and works

## Johannes Hevelius

Sextans is one of the most autobiographical constellations. It embodies:

- Hevelius’s identity as a successor to Tycho Brahe;
- his open-sight astrometry;
- collaboration with Elisabeth Hevelius and workshop assistants;
- the Hooke–Flamsteed–Halley instrumental dispute;
- destruction of the Danzig observatory;
- reconstruction of his scientific program;
- posthumous publication by Elisabeth.

## Elisabeth Hevelius

Although the constellation’s traditional history often names only Johannes, Elisabeth observed with the sextant, assisted in the catalogue, preserved the enterprise after the fire and Johannes’s death, and brought the *Prodromus* and atlas to publication. Calling it simply “Hevelius’s sextant” is historically conventional but socially incomplete.

## Edmond Halley

Halley’s 1679 visit ties Sextans to one of the major methodological transitions in astrometry. The encounter did not prove that telescopic sights were useless; it demonstrated how far an exceptionally skilled observer could push the older technology.

## William Herschel

Herschel’s 1787 observation of NGC 3115 made the constellation’s most famous telescopic object part of the deep-sky record.

## Mike Irwin and collaborators

Their 1990 discovery of the dwarf spheroidal transformed “Sextans” into the name of a Milky Way satellite, distinct from the constellation and from Sextans A and B.

## David Sobral and collaborators

Their work on CR7 attached Sextans to research on reionization, early chemical enrichment, Population III candidates, and direct-collapse black holes.

## Navigation

No evidence was found that explorers commonly navigated by the stars of Sextans. Its stars are too faint for it to be a practical traditional guide comparable with Ursa Minor, Crux, Canopus, Sirius, or the Southern Triangle.

Sextants as instruments were fundamental to maritime navigation, but that does not mean the constellation Sextans was used for navigation. Popular accounts frequently conflate these propositions.

## Literature and art

Sextans lacks the extensive literary afterlife of ancient constellations. Its strongest cultural appearances are:

- the atlases of Hevelius, Flamsteed, Bode, Jamieson, and Burritt;
- *Urania’s Mirror*;
- historical writing on Hevelius and Elisabeth;
- modern astronomical prose about the Spindle Galaxy and CR7;
- science-fictional or poetic uses of the sextant as a symbol of orientation and measure.

No canonical ancient poem, Renaissance painting, or opera is devoted to this constellation.

## NASA SEXTANT

NASA’s **Station Explorer for X-ray Timing and Navigation Technology**, abbreviated **SEXTANT**, demonstrated pulsar-based autonomous navigation using NICER hardware aboard the International Space Station. The acronym refers to the navigational instrument rather than demonstrably to the constellation, though both share the word.

[NASA SEXTANT project](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/station-explorer-for-x-ray-timing-and-navigation-sextant/)

## Ships and missions

Numerous vessels have borne names involving “Sextant” because of the navigational instrument. No historically prominent ship was found to have been named explicitly for the constellation rather than the device. That distinction should be maintained unless commissioning records state otherwise.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was Sextans introduced in 1687 or 1690?

**Resolution:** both dates describe different stages.

- 1687 appears on plates/front matter and is the year of Hevelius’s death.
- 1690 is the conventional publication date of the completed posthumous *Prodromus* and *Firmamentum*.

“Created by 1687, published in 1690” is the least misleading formula.

## 2. Was the fire on 16 or 26 September 1679?

Both are correct under different calendars:

- 16 September Old Style;
- 26 September Gregorian/New Style.

Modern biographies that give one date without calendrical explanation appear to contradict each other.

## 3. Did the fire destroy all Hevelius’s observations?

No. It destroyed instruments, books, presses, structures, and much observational material, but vital catalogue manuscripts survived. “Everything was lost” is a popular exaggeration contradicted by the eventual publication.

## 4. Did a malicious servant burn the observatory?

**Disputed.** A contemporary report says a candle left in the stable started the fire and records suspicion of deliberate action. Negligence is well attested as the immediate story; intent was never proved.

## 5. Did Halley prove naked-eye instruments were superior?

No. Halley confirmed the excellence of Hevelius’s measurements, but telescopic sights ultimately prevailed. His public testimonial and private criticism do not perfectly agree.

## 6. Did Hevelius reject telescopes?

No. He rejected or distrusted telescopic sights for key positional instruments. He was otherwise a prolific telescopic observer and telescope builder.

## 7. Is the pictured instrument a nautical sextant?

No. Hevelius’s instrument predates the mature reflecting marine sextant and is structurally a large astronomical angular-measuring frame with open sights. Later atlas art can make it look navigational, particularly when placed near Argo Navis.

## 8. Did Rheita invent Sextans first?

No. Rheita proposed the Sudarium Veronicae in approximately the same region. It was a different object, different religious interpretation, and different stellar grouping.

## 9. Is Sextans an ancient constellation?

No. It has no entry in Ptolemy, al-Ṣūfī, Bayer, or the ancient mythographers. Its documented origin lies in Hevelius’s late-seventeenth-century atlas.

## 10. Does it have classical mythology?

Not in the proper historical sense. Urania and Vulcan are classical divine figures, but their encounter over the sextant is Hevelius’s modern allegory.

## 11. Are its Greek-letter stars Bayer stars?

They use Bayer designations generically, but Bayer did not assign them. Gould supplied α–ε in 1877.

## 12. Does α Sextantis have an Arabic proper name?

No approved one is known. Websites assigning it an exotic Arabic, Persian, or Chinese name generally conflate nearby asterisms, obsolete catalogue labels, or modern commercial naming.

## 13. Is “Spindle Galaxy” unambiguous?

No. Both NGC 3115 in Sextans and NGC 5866 in Draco are widely called the Spindle Galaxy. Catalogue numbers are essential.

## 14. Was CR7 named after Cristiano Ronaldo?

Partly as wordplay, not simply or officially. “COSMOS Redshift 7” is the scientific expansion; the discoverers acknowledged the footballer’s nickname as an inspiration or pleasing coincidence. Headlines that omit the survey derivation overstate the celebrity naming.

## 15. Did CR7 prove Population III stars existed?

No. The 2015 interpretation made CR7 a candidate. Later spectroscopy and modeling made a chemically enriched population and/or accreting black-hole explanation more plausible. “First stars discovered” was a hypothesis amplified by press coverage.

## 16. Is Sextans a southern constellation?

It straddles the celestial equator and is conventionally grouped with the southern sky because most of its area and figure lie south of the equator. Calling it wholly southern is geometrically imprecise.

## 17. Is Hydra a fire sign?

Hydra is not one of the twelve zodiacal signs. Hevelius nevertheless described Leo and Hydra as nearly fiery animals “to speak with astrologers.” Attempts to force this into the modern four-element zodiac system miss the seventeenth-century wording.

## 18. Does “Sextans” mean six stars?

No. It refers to one sixth of a circle—the 60-degree arc of the instrument—not the number of stars.

## 19. Is **Sex** a modern joke abbreviation?

No. It is the official abbreviation produced mechanically from *Sextans*. Humorous treatments are secondary.

## 20. Was Sextans ever abolished?

No. It was retained by the IAU. Lists of obsolete constellations that include it are erroneous or confuse it with discarded neighboring figures such as Felis.

## 21. “830 stars in 109 groups”

No matching statement was found in Bode’s atlas or companion catalogue. Bode’s documented total is 17,240 stellar and nebulous catalogue entries, with more than one hundred constellation figures. “830 stars in 109 groups” remains an unverified description of an unidentified modern collection.

## Open questions

1. **Exact reconstruction of Rheita’s Sudarium.** A rigorous comparison of Rheita’s original diagram with modern coordinates is needed to establish precisely which present Sextans stars were included.

2. **Hevelius’s astrological source for fiery Hydra.** His language is explicit, but the particular astrological authority or chain of authorities behind it remains unidentified.

3. **Original twelve-star membership.** Later histories repeatedly say twelve stars, while catalogue transcriptions and omitted-entry problems complicate exact cross-identification. A definitive table should compare the printed 1690 catalogue, surviving manuscript, Baily’s edition, and Hipparcos identifications.

4. **Hevelius versus Flamsteed membership.** A complete coordinate-by-coordinate account would show which stars Flamsteed added, omitted, or transferred relative to Hevelius.

5. **Delporte boundary transfers.** A computational overlay of Ptolemaic descriptions, Bayer’s chart, Hevelius’s figure, Flamsteed numbering, Bode’s plotting, and the B1875 Delporte polygon would quantify precisely what territory was taken from Leo, Hydra, and Crater.

6. **Chinese identification “Tien Seang.”** Allen’s dependence on Reeves and the obsolete “q, Bode 2306” notation requires verification against the original nineteenth-century Chinese chart.

7. **Arabic vernacular asterisms.** No secure Bedouin group specific to α–ε Sextantis was found, but detailed comparison with the full *anwāʾ* literature may recover individual identifications.

8. **The Bode “on-chain” collection.** A collection title, contract address, repository, or URL is needed before its claimed 830-star/109-group structure can be evaluated.

9. **The fire memorial’s chronology.** Hevelius’s published explanation is clear, but surviving drafts, correspondence, or plate states might establish exactly when after 1679 he first conceived *Sextans Uraniae*.
