# Honores Friderici: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Status: abolished / not retained by the IAU.** Honores Friderici is not one of the 88 official constellations. It has no official constellation abbreviation, genitive, boundary, area, or meteor-shower designation. Its principal stars now lie in **Andromeda**; other stars that Bode appropriated belong under the modern boundaries to **Lacerta, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, and Pegasus**.

The statement that the IAU “abolished it in 1922” is convenient shorthand, but not literally the text of an individual abolition decree. At Rome in 1922 the IAU standardized a list of 88 constellation names and three-letter abbreviations; Honores Friderici was simply not selected. Eugène Delporte’s boundaries were proposed in 1925, approved at Leiden in 1928, and published in 1930. Those boundaries completed its disappearance as a possible territorial constellation. [IAU history of the constellations](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

**Classification:** documented modern invention, not an ancient constellation. It was conceived by the Prussian astronomer Johann Elert Bode in January 1787 as a memorial to Frederick II “the Great” of Prussia, who had died on 17 August 1786.

### Names

| Form | Language/date | Meaning and status |
|---|---|---|

**Unofficial abbreviation and genitive:** Modern obsolete-constellation lists sometimes supply **Hon** and *Frederici Honorum* or *Honorium Friderici*. These are retrospective conveniences, not IAU forms. Because the IAU did not retain the constellation, it has **no IAU abbreviation or official genitive**.

The spelling **Friderici** is the conventional Latinization of German *Friedrich*. English sources’ *Frederici*, *Friderici*, *Frederica*, and inverted word orders reflect translation and copying, not several independently invented constellations.

### Form and emblem

Bode represented the memorial as:

- a radiant or royal crown;
- a sword;
- a writing feather or pen;
- an olive branch;
- an encircling laurel;
- ribbons joining the attributes.

Bode’s own explanation is explicit:

> “Südwärts unter derselben hängen, mit dem unverwelklichen Lorbeer des Nachruhms umwunden: Ein Schwerdt, eine Feder und ein Oelzweig, um diesen ewig unvergesslichen Monarchen als Helden, Weisen und Friedensstifter zu bezeichnen.”

That is: beneath the crown hang a sword, pen, and olive branch, entwined with the imperishable laurel of posthumous fame, marking Frederick as **hero, sage, and peacemaker**. This is documented royal panegyric, not an inherited mythology. [Bode’s account transcribed and discussed by the All Skies Encyclopaedia](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Honores_Friderici)

### Position and boundaries

Bode described it as lying between **Cassiopeia, Cepheus, Andromeda, and Cygnus**, along the southern edge of the Milky Way and near Cepheus’s crown. His figure occupied the northern hand and end of the chain of Andromeda and intruded into the area claimed by Hevelius’s Lacerta.

It never possessed precise polygonal boundaries in the modern sense. Bode supplied a pictorial domain and catalogue membership. Consequently, assigning every modern NGC object or transient to “Honores Friderici” requires reconstructing the outline from his plate; it cannot be done merely by applying an official coordinate table.

Bode reported:

- **76 stars**, 27 of them newly plotted by him;
- **two small star clusters**;
- four stars of fourth magnitude;
- three of fifth;
- 24 of sixth;
- 16 of seventh;
- 24 of eighth;
- five of ninth magnitude.

He said the previously catalogued stars had belonged to:

- Pegasus: 5;
- Andromeda: 26;
- Cepheus: 6;
- Lacerta: 9;
- Cassiopeia: 3.

These numbers total 49, matching Bode’s statement that 49 were already on his charts; adding his 27 newly observed stars gives 76.

The prompt’s separate phrase “on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be traced in Bode’s description, catalogue, the atlas literature, or the modern constellation studies consulted. It should not be conflated with Bode’s documented count of 76 stars and two clusters.

## Stars and objects

### Principal stars

The conspicuous spine of the figure is normally reconstructed from ο, ι, κ, λ, and ψ Andromedae. Bode did not create their Bayer or Flamsteed designations; those belong to the older Andromeda framework.

| Star | Flamsteed | V magnitude | Position in Bode’s emblem | Current proper name and derivation |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| **ο Andromedae** | commonly identified near the beginning of the Andromeda Flamsteed sequence | about 3.62 | end or point of the sword; brightest principal star | **Alfarasalkamil**, approved by the IAU in 2025, from Arabic *al-faras al-kāmil*, “the complete horse,” a non-Ptolemaic Arabic figure distinguished from the half-horse Pegasus. It is a Be/γ-Cassiopeiae-type variable multiple system. |
| **λ Andromedae** | **16 And** | about 3.65–4.05 | head or hilt of the sword | **Udkadua**, approved in 2025, drawn from the reconstructed Babylonian/Sumerian demon figure U₈.KA.DU₈.A. It is an RS Canum Venaticorum-type spotted binary. |
| **κ Andromedae** | **19 And** | about 4.14 | ribbon near the sword hilt; one of Andromeda’s old hand stars | **Kaffalmusalsala**, approved in 2025, from Arabic *kaff al-musalsala*, “the hand/palm of the chained woman.” It has a directly imaged substellar companion whose mass classification has been disputed between giant planet and brown dwarf. |
| **ι Andromedae** | **17 And** | about 4.29 | beside κ, in the old hand | **Rasalnaqa**, approved in 2025, Arabic *raʾs al-nāqa*, “head of the she-camel.” The name originally concerned an asterism rather than this star alone. |
| **ψ Andromedae** | **20 And** | about 4.95 | within the sword head/hilt grouping | No widely established pre-2025 individual Western proper name; a multiple system and member of the Chinese Flying Serpent. |
| **7 Andromedae** | **7 And** | about 4.5 | in the feather/pen | **Honores**, approved by the IAU Working Group on Star Names on 14 May 2025 in memory of the extinct constellation. This is now an official stellar name, not a revival of the constellation. |

Other identifiable stars plotted in the principal ornament include **2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, and 18 Andromedae**, along with several sixth-magnitude HIP stars. A modern plate-by-plate reconstruction identifies, among others, HIP 113750, 113802, 114981, 115996, 112731, 113501, 113561, 114162, 114622, and 114924. These identifications are scholarly reconstruction, not names supplied by Bode in modern catalogue form.

The most useful reconstructed catalogue is the star-by-star table on the [All Skies Encyclopaedia page](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Honores_Friderici).

### The 2025 proper-name restoration

In May 2025 the IAU Working Group on Star Names assigned several names in this field:

- **Honores** — 7 Andromedae;
- **Alfarasalkamil** — ο Andromedae;
- **Rasalnaqa** — ι Andromedae;
- **Kaffalmusalsala** — κ Andromedae;
- **Udkadua** — λ Andromedae.

This unusually dense act of naming explains why older books say that the principal stars had no proper names, whereas current catalogues list several. Both statements can be correct relative to their dates. The official announcement records Honores as HR 8830/HIP 114570/7 Andromedae. [IAU announcement and table](https://www.iau.org/IAU/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx)

### Internal asterisms

**Frederick’s Glory / Frederick’s Honours.** Modern observing guides sometimes retain a Y-shaped grouping of ο, ι, κ, λ, and ψ Andromedae under this informal name. This is an asterism commemorating the abolished figure, not an IAU constellation.

**Andromeda’s hand.** In the Ptolemaic and Arabic constellation tradition, ι, κ, and λ Andromedae were associated with the northern hand or arm. Bode explicitly admitted moving the hand eastward to make room.

**Kaff al-Musalsala.** The Arabic “Hand of the Chained Woman,” normally attached collectively to ι, κ, and λ Andromedae.

**Raʾs al-Nāqa.** In indigenous Arabic or Bedouin uranography the same three-star group could instead be the “Head of the She-Camel.” The contrast is important: *Kaff al-Musalsala* translates a Greco-Arabic Andromeda body part, while *Raʾs al-Nāqa* belongs to an Arabic animal image.

**Téngshé.** The Chinese Flying Serpent crosses the entire region; details appear below.

### Variables and substellar systems

- **ο Andromedae / Alfarasalkamil:** a complex Be multiple and γ-Cassiopeiae-type variable. Its spectral emission and uncertain hierarchy have generated revisions to the number and arrangement of components.
- **λ Andromedae / Udkadua:** prototype-level object in the history of chromospherically active binaries, classified as an RS CVn variable; its visible brightness ranges roughly from magnitude 3.65 to 4.05.
- **κ Andromedae b:** directly imaged in 2012. Early estimates encouraged “super-Jupiter” language; later age and mass estimates reached well into the brown-dwarf regime. The boundary is model-dependent, making the popular statement “κ And has a planet” a disputed classification rather than a settled fact. [2013 mass reassessment](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.3372)
- **ι Andromedae:** some catalogues have entertained low-amplitude variability, but Hipparcos found it among the more nearly constant comparison stars. It should not be called a conspicuous historical variable.

### Deep-sky objects

No Messier, NGC, IC, or modern galaxy bears the official name “Honores Friderici.” Nor is there a securely documented “Honores Friderici Nebula” or cluster.

Objects falling within or very near plausible reconstructions of the Bode figure include:

- **NGC 7662**, the Blue Snowball Nebula, a planetary nebula in modern Andromeda;
- **NGC 7640**, a barred spiral galaxy in modern Andromeda;
- **NGC 7686**, a sparse open cluster in modern Andromeda, close to the crown/upper ornament in some reconstructions;
- faint galaxies and reflection nebulosity in the ο Andromedae region, including **van den Bergh 156**.

Bode’s “two small star clusters” have not been conclusively matched in the source material consulted. NGC 7686 is a plausible candidate for one, but presenting both as secure modern NGC identifications would exceed the evidence.

The Andromeda Galaxy, M31, and its famous nova/SN 1885A lie much farther along the classical Andromeda figure and were **not** part of Bode’s Honores emblem.

### Novae, supernovae, and meteor showers

No historical nova or supernova is securely catalogued under the extinct constellation’s name. Transients in the territory are now reported as Andromedae, Lacertae, Cassiopeiae, Cephei, or Pegasi.

No recognized meteor shower is named the **Honores Fridericids**, **Fredericids**, or equivalent. This distinguishes Honores Friderici from another obsolete figure, Quadrans Muralis, whose name survives in the Quadrantids.

Claims that the Andromedids or another modern shower “belong” to Honores Friderici confuse broad sky proximity with a historically recorded radiant.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 150 CE: no Honores Friderici

**Documented absence.** No Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Persian, Hebrew, Indian, or Chinese source contains Frederick’s crown, sword, pen, and branches. Any claim of ancient ancestry for that composite emblem is anachronistic.

The relevant stars nevertheless had earlier lives in other figures:

- λ Andromedae has been identified in modern Babylonian reconstruction with a heel of U₈.KA.DU₈.A;
- ι, κ, and λ Andromedae later formed part of Andromeda’s hand;
- the same region entered independent Arabic camel imagery;
- Chinese astronomy placed many of the stars in Téngshé.

### c. 150 CE — Ptolemy

In the *Almagest* star catalogue, the region belonged principally to **Andromeda**, especially her extended hand and arm, with adjacent unformed stars. Ptolemy did not draw boundary polygons; stars were assigned anatomically or listed as “unformed” near a figure.

Bode’s intervention was therefore not the occupation of an entirely blank sky. His own statement acknowledges that he changed Andromeda’s ancient anatomy.

### c. 964 — al-Ṣūfī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* revised Ptolemy’s catalogue and pictured Andromeda in both internal and external views. The Greco-Arabic figure was **al-Marʾa al-Musalsala**, “the Chained Woman.” Her hand was *kaff al-musalsala*. Al-Ṣūfī did not possess or anticipate the Frederick memorial.

The independent Arabic name *Raʾs al-Nāqa*, “Head of the She-Camel,” reflects a parallel desert-sky interpretation. It should not be represented as a translation made by al-Ṣūfī for the whole constellation.

### 1515 — Dürer’s northern celestial map

Albrecht Dürer’s woodcut map, prepared with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, depicts the classical northern constellations. The later Honores field remains within Andromeda and neighboring northern figures. No crown-sword-pen memorial appears.

### 1603 — Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* formalized Greek-letter nomenclature, including the stars now called ι, κ, λ, ο, and ψ Andromedae. Bayer’s plate preserves Andromeda and the chain/hand framework. Honores Friderici did not yet exist.

### 1627 — Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, assisted by Bayer and engraved by Lucas Kilian, replaced Andromeda with the **Sepulchrum Christi Triumphatoris**, the Sepulchre of Christ Triumphant. Thus the future Honores stars participated in the sepulchre or its surroundings. [Linda Hall Library on Schiller’s Andromeda/Sepulchre plate](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/13-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum-concavum/)

### 1679 — Augustin Royer

Royer introduced **Sceptrum Regium et Manus Iustitiae**, the Royal Sceptre and Hand of Justice, honoring Louis XIV, in substantially overlapping modern Andromeda–Lacerta territory. It included stars now identified with α and β Lacertae and stars 3, 7, and 8 Andromedae.

The catalogue coordinates used by Royer derived in part from the Carthusian astronomer Anthelme Voituret. Because Voituret’s independent work is not preserved, the precise division of responsibility between Royer and Voituret is unresolved.

**Disputed relationship:** Later writers often say Bode “laid” his memorial over Royer’s or transformed the French sceptre into a Prussian trophy. The geographic overlap is demonstrable. Direct documentary borrowing is not: Bode did not cite Royer in the 1787 explanation. The safest judgment is **possible visual or cartographic precedent, not proven source**.

### 1687/1690 — Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius created **Lacerta**, the Lizard, in the same crowded northern region; it appeared in the posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* and *Prodromus Astronomiae* of 1690.

The chronology matters: Royer’s 1679 sceptre preceded the publication of Hevelius’s Lacerta, although descriptions calling Royer’s figure a “replacement of Hevelius’s earlier Lacerta” reverse the dates. Bode later encountered Lacerta as the better-established figure and explicitly said he shifted it into a “more suitable position.”

### 1782 — Bode’s first small atlas

The first edition of Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln* does **not** contain Friedrichsehre. Statements that Bode put it in “the atlas of 1782” conflate the original publication date with later revised editions.

### 25 January 1787 — invention and Academy presentation

At a public memorial meeting of the Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin, Bode proposed the new constellation and displayed an engraved chart.

He wrote that the crown was placed near Cepheus because, “in Frederick’s century,” the stars there had stood vertically above the king’s earthly realm. Literally they culminated high over Prussian latitudes; the broader correspondence between a heavenly royal family and Prussian royalty is symbolic.

Bode claimed no personal interest beyond commemorating the dead king. The proposal should nevertheless be understood within the eighteenth-century practice of astronomers honoring patrons, dynasties, inventions, and political events in the sky.

### February 1787 and the yearbook for 1790

Bode’s *Friedrichs Sternendenkmal* appeared in the *Berlinische Monatsschrift* in February 1787 and in the *Astronomisches Jahrbuch für das Jahr 1790*, which was issued in advance.

This publication reports approving letters or messages from:

- the Imperial Academy at St Petersburg, conveyed through Euler;
- the Royal Society in London, through Daniel Peter Layard;
- the Royal Danish scientific society, through Thomas Bugge;
- Pierre Méchain in Paris;
- Jérôme Lalande;
- other unnamed astronomers.

The letters demonstrate polite institutional reception and willingness by some correspondents to use the name. They do not demonstrate that all European astronomers actually adopted it thereafter. Later biographical claims that it was accepted “by all astronomers” are ceremonial exaggeration.

### 1799 — Goldbach and Zach

Christian Friedrich Goldbach’s *Neuester Himmels-Atlas*, produced under the scientific direction of Franz Xaver von Zach at Seeberg Observatory, included **Friedrichsehre** before Bode’s great atlas appeared. This is the earliest securely traced independent atlas reception in the present research. [Osher Map Library record](https://oshermaps.org/map/4406.0024)

### 1801 — Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode placed the figure on **Plate IV**, with Perseus, Cassiopeia, Triangulum, Andromeda, and Lacerta. The great atlas gave the Latin label *Honores Friderici* or, on the engraving as read by recent investigators, *Honores Fridericis*. [Plate identification and reproduction](https://www.sunflower-astronomy.com/Books/Bode_Uranographia/Bode_Uranographia.html)

The accompanying *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* explains that prominent stars had previously belonged to Andromeda’s chain and northern hand. The *Uranographia* as a whole catalogued approximately 17,240 stars; that total should not be mistaken for the membership of Honores.

### 1804–1805 — globes and revised popular atlas

Bode emphasized the memorial on his 1804 celestial globe. A surviving Klinger globe at Gotha includes *Gloria Friderici II Regis Borussorum*, while another related specimen lacks it. Researchers have proposed either different production dates or later political removal, but the sequence remains unresolved.

Bode’s revised 1805 popular atlas added Friedrichsehre to the Andromeda plate. The Pegasus plate, however, retained the older hand arrangement and no memorial. The catalogue/header also associates Friedrichsehre with Triangulum in an erroneous position. These internal inconsistencies show how awkwardly the invention fitted the inherited atlas plates.

August Gottlieb Meissner’s *Astronomischer Hand-Atlas* also included it in 1805.

### 1807 — Thomas Young

Young’s *Course of Lectures on Natural Philosophy and the Mechanical Arts* reproduced it in hemispheric charts as **Frederic’s Trophy**.

### 1822 — Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* adopted Bode’s image but called it **Gloria Frederici**. Jamieson became the chief English-language visual intermediary for later popular copies. [Jamieson atlas facsimile index](https://meteo-maarssen.nl/lib_jamieson_1822.html)

### 1824/25 — Jacob Green and *Urania’s Mirror*

Jacob Green’s *Astronomical Recreations* described it as an artificial small asterism north of Andromeda’s hand. His treatment belongs to an emerging critical reaction against crowded dynastic inventions.

Card 5 of *Urania’s Mirror*, engraved by Sidney Hall and derived closely from Jamieson, depicts **Gloria Frederici, Andromeda, and Triangula**. The figure is a crown over crossed or pendant royal and intellectual emblems. [Library of Congress, card 5](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2002695398/)

### 1830s — Burritt

Elijah Hinsdale Burritt’s American atlas retained the figure as **Gloria Frederica**. This represents popular pedagogical survival, not professional consensus.

### 1840 — Olbers’s criticism

Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers objected that, to make room for Friedrichsehre, Andromeda had to put her right arm somewhere other than the place it had occupied for three thousand years:

> “Um der Friedrichsehre am Himmel Platz zu machen, musste die Andromeda ihren rechten Arm an eine andere Stelle legen, als dieser seit 3000 Jahren eingenommen hatte.”

This is the best-traced example of the “sarcasm of later atlas-makers,” although Olbers was an astronomer writing about newer constellations rather than merely a commercial cartographer. It targeted both Bode’s alteration of an ancient figure and the proliferation of flattering modern inventions.

### 1843 onward — scientific atlases omit it

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* of 1843 emphasized observed positions and magnitudes and omitted the decorative political figure. Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* of 1872 followed the increasingly standardized northern sky. These scientific atlases helped establish the practical constellation repertory inherited by the IAU.

By 1885–96, the German *Meyers* and *Brockhaus* encyclopedias already said Friedrichsehre was “gegenwärtig nicht mehr üblich” and that modern astronomers did not recognize it. Its effective death therefore preceded the IAU by decades.

### 1922–1930 — formal exclusion

The IAU’s 1922 list excluded it. Delporte’s final Andromeda, Lacerta, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, and Pegasus boundaries gave every point in its former territory another official home. There was no surviving figure for Delporte to delimit.

### 2025 — Honores restored as a star name

The IAU did not revive the constellation, but named **7 Andromedae “Honores.”** The choice preserves the historical term while omitting Frederick’s name. This is a modern commemorative decision, not continuity from Bode.

## Invention, political context, and rival royal constellations

Honores Friderici belongs to a wider early-modern fashion for placing dynastic patrons in the heavens.

### Sceptrum Brandenburgicum

Gottfried Kirch created the **Brandenburg Sceptre** in 1688 to commemorate the Brandenburg elector Frederick III, later Frederick I of Prussia. Bode preserved it in his atlases, but did not invent it. Later sources sometimes wrongly credit Bode because his *Uranographia* is its most familiar depiction.

### Robur Carolinum

Edmond Halley introduced **Charles’s Oak** in 1679, recalling the Royal Oak in which the future Charles II was said to have hidden after Worcester. It occupied part of Argo Navis and disappeared during the southern-sky reforms.

### Sceptrum et Manus Iustitiae

Royer’s 1679 sceptre and Hand of Justice honored Louis XIV and overlapped Honores geographically. The repeated use of sceptres and crowns shows a shared genre of royal iconography; it does not prove that Bode copied Royer’s exact design.

### Taurus Poniatovii

Marcin Odlanicki Poczobut introduced **Poniatowski’s Bull** in 1777 in honor of King Stanisław August Poniatowski of Poland. It occupied stars now in Ophiuchus and Aquila. A remnant survives informally as the Taurus Poniatovii asterism.

### Harpa or Psalterium Georgii

Maximilian Hell proposed **George’s Harp** in 1789 in honor of George III. It lay near Taurus and Eridanus. Its imagery referred to the harp in the British royal arms or to royal patronage, depending on the source.

### Gladii Electorales Saxonici

The **Crossed Swords of the Elector of Saxony** commemorated the Saxon electoral insignia. It belongs to the same dynastic-cartographic genre and was among the many minor figures reproduced by Bode rather than necessarily invented by him.

Honores’s failure was therefore not exceptional. Political figures generally lacked ancient authority, universally recognized outlines, bright defining stars, or enduring practical value. National rivalry made foreign monarchs particularly poor candidates for an international nomenclature.

## Myths and legends

### Classical mythology

There is **no Greek or Roman myth of Honores Friderici**. It appears in none of the following:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its epitome;
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus or the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus’s *Aratea* or its scholia;
- Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, or Pausanias.

No classical god placed Frederick’s sword or crown in the sky. Bode, acting as an eighteenth-century astronomer and court-connected Academy official, did so symbolically.

The classical myths present in the **same stars** are chiefly those of Andromeda:

- Cassiopeia boasted that she or Andromeda surpassed the Nereids;
- Poseidon sent a sea monster and, in many versions, a flood;
- an oracle required Andromeda’s exposure as a sacrifice;
- Perseus saw her chained to rock, obtained a promise of marriage, and killed the monster;
- Phineus or Agenor contested the marriage and was petrified by Medusa’s head;
- Athena/Minerva or the gods placed Andromeda among the stars.

Those stories explain the ancient Chained Woman, not Bode’s memorial. Exhaustively retelling all Andromeda variants here would turn the dossier into a history of another constellation and would falsely imply that Bode inherited their mythology. The relevant historical fact is that he physically displaced part of that mythic body.

### Frederickian “myth”

Bode’s triad—Frederick as **hero, sage, and peacemaker**—is documented commemorative rhetoric. It is not a neutral biography of Frederick’s reign. The sword invokes his wars, the pen his writings and cultivated self-image, and the olive branch a posthumous peace-making ideal. The laurel promises literary and military immortality; the crown asserts royal dignity.

Later patriotic biographies amplified this into a story that the entire astronomical world “willingly accepted” Frederick’s immortalization. Bode’s quoted correspondence documents genuine compliments, but the constellation’s rapid professional disappearance contradicts universal adoption.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

Modern historians and the 2025 IAU naming project associate λ Andromedae with one heel of **U₈.KA.DU₈.A**, read *Udkadua/Ukadua*, a gaping-mouthed storm or weather demon known from the *MUL.APIN* tradition. The full Babylonian figure extended through stars now divided among Andromeda, Cepheus, Cygnus, Lacerta, and Vulpecula.

This identification is a scholarly reconstruction from cuneiform star lists, not a continuously transmitted popular name for λ Andromedae. The IAU’s assignment of **Udkadua** to that single star is a modern standardization.

### Egyptian

No securely documented pharaonic Egyptian constellation corresponds to Bode’s field or emblem. Broad comparisons between Andromeda and figures on Egyptian zodiac ceilings do not establish an Egyptian antecedent for Honores Friderici.

### Greek and Latin

The stars formed Andromeda’s northern hand and arm or lay as adjacent unformed stars. Greek **Ἀνδρομέδα** means “ruler/protector of men”; Latin authors borrowed *Andromeda* and used descriptive phrases such as the chained or exposed maiden. None supplies a royal trophy resembling Bode’s.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

The translated classical constellation is **al-Marʾa al-Musalsala**, “the Chained Woman.” The phrase **kaff al-musalsala** denotes her hand. In later catalogues, variants such as *Keff al-Salsalat* and Latin *Manus Catenata(e)* appear.

The same stars could form **Raʾs al-Nāqa**, “Head of the She-Camel,” in indigenous Arabian star lore. Assigning Rasalnaqa to ι And and Kaffalmusalsala to κ And in 2025 distributes two historical group names among individual stars; it should not be mistaken for the original tradition having used exactly that one-name-per-star scheme.

Ο Andromedae’s **al-faras al-kāmil**, “Complete Horse,” belongs to a larger Arabic horse image opposed to the incomplete or half-formed Pegasus.

### Persian

Persian astronomical works usually transmitted the Ptolemaic-Arabic constellation and its Arabic names, with Andromeda rendered as the chained woman. No independent pre-Bode Persian crown-and-sword figure has been traced in these stars. Modern Persian forms are translations of Andromeda/Lacerta or transliterations of the IAU names.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

Medieval Hebrew astronomy translated Arabic and Latin astronomical material. Andromeda could be described circumlocutorily as a woman without a husband or as a chained woman. No securely sourced Hebrew constellation named for Frederick occurs before modern European atlases.

No classical Kabbalistic text was found that maps ι–ο Andromedae to Frederick’s crown, sword, pen, or olive branch. Online claims of an ancient Kabbalistic “Frederick’s Glory” should be classed as unverified or modern invention unless a manuscript and passage are produced.

### Chinese

Much of the field belonged to **螣蛇 Téngshé**, the Flying or Soaring Serpent, in the northern Black Tortoise region. Modern identifications include:

ψ And, α and β Lac, 4 and 9 Lac, π¹ and π² Cyg, ε Cep, σ, ρ, and τ Cas, AR Cas, 3, 7, 8, ι, κ, and λ And, and other stars.

The precise ordinal assigned to a component varies with catalogue reconstruction. Published modern lists give, for example:

- 7 And as Téngshé 17;
- λ And as Téngshé 19;
- κ And as Téngshé 21;
- ι And as Téngshé 22;
- ψ And as Téngshé 20 in one enumeration.

These numbers should be cited with the particular Chinese catalogue because historical lists and modern identifications are not entirely uniform.

### Indian astronomy

The Honores field lies well north of the ecliptic. No principal Indian *nakṣatra* is constituted by its defining Andromeda stars. Although precession and large mansion sectors can place them under the longitude of an ecliptic mansion, that does not make them mansion-marker stars. No independent Sanskrit Frederick-like emblem has been traced.

### Christian sky

Schiller’s 1627 **Sepulchrum Christi Triumphatoris** replaced Andromeda. The future Honores stars therefore lay in or near Christ’s triumphant sepulchre. Schiller’s transformation predates Bode by 160 years and is independent of him.

The claim sometimes repeated from Richard Hinckley Allen that the biblical expositor Willem Janszoon Blaeu or Philip van Zesen made Andromeda into Abigail belongs to a broader tradition of biblical reinterpretation; it is not an antecedent of Honores.

### Astrology, Behenian stars, and lunar mansions

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* assigned planetary qualities to the stars of Andromeda as a whole, conventionally comparing the constellation’s bright stars to Venus and its body parts to related planetary mixtures. It gives no influence for “Honores Friderici,” which did not exist.

None of the principal Honores stars is one of the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars.

Vivian Robson’s 1923 *Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology* speculated that the sword, pen, olive, laurel, and crown might confer qualities of war, literature, peace, fame, and dignity. This is a **modern astrological invention extrapolated from Bode’s iconography**, not an ancient doctrine.

The figure does not define a Babylonian, Arabic, Indian, or Chinese lunar mansion. Modern tropical-zodiac tables placing its stars at Aries degrees are astrological coordinate conversions, not evidence that the constellation occupies the zodiac.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No pre-1787 alchemical or Hermetic text naming Honores Friderici has been located. Crown, sword, olive, and laurel all possess broad emblematic meanings in European art, but reading Bode’s exact assemblage as an alchemical diagram lacks documentary support. It is best labelled an unverified modern interpretation.

## Iconography

### Before 1787

- **Farnese Atlas, Roman copy of a Hellenistic celestial globe, Naples:** contains the classical Andromeda region but nothing resembling Honores Friderici.
- **Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, from the medieval Islamic world:** depict Andromeda as the Chained Woman, generally in two orientations.
- **Leiden Aratea manuscript, ninth century:** Andromeda is exposed between rocks or supports, with stars marked on her body.
- **Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515:** classical northern celestial map; no Frederick memorial.
- **Bayer, Augsburg, 1603:** Andromeda plate with Bayer letters.
- **Schiller, Augsburg, 1627:** Andromeda’s stars transformed into the Sepulchre of Christ.
- **Royer, Paris, 1679:** sceptre and Hand of Justice for Louis XIV in overlapping territory.
- **Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690:** Lacerta and Andromeda divide the region.

These are iconographic predecessors of the **territory**, not depictions of Bode’s figure.

### 1787 memorial chart

Bode displayed an engraved chart at the Berlin Academy meeting. The version reproduced in the 1790 yearbook issue is the earliest attested image of Friedrichsehre.

### Goldbach, Weimar/Gotha, 1799

Goldbach drew Friedrichsehre in his school and academic atlas, under von Zach’s supervision. It demonstrates actual reception before Bode’s monumental atlas.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Plate IV of the *Uranographia* is the canonical representation. The crown stands near the Milky Way; the sword, pen, olive, laurel, and ribbons descend into northern Andromeda. The figure visually competes with Andromeda’s displaced arm and Hevelius’s Lacerta.

### Bode and Klinger globes, 1804 era

Bode highlighted the constellation on his own globe. One Gotha Klinger globe includes a Latinized Prussian royal inscription; a related specimen does not. Whether this is chronological revision or political deletion remains open.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s *Gloria Frederici* simplified Bode’s monumental engraving for a popular atlas and became the direct visual model for subsequent English versions.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/25

Card 5, engraved by Sidney Hall, presents *Gloria Frederici* above Andromeda. Pierced star holes allowed the card to be held to light. Its popular survival is ironic: the constellation remained visually familiar in parlour astronomy while professional astronomers were abandoning it.

### Burritt, United States, 1830s

Burritt’s atlas reproduced the emblem as *Gloria Frederica*. This is another derivative of the Bode–Jamieson line.

### Later omission

Argelander and Heis suppressed the ornate political image. By the late nineteenth century it survived mainly in historical, encyclopedic, and antiquarian references.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

No securely documented coin, cathedral portal, ancient calendar, or monumental ceiling depicts **Honores Friderici as a constellation**. Frederick’s crowns, swords, laurels, and olive branches occur abundantly in medals and Prussian commemorative art, but resemblance alone does not demonstrate derivation from Bode’s sky figure.

A memorial plaque or monument to Bode has reportedly reproduced a chart containing Friedrichsehre, but this is commemoration of Bode’s cartography rather than an independent iconographic tradition.

## People, events, and works

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode was called to Berlin under Frederick’s reign, became a member of the Prussian Academy, and directed the Berlin Observatory. The royal memorial therefore combined personal institutional gratitude, Academy ceremonial culture, and contemporary constellation-making.

His most durable contributions were not this constellation but his ephemerides, atlases, popular astronomy, promotion of the name Uranus, and association with the Titius–Bode rule.

### Frederick II

The constellation memorialized the dead king as soldier, author-philosopher, patron, and supposed peacemaker. The picture contains no portrait or body of Frederick; it is a trophy of attributes.

### European correspondents

Euler, Bugge, Layard, Méchain, and Lalande appear in Bode’s publication as approving intermediaries. Their letters are evidence for courteous reception, but their subsequent catalogues and the broader history do not support permanent international acceptance.

### Navigators and explorers

No expedition is documented as steering by “Honores Friderici.” Navigators could use its brighter stars as ordinary northern stars, but the figure was faint and short-lived, and it lacked a prominent navigational star comparable to Polaris or the Great Square.

### Literature and art

No major poem, opera, symphony, building, ship, or spacecraft was found to have been securely named for Honores Friderici. References concentrate in astronomical atlases, histories of cartography, Prussian commemorative writing, and modern lists of lost constellations.

The absence is significant: unlike Argo, Andromeda, or Orion, Honores did not develop an independent literary afterlife.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was it invented in 1782, 1787, 1790, 1798, or 1801?

**Resolution:**

- 1782: first edition of Bode’s smaller atlas—figure absent.
- 1787: conception, Academy presentation, memorial discourse, and first publication.
- 1790: date printed on the advance yearbook volume in which the account appeared.
- 1798: error in the Dutch *Oosthoek* encyclopedia.
- 1801: canonical Latin atlas publication.
- 1805: insertion into a revised edition of the smaller atlas.

Thus **1787 is the correct invention date**.

### 2. Did Bode call it *Honores Friderici* or *Frederici Honores*?

Bode’s accepted Latin form is **Honores Friderici**. Allen’s **Frederici Honores** is a later inversion. *Gloria Frederici* and *Gloria Frederica* are adaptations. None justifies choosing *Frederici Honores* as the uniquely original form.

### 3. Did it contain 34, 49, or 76 stars?

Bode’s detailed primary statement gives **76**: 49 already charted plus 27 newly observed. Allen’s statement that it was “made up from thirty-four stars” conflicts with Bode and may count only a subset or descend from an error. Without an explicit alternate catalogue definition, 76 has primary-source priority.

### 4. Was it built only from Andromeda and Lacerta?

No. Those are the principal modern homes, but Bode explicitly counted contributions from Pegasus, Andromeda, Cepheus, Lacerta, and Cassiopeia. Modern summaries saying “Andromeda and Lacerta” describe the main visible emblem, not the complete 76-star catalogue.

### 5. Did Bode copy Royer?

**Disputed.** The figures overlap and both are royal monuments. Royer’s sceptre used several of the same stars. But no statement by Bode acknowledging Royer as his source has been found. Cartographic influence is plausible; direct derivation remains unproved.

### 6. Was Hevelius’s Lacerta earlier than Royer’s sceptre?

Royer published in **1679**; Hevelius’s Lacerta dates to **1687/1690**. Sources calling Royer’s object an attempt to replace an already published Hevelian Lacerta reverse the chronology. By Bode’s time, however, Lacerta was the established rival, so the historical competition described by later writers is real.

### 7. Was Frederick alive when Bode made it?

No. Frederick died in 1786; Bode proposed the memorial in 1787. One modern article’s reference to open homage to a “still-living ruler” confuses Honores with the wider fashion for honoring living monarchs.

### 8. Did “all astronomers” adopt it?

No. Bode printed genuine approvals, and Goldbach, Meissner, Young, Jamieson, *Urania’s Mirror*, and Burritt adopted it. Olbers objected; Argelander and Heis omitted it; late nineteenth-century encyclopedias called it obsolete. “All astronomers” is patriotic or biographical hyperbole.

### 9. Did the IAU actively abolish it in 1922?

Not by a separate named prohibition. The IAU selected 88 constellations and their abbreviations, excluding Honores. Delporte then partitioned the entire sky among those 88. “Abolished” accurately describes the result, but **“not retained in 1922 and territorially superseded by the 1930 system”** is more precise.

### 10. Does it now have an IAU abbreviation?

No. **Hon** is retrospective. The 2025 star name **Honores** does not make *Hon* an official constellation abbreviation.

### 11. Did Bode move an arm “fixed for 3,000 years”?

Bode demonstrably altered the placement of Andromeda’s hand, and Olbers’s complaint is authentic in substance. “Three thousand years” is rhetorical: constellation anatomy and star identifications varied across manuscripts and atlases, although the hand tradition was ancient.

### 12. Is κ Andromedae b a planet?

Disputed. Its directly imaged status is documented, but inferred mass depends strongly on the age adopted for the host star. Published estimates range from the deuterium-burning boundary into unambiguous brown-dwarf territory. “Substellar companion” is the least controversial description.

### 13. Are the new proper names ancient individual names?

Only partly. The linguistic materials are old, but several originated as **asterism or body-part names**, not unique proper names for precisely the stars selected in 2025. The individual assignments are modern IAU decisions.

### 14. Misprints and unstable Latin

*Honores Fridericis*, *Honores Frederici*, *Frederici Honores*, *Gloria Frederici*, and *Gloria Frederica* have sometimes been treated as equivalent originals. They arose through engraving, grammatical normalization, translation, and copying. This is a textual history, not evidence for multiple inventions.

### 15. Hoaxes and popular errors

The dedicated searches found no important historical hoax centered on Honores Friderici. The recurring errors are more mundane:

- dating it to the 1782 first edition;
- saying Bode invented Sceptrum Brandenburgicum;
- calling its 1922 disappearance a specific IAU abolition resolution;
- assigning it official abbreviations and genitives;
- treating every nearby NGC object as historically “in” it;
- attributing classical mythology or ancient astrology to the eighteenth-century trophy;
- presenting recent IAU names as uninterrupted ancient individual-star usage.

## Open questions

1. **The two clusters:** Which modern objects correspond exactly to Bode’s *zwey Sternhäuflein*? A plate-and-catalogue positional analysis may establish this; the sources consulted do not do so conclusively.

2. **Royer’s influence:** Did Bode inspect Royer’s 1679 chart directly, encounter it through a derivative atlas, or independently choose a royal assemblage for the same crowded region?

3. **The engraved Latin form:** Surviving impressions should be collated to determine whether *Honores Fridericis* is consistent across copies and whether it represents an engraver’s error, Bode’s own usage, or a misreading of the lettering.

4. **The two Klinger globes:** Is the Gotha specimen with Friedrichsehre demonstrably later, or was the figure erased/omitted for political reasons?

5. **Complete 76-star concordance:** The conspicuous stars have been identified, but a peer-reviewed concordance from all Bode catalogue entries to Gaia identifiers would be valuable.

6. **Reception outside Europe:** English, German, French, and American atlas reception is documented. Evidence for sustained Spanish, Italian, Russian, Ottoman, Persian, or colonial navigational use remains thin.

7. **The “830 stars in 109 groups” phrase:** No connection to Honores Friderici could be established. Its originating catalogue or database needs identification before it can be evaluated.
