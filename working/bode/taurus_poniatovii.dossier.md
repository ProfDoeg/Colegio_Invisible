# Taurus Poniatovii: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **Abolished/obsolete.** *Taurus Poniatovii* was not included in the International Astronomical Union’s canonical constellation list adopted at Rome in 1922. It consequently has no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, area, or boundaries. Eugène Delporte’s boundaries, approved in 1928 and published in 1930, assigned its principal stars to **Ophiuchus**; some of the broader figure’s eastern or northeastern telescopic stars fall in **Aquila** and, depending on how Bode’s artwork is interpreted, near **Serpens Cauda**. The conspicuous core survives informally as the binocular asterism **Poniatowski’s Bull**. [IAU history and official list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), [1922 list transcription and documentary notes](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html), [Delporte, *Délimitation scientifique des constellations*](https://books.google.com/books?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

## Method and evidentiary labels

The following labels distinguish kinds of claims:

- **Documented fact:** supported directly by a contemporary publication, manuscript, chart, catalogue, letter, or institutional record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a later conclusion derived from comparison of historical sources or modern stellar identifications.
- **Tradition:** a historically repeated identification or name whose origin or exact evidential basis is incomplete.
- **Disputed claim:** incompatible sources or identifications exist.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in modern literature but not located in the relevant primary evidence.
- **Modern invention:** a recent nickname, astrological meaning, fictional elaboration, or retrospective system.
- **Myth/legend:** a narrative not offered as literal astronomical history.

Because the constellation was invented in 1777, it has **no ancient classical catasterism of its own**. Greek and Roman stories about the zodiacal Taurus or Ophiuchus must not be transferred to it merely because it was drawn as a bull inside territory formerly associated with Ophiuchus.

## Basic identification

### Canonical name and grammar

**Documented fact:** Bode’s 1801 atlas labels the figure **TAURUS PONIATOVII**, literally “the Bull of Poniatowski.” *Taurus* is Latin “bull”; *Poniatovii* is a Neo-Latin possessive form of the Polish surname *Poniatowski*. The normal full Latin genitive, if required grammatically, is **Tauri Poniatovii**.

The form *Poniatovii* reflects eighteenth-century Latinization rather than an ancient Latin personal name. Polish *Poniatowski* is adjectival, “of/from Poniatów,” ultimately a toponymic surname.

**Documented historical forms include:**

| Language or source | Form | Meaning/status |
|---|---|---|
| Poczobut’s Latin | *Poniatovii taurus*; *Vitulus Poniatovianus* | Poniatowski’s Bull; Poniatowskian Calf |
| Bode, Latin | *Taurus Poniatovii* | Poniatowski’s Bull |
| Bode/German tradition | *Königlicher Stier von Poniatowski*; *Poniatowsky’s Stier* | Royal Bull/Bull of Poniatowski |
| French atlases | *Taureau royal de Poniatowski* | Royal Bull of Poniatowski |
| Italian | *Toro di Poniatowski* | Bull of Poniatowski |
| Spanish | *Toro de Poniatowski* | Bull of Poniatowski |
| Polish | *Ciołek Poniatowskiego* | Poniatowski’s calf/young bull |
| Lithuanian | *Poniatovskio Tauras* or *Jautukas* | Poniatowski’s bull/little bull |
| English | Poniatowski’s Bull; occasionally Poniatowski’s Calf | Vernacular translations |
| Houzeau | *Taurus Regalis* | Royal Bull |
| Stieler planisphere | *Poln. Stier* | Polish Bull |

These forms are summarized by R. H. Allen, who gives *Taurus Regalis*, *Taureau Royal*, *Toro di Poniatowski*, *Poniatowsky’s Stier*, and *Poln Stier*. [Allen, *Star-Names and Their Meanings*, pp. 413–14](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Taurus_Poniatovii*.html)

### “Bull” or “calf”?

**Documented fact:** The Poniatowski family’s coat of arms is **Ciołek**, Polish for a calf or young bull. Poczobut’s 1785 title speaks of a *Vitulus Poniatovianus*, likewise a calf. More elevated French, German, Italian, and Latin atlas usage converted the heraldic calf into a royal “bull”: *taureau*, *Stier*, *toro*, *taurus*. A Polish historical discussion expressly notes this inflation from *Ciołek* to the more imposing *Taurus*. [Urania, “Gwiazdozbiór króla Stanisława Augusta”](https://www.urania.edu.pl/pliki/archiwum/urania_1984_09.pdf)

This is not an etymological error so much as a panegyrical change of register.

### IAU abbreviation and genitive

**Documented fact:** None. *Taurus Poniatovii* does not appear among the IAU constellations.

- **TaP** is a convenient **modern unofficial abbreviation**, used by some astrology and observing websites.
- **Tauri Poniatovii** is grammatically valid Latin and occurs in post-Bode discussion, but is not an IAU genitive.
- The surviving stars use **Oph/Ophiuchi**, **Aql/Aquilae**, or occasionally **Ser/Serpentis**, according to Delporte’s boundaries.

Claims that “TaP” was assigned by the IAU are false.

### Position

The figure lies in the summer Milky Way between the eastern shoulder of Ophiuchus, the tail of Serpens, and Aquila. Its recognizable centre is near approximately **RA 18h, Dec +2° to +4° (J2000)**, about five degrees east or east-southeast of β Ophiuchi, Cebalrai.

Poczobut chose the region because several naked-eye stars form a compact V reminiscent of the Hyades—the triangular face of the ancient zodiacal Bull. The resemblance provided the image; the Poniatowski heraldic calf supplied the dedication.

### Constituent stars: three different answers

The literature contains three nested definitions:

1. **Poczobut’s emblematic constellation:** seven principal stars, according to nineteenth-century summaries.
2. **Poczobut’s measured field:** sixteen stars in his 1785 paper, *Observationes ad determinandam positionem XVI stellarum, e quibus constellatio Viteli Poniatoviani formatur*.
3. **Bode’s illustrated territory:** approximately eighty stars, most telescopic, according to Allen’s reading of the 1801 atlas.

The modern asterism is narrower still, usually five stars. This difference explains many contradictory star lists.

### Core asterism

The most securely identified historical nucleus is:

| Modern star | V magnitude, approximately | Historical role |
|---|---:|---|
| **66 Ophiuchi** | 4.8, variable | Western side/eye of the V |
| **67 Ophiuchi** | 3.9 | Brightest core star; principal head star |
| **68 Ophiuchi** | 4.4 | Point or nose of the triangular head |
| **70 Ophiuchi** | 4.0 combined | Eastern side of the V; celebrated binary |
| **72 or 73 Ophiuchi** | 3.7 or 5.7 | Competing modern choices for horn or Hyades-like extension |

**Disputed identification:** Historical and modern reconstructions disagree over whether the fifth core star should be **72 Oph** or **73 Oph**.

- Ian Ridpath’s Ptolemaic reconstruction gives **66, 67, 68, 70 and 72 Oph**. [Ridpath, Ophiuchus](https://www.ianridpath.com/startales/ophiuchus.html)
- The Astronomical League’s current asterism list gives **66, 67, 68, 70 and 73 Oph**. [Astronomical League asterism list](https://www.astroleague.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Asterism_List_6.pdf)
- Modern observing descriptions commonly place **72 Oph at a horn tip** and **73 Oph in the face or Aldebaran-like position**, meaning both may belong to a fuller seven-star animal. [*Astronomy*, “Poniatowski’s Bull”](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/poniatowskis-bull/)

The disagreement comes from reducing an illustrated seven-star constellation or sixteen-star measured field to a five-star modern line figure. It is not proof that Poczobut confused the stars.

### Bode’s labels

Bode did not simply transfer Flamsteed’s numbers. His catalogue used lower-case Roman letters for many stars inside the new figure, producing designations such as *a, b, c, d, e, g, h, k, p, r, s¹,* and *x Tauri Poniatovii*. Several had previously appeared as lettered stars of Ophiuchus in Flamsteed-derived catalogues. These Bode designations are now obsolete and should not be confused with Bayer Greek letters. [Modern cross-index notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stars_in_Ophiuchus)

No securely demonstrated official **α Tauri Poniatovii** or **β Tauri Poniatovii** has been found in Bode’s atlas. Modern statements calling 67 Oph “Alpha Tauri Poniatovii” are retrospective conveniences.

## Stars and objects

### 67 Ophiuchi

**Documented astronomical identification:** HD 164353, HR 6714, HIP 88192; magnitude about 3.93; spectral type B5 Ib. It is the brightest star in the compact V if 72 Oph is treated as a horn rather than part of the face.

It is a luminous blue supergiant and a multiple visual system. Modern distance estimates have varied substantially because bright supergiants and multiple systems present astrometric difficulties.

**Controversy:** Some older descriptions call 67 Oph the unambiguous brightest star of the constellation; others give that distinction to magnitude-3.7 **72 Oph**. Both can be correct under different definitions—core head versus full horned figure. [67 Ophiuchi data and identifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/67_Ophiuchi)

### 66 Ophiuchi

66 Oph, HD 164284, is a **Be star** and variable **V2048 Ophiuchi**. “Be” means a B-type star showing hydrogen emission associated with circumstellar gas. Long-term spectroscopy documents formation and dissipation of its disk; variability is therefore physical, not simply an old catalogue discrepancy. [Marr et al., “The Be Star 66 Ophiuchi: 60 Years of Disk Evolution”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06948)

### 68 Ophiuchi

68 Oph is approximately magnitude 4.4 and spectral type A. It forms the compact triangular face with 67 and 70 Oph in many modern reconstructions. It has no IAU-approved individual proper name.

### 70 Ophiuchi

70 Oph is a nearby high-proper-motion visual binary, approximately 16.6 light-years away. Its main components are orange/yellow-orange dwarfs, conventionally 70 Oph A and B, orbiting in roughly eighty-eight years.

#### Binary discovery and study

William Herschel included 70 Oph among his double stars in the late eighteenth century. Because the components complete an appreciable fraction of an orbit within a human lifetime, it became important in early binary-orbit work.

#### William Stephen Jacob’s 1855 planet claim

**Documented fact:** In 1855 Captain William Stephen Jacob of the Madras Observatory published “On certain Anomalies presented by the Binary Star 70 Ophiuchi” in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, volume 15, pp. 228–31. He judged it “highly probable” that a planetary body was connected with the system. [Jacob paper, DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/15.9.228)

**Historical significance:** It is commonly described as the first published astrometric claim for an extrasolar planet around a stellar binary, although Bessel had already used positional perturbations to infer unseen stellar companions.

**Disputed/rejected claim:** Jacob’s planet was never confirmed. T. J. J. See revived a dark-companion interpretation in 1899; Forest Ray Moulton showed that See’s proposed configuration was dynamically unstable. Louis Berman proposed another dark companion in 1932. These are successive claims, not confirmations of the same planet.

It remains possible on modern dynamical grounds for lower-mass planets to occupy some stable orbits in the system, but that does not vindicate Jacob’s particular inference.

### 72 Ophiuchi and “Phorbaceus”

72 Oph is about magnitude 3.7 and often marks a horn of Bode’s bull.

**Tradition, not current official nomenclature:** It is sometimes called **Phorbaceus**, after Phorbas, the hero credited in one Rhodian tradition with destroying a plague of snakes. The name was attached through the surrounding Ophiuchus mythology, not through Poczobut’s royal bull. It is not currently an IAU-approved proper star name.

### 73 Ophiuchi

73 Oph is around magnitude 5.7. Modern observers sometimes assign it the Aldebaran-like position in the miniature Hyades pattern. It is a frequent member of modern five-star lists but not of every historical reconstruction.

### Collinder 359 / Melotte 186

The stellar field around 67, 68 and 70 Oph is catalogued as **Collinder 359 = Melotte 186**. Several accessible observing sources call it a sparse open cluster.

**Disputed physical status:** Later studies have questioned whether Collinder 359 is a gravitationally coherent open cluster; it may be a loose association or moving group, and some conspicuous foreground stars—including 70 Oph—are plainly not coeval members. Therefore:

- The visual association of stars is real.
- The catalogue object is real.
- The assertion that the whole V is one physical cluster is not.

[*Astronomy* observing account](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/poniatowskis-bull/)

### Other deep-sky objects

No Messier, NGC, IC, or planetary nebula officially bears the name *Taurus Poniatovii*. Bode’s approximately eighty-star territory overlapped a rich Milky Way field, but later object catalogues indexed its contents under Ophiuchus, Serpens, or Aquila.

Objects close to, but not necessarily inside every reconstruction of the bull, include:

- **NGC 6572**, a bright compact planetary nebula in Ophiuchus.
- **NGC 6633**, an open cluster eastward in Ophiuchus.
- **IC 4756**, a large open cluster in Serpens.
- **IC 4665**, farther north near β Ophiuchi.
- **NGC 6426** and **NGC 6535**, globular clusters in the broader region.

Descriptions saying NGC 6572 “belongs to Taurus Poniatovii” are retrospective and boundary-dependent. A modern account locates it roughly five degrees north-northeast of the V. [Kaler, NGC 6572](https://stars.astro.illinois.edu/sow/n6572.html)

### Barnard’s Star

Barnard’s Star, **V2500 Ophiuchi**, is nearby on the sky but is **not one of the naked-eye bull stars**. It is a tenth-magnitude red dwarf with the greatest known proper motion of any star. E. E. Barnard measured and announced that proper motion in 1916.

**Correct relationship:** It lies north or north-northwest of the V-shaped asterism, in northeastern Ophiuchus.

**Popular error:** Some websites call Barnard’s Star one of the five stars of the Bull. This is impossible as a historical naked-eye identification: it is far too faint and was not recognized as a distinctive object in 1777. [Kaler, Barnard’s Star](https://stars.astro.illinois.edu/sow/barnard.html)

Later claims by Peter van de Kamp that Barnard’s Star had planets are unrelated to Jacob’s 70 Oph claim and should not be merged with it.

### Novae and supernovae

No historically recorded naked-eye nova or supernova can be securely attributed to the small Poczobut figure. Modern variables and transients in that direction are catalogued under Ophiuchus, Serpens, or Aquila.

A comprehensive modern transient query would depend on choosing a particular reconstructed outline—a choice for which no canonical boundary exists. Accordingly, claims such as “nova X occurred in Taurus Poniatovii” are intrinsically retrospective.

### Meteor showers

No recognized meteor shower takes its name from Taurus Poniatovii. The **Taurids** radiate from the official zodiacal Taurus and have no relation to Poniatowski’s Bull. Ophiuchid radiants may geometrically cross the old field, but they are named for Ophiuchus.

### Deep-sky naming conclusion

The only astronomical object name preserving the constellation widely today is the **asterism itself**. *Poniatowski’s Bull* is not a Messier object, not a single open cluster, and not an IAU constellation.

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity: stars without the bull

#### Second century CE — Ptolemy

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The conspicuous stars later used for the bull appear among Ptolemy’s *amorphōtoi*—“unformed” stars—associated with Ophiuchus but outside the drawn human figure. “Unformed” did not mean unknown; it meant catalogued under a constellation without lying on the illustrated body.

The secure conclusion is that Ptolemy knew the stellar field. There was no Poniatowski bull, royal calf, or equivalent classical catasterism.

#### Tenth century — al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* followed Ptolemy’s 48-constellation framework, corrected positions and magnitudes, and compared Greco-Arabic catalogue descriptions with indigenous Arabic stellar lore. The future bull stars remained associated with the Ophiuchus region, not an independent bovine figure. [Library of Congress al-Ṣūfī manuscript](https://www.loc.gov/item/2008401028/), [overview of al-Ṣūfī’s method](https://www.ianridpath.com/startales/alsufi.html)

No substantiated Bedouin bull name for precisely 66–73 Oph was found.

### Medieval Islamic globe tradition

Allen reports that the medieval **Borgia globe** represented stars in this area by a triangular form. That observation may preserve a real iconographic feature, but it is not evidence for a pre-Poczobut bull. A triangle can be a positional schema without being a named constellation. Allen’s dating—“three or four centuries before” Poczobut—is too imprecise to establish an independent animal tradition. [Allen, pp. 413–14](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Taurus_Poniatovii*.html)

### 1624 — Bartsch’s Tigris

Petrus Bartsch introduced **Tigris**, a long celestial river beginning near β and γ Ophiuchi, passing through the future Poniatowski region, then running between Aquila, Hercules, Cygnus, Sagitta, Equuleus and Pegasus. It was one of several biblical rivers placed on early-modern charts.

**Documented overlap, not identity:** Some future Bull stars were placed in or beside Tigris. The bull did not directly “replace” Tigris as a one-for-one constellation: Tigris was vastly longer and had largely disappeared from practice before 1777. Royer still depicted it in 1679. [Allen’s Tigris summary](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Taurus_Poniatovii*.html)

### 1627 — Julius Schiller

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaced pagan constellations with biblical and Christian figures. Because Taurus Poniatovii did not yet exist, Schiller could not replace it with a particular saint.

The relevant stars remained inside Schiller’s Christianized equivalent of the broader **Ophiuchus** field. Statements that Schiller replaced “Poniatowski’s Bull” with St Andrew arise from confusing it with zodiacal **Taurus**, which Schiller did identify with **St Andrew the Apostle**. [Linda Hall Library, Schiller atlas](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/julius-schiller-2/)

### 1773–1777 — Poczobut’s observations and invention

Marcin Odlanicki Poczobut (Lithuanian Martynas Počobutas; 1728–1810), Jesuit-trained astronomer and director of the Royal Observatory at Vilnius, observed the relevant stars during his work at Vilna.

**Documented first publication:** The constellation appeared in his *Cahier des observations astronomiques faites à l’Observatoire royal de Vilno en 1773*, printed at Vilnius in **1777**. Modern scholarship on Poczobut’s correspondence identifies this notebook as the first publication, not the 1785 Latin article. [Triaire, “Lalande, Bernoulli, Poczobut…”](https://shs.cairn.info/article/E_RHS_651_0159?lang=en)

Poczobut’s selection joined:

- the V’s resemblance to the Hyades;
- the Poniatowski family’s Ciołek arms;
- gratitude for royal patronage of the Vilnius Observatory.

### 27 October 1777 — the king’s letter

A surviving letter from Stanisław August to Poczobut thanks him for the presented work, discusses the astronomer Strzecki’s proposed English journey to obtain instruments for an atlas, and praises Poczobut’s scientific merits. [Lithuanian Academy of Sciences digital record and manuscript](https://elibrary.mab.lt/items/46c9cf1f-c289-4cea-ba52-89430d87cdc6)

**Disputed interpretation:** A later review said the king declined the personal honour. Polish historical writing also says the panegyrical constellation was created somewhat against his preference. The surviving letter is courteous but, in its catalogue summary, does not itself constitute an explicit royal prohibition.

### 1777–1778 — promotion in Paris and London

Andrew Strzecki carried copies of Poczobut’s work to Warsaw, Paris, and London. Remigiusz Kossakowski promoted Poczobut among French academicians, circulated a memorial, cultivated Academy members, and helped obtain coverage in the *Journal des Sçavans*. [Jakubėnas, Kossakowski’s reports](https://zurnalai.vu.lt/sll/en/article/view/27310)

The *Journal des Sçavans* reviewed Poczobut’s work in 1778; the English *Critical Review* also noticed it.

**Correction to Allen:** Allen says formal permission was obtained from the French Academy. The surviving evidence more securely shows French scientific approval and active lobbying after Poczobut had already introduced the constellation. The Academy cannot therefore have been the original constituting authority. The “formal permission” formula remains unverified unless an Academy minute explicitly granting it is produced.

### 1778–1795 — Fortin, Lalande, and Méchain

Jean-Nicolas Fortin’s revisions of the Flamsteed atlas admitted the new figure as **Le Taureau Royal de Poniatowski**. Later editions revised by Lalande and Méchain continued it; a 1795 edition explicitly carries the French name.

This was important reception: the figure moved from a Vilnius publication into a widely used French Flamsteed-derived atlas. It did not appear in Flamsteed’s original seventeenth-century catalogue or atlas, since Flamsteed died decades before its invention.

### 1781–1782 — Bode’s first reception

Poczobut and Bode corresponded, and a 1781 letter requested observations of the Bull’s stars for Bode’s ephemerides. The *Berliner Astronomisches Jahrbuch* publicized the constellation as the royal bull.

Bode incorporated it into the **1782** edition of *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln*, establishing the form he later elaborated in *Uranographia*.

### 1785 — sixteen-star Latin paper

Poczobut published:

*Observationes ad determinandam positionem XVI stellarum, e quibus constellatio Viteli Poniatoviani formatur*  
“Observations for determining the positions of sixteen stars from which the constellation of the Poniatowskian Calf is formed.”

**Documented fact:** This appeared in the Berlin ephemerides in 1785. It is not the first attestation, but it is the most explicit title defining a sixteen-star measured constellation.

### 1790 — Klinger globe

A celestial globe by Johann Georg Klinger, dated about 1790 and preserved in Lublin, bears a partly surviving French label interpreted as something like *Taureau Royal de Poniatowski*. The globe documents the constellation’s circulation in commercial European globe production before Bode’s 1801 masterpiece. [Discussion of the Klinger globe](https://www.nestor-krasnystaw.eu/numery/nestor_25.pdf)

### 1795 — revised Fortin atlas

The third Fortin edition, revised by Lalande and Méchain, included the Bull. This is sometimes misdescribed as “Flamsteed accepting it.” More accurately, French editors inserted it into a posthumous, revised atlas descended from Flamsteed.

### 1801 — Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact:** Bode published *Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio* at Berlin in 1801. The large copperplate atlas contained twenty principal double-page charts, supplemented by textual and tabular volumes. Its catalogue listed 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. [Complete ETH Zürich facsimile](https://doi.org/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [Marseille bibliographic record and facsimile](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035)

Taurus Poniatovii appears on the chart of **Aquila, Antinous, Scutum Sobiesii, Ophiuchus and Serpens**, commonly numbered **Plate IX**. The animal is a complete naturalistic bull wedged awkwardly between the older figures. Its head contains the compact V; its horns and body extend among fainter stars.

Bode’s label is Latin, **TAURUS PONIATOVII**. A surviving detail is available here: [Bode’s 1801 bull](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bode_pontowski.jpg).

Allen’s statement that Bode “scraped together no fewer than eighty” stars is a colourful nineteenth-century evaluation, not Bode’s own wording. It reflects the difference between Poczobut’s seven principal stars and Bode’s densely populated telescopic atlas.

### Bode’s “830 stars in 109 groups” claim

**Unverified as phrased:** The supplied lead “on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be traced to Bode’s text or to a standard bibliographic description of *Uranographia*. Bode’s accompanying catalogue contains **17,240 entries**, and atlas constellation counts vary according to whether subdivisions and obsolete figures are counted. The numbers 830 and 109 may belong to a modern collection, exhibition, image chain, or database classification rather than the 1801 publication itself.

### 1822 — Alexander Jamieson

Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* retained the figure, with cleaner naked-eye mapping and freer pictorial treatment than Bode’s highly crowded engravings. Jamieson’s atlas became the immediate visual source for *Urania’s Mirror*. [David Rumsey collection, Jamieson atlas](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/view/all/who/Jamieson%2C%2BAlexander/what/Celestial%2BAtlas)

### 1824 — *Urania’s Mirror*

Card 12 of the anonymous boxed set *Urania’s Mirror* depicts **“Taurus Poniatowski, Serpentarius, Scutum Sobiesky, and Serpens.”** Its perforated stars could be held to a light. The design closely follows Jamieson rather than independently observing or redefining the constellation. [Card description](https://one.bid/en/prints-and-drawings/urania-s-mirror-poniatowski-s-calf-sobieski-s-shield-chart-from-urania-s-mirror-1824/2361609)

Some auction OCR has rendered the title as “Poniatowski’s Wormhole.” That is a modern OCR error, not a historical constellation name.

### 1830s–1860s — Burritt and popular astronomy

Elijah H. Burritt retained **Taurus Poniatowskii** in *The Geography of the Heavens*. He described it as a small asterism between Ophiuchus’s shoulder and Aquila, with fourth-magnitude head stars arranged like a V and resembling the Hyades. [Burritt facsimile](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/81/The_geography_of_the_heavens_-_and_class-book_of_astronomy_-_accompanied_by_a_celestial_atlas_%28IA_geographyofheav00burr%29.pdf)

Burritt’s text shows that the figure remained teachable in nineteenth-century Anglo-American popular astronomy even when professional astronomers increasingly treated it as optional.

### 1843 — Argelander

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* sought a practical naked-eye atlas and helped consolidate a more conservative constellation set. Taurus Poniatovii did not acquire the durable standing of neighbouring Ophiuchus, Serpens, Aquila, or Scutum.

### 1872 — Heis

Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus*, building on Argelander, helped stabilize the northern constellation repertoire later used by the IAU. The Royal Bull’s fading from serious catalogue practice preceded the IAU. The Union completed rather than initiated its decline.

### 1889 — Allen

R. H. Allen recorded that the Bull was already not generally recognized and that its stars had been returned to Ophiuchus. His account is invaluable for nineteenth-century names and reception but also spread several claims requiring caution: seven original stars, eighty in Bode, formal French Academy permission, “Tsung Ting or Tsung Jin,” and a Borgia-globe triangle. [Allen, pp. 413–14](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Taurus_Poniatovii*.html)

### 1895 — third partition centenary context

The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth was erased by the partitions of 1772, 1793, and 1795; Stanisław August abdicated in 1795. The constellation thus outlived the monarchy it honoured.

**Scholarly interpretation:** The partitions made the bull a memorial to a vanished state and dynasty. They did not astronomically cause its rejection: many patronal constellations failed regardless of political survival, while Hevelius’s **Scutum Sobiescianum** survived as Scutum because it was more widely adopted and occupied an otherwise useful region.

### 1922 — IAU canonical list

At its first General Assembly in Rome, the IAU Commission on Notations adopted a list of the 88 principal constellations and their three-letter abbreviations. Taurus Poniatovii was absent. [IAU account](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

**Precise status:** The IAU did not hold a trial of Taurus Poniatovii or resolve “abolish the Polish Bull.” It standardized a positive list. Omission made the figure noncanonical.

### 1928–1930 — Delporte

The IAU approved Delporte’s boundaries at Leiden in 1928. His 1930 publication divided the entire celestial sphere among the accepted constellations along right-ascension and declination lines for equinox B1875.0.

The core V fell in **Ophiuchus**. Thus 66, 67, 68, 70, 72, and 73 are now Ophiuchi. Stars at the extended eastern edge of older drawings may lie in Aquila or near Serpens, but the bull has no residual territorial claim.

### Present

The figure survives in:

- historical-atlas studies;
- Polish and Lithuanian cultural history;
- binocular observing lists;
- obsolete-constellation observing programs;
- planet-history discussions through 70 Oph;
- modern artistic projects about abolished constellations.

It does not survive as a scientific sky division.

## Invention and reception

### Inventor

**Documented fact:** Marcin Odlanicki Poczobut, not Bode, invented the constellation. Bode popularized and Latinized its mature atlas form.

“Poczobutt,” “Poczobut,” “Počobutas,” and “Poczobut-Odlanicki” are variants of the astronomer’s name, not different claimants.

### Patron and political meaning

The honoree was **Stanisław August Poniatowski** (1732–1798), elected king of Poland and grand duke of Lithuania from 1764 to 1795. He supported scientific, artistic, architectural, and educational projects, including Poczobut and the Vilnius Observatory.

The bull represented the Poniatowski **Ciołek** coat of arms. This is more specific than the generic statement “it honoured the king”: the image was his family’s heraldic animal translated into a Hyades-like stellar pattern.

### Relation to Scutum Sobiescianum

Hevelius had introduced **Scutum Sobiescianum** in honour of King John III Sobieski. Poczobut’s Bull consequently joined a conspicuous pair of Polish royal constellations in the same broad summer sky.

The comparison was politically and iconographically deliberate: both used royal arms. Their outcomes differed:

- **Scutum Sobiescianum** was shortened to **Scutum** and retained by the IAU.
- **Taurus Poniatovii** disappeared into Ophiuchus.

There is no evidence that the IAU rejected one king while accepting another. Scutum had more than a century of broader cartographic entrenchment and a more convenient position.

### Why it failed

**Scholarly reconstruction:**

1. It occupied territory already comfortably catalogued under Ophiuchus.
2. Its body required many faint stars; only the small V was memorable.
3. It was a patronal compliment tied to a deposed monarch and vanished commonwealth.
4. Atlas makers never adopted it uniformly.
5. Nineteenth-century practical atlases were reducing decorative overlays.
6. Variable-star and catalogue nomenclature made competing constellation assignments inconvenient.
7. The IAU ultimately favoured an established list derived substantially from Argelander/Heis-era practice.

The partitions are therefore historical context, not a sufficient causal explanation.

## Myths and legends

### The Royal Bull’s own “myth”

**Modern invention/tradition:** Its narrative is heraldic, not mythological: an astronomer sees a miniature Hyades, recognizes the king’s calf emblem, and raises that emblem to the sky in gratitude for patronage.

Poczobut’s act resembles a catasterism structurally—earthly patron and emblem become celestial—but it was conducted through publication, coordinates, and atlas-making rather than through a divine transformation story.

### No Greek or Roman catasterism

There is no entry for Taurus Poniatovii in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus or the scholia;
- Germanicus;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias.

Those authors predate Poczobut by many centuries.

### Zodiacal Taurus myths are not its myths

The Europa bull, the Cretan Bull, Io, and Zeus’s taurine transformations belong to **zodiacal Taurus**, not Poniatowski’s Bull. Bode’s visual echo of the Hyades does not transfer these catasterisms.

### Ophiuchus and Phorbas

The underlying star field belonged in classical uranography to Ophiuchus, normally identified with Asclepius. Alternative traditions connected the snake-holder with Phorbas, Heracles, Carnabon, or Triopas in later mythographic discussions. These concern the surrounding ancient constellation—not the eighteenth-century bull.

The occasional name **Phorbaceus** for 72 Oph has encouraged a misleading impression that the Royal Bull possessed an ancient hero-myth. It did not.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

No securely identified Babylonian asterism equivalent specifically to the compact 66–73 Oph V was found. The region fell within the much broader serpent, god, and Milky Way structures of Mesopotamian uranography. Any statement that “the Babylonians knew Poniatowski’s Bull” is anachronistic.

### Egyptian

No Egyptian constellation corresponding specifically to these stars has been demonstrated. General comparisons between bulls in Egyptian religion and the zodiacal Taurus are irrelevant to Poczobut’s field.

### Greek

The stars were catalogued as unformed or peripheral stars of **Ὀφιοῦχος**, Ophiouchos, “Serpent-holder.” They were not a separate *Tauros*.

### Latin

Latin astronomical authors used **Ophiuchus**, **Serpentarius**, and variant transliterations for the larger constellation. *Taurus Poniatovii* is Neo-Latin.

### Arabic and Persian

Al-Ṣūfī, a Persian astronomer writing in Arabic, retained the Ptolemaic Ophiuchus framework. The relevant stars therefore carried positional descriptions rather than an independent bull name.

No well-supported indigenous Bedouin bovine asterism exactly matching the Royal Bull was located. Allen’s statement that the stars had no individual names remains broadly correct, although catalogued positional phrases should not be mistaken for “namelessness.”

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No pre-1777 Hebrew or Kabbalistic mapping of these exact stars as a Poniatowski-like bull was found. Hebrew zodiacal **Shor** (“bull”) concerns Taurus. Later Christian-Hebraic and occult tables that map zodiacal signs, tribes, letters, or paths do not establish a tradition for this non-zodiacal V.

### Chinese: Zōngrén

The strongest non-Western equivalent is Chinese **宗人, Zōngrén**, an asterism in the **Tianshi Yuan**, the Heavenly Market Enclosure.

Modern identifications give:

- 66 Oph — 宗人一, Zōngrén 1
- 67 Oph — 宗人二, Zōngrén 2
- 68 Oph — 宗人三, Zōngrén 3
- 70 Oph — 宗人四, Zōngrén 4

Additional stars 72, 71, 73, and 74 Oph were later designated “added stars” in expanded charts. [Chinese Text Project summary](https://ctext.org/datawiki.pl?if=gb&res=791495)

**Translation dispute:** Zōngrén can mean members or relatives of the imperial clan, or an official responsible for clan and ancestral rites. Allen’s “Tsung Ting, or Tsung Jin, a Relative” is an obsolete and confused romanization/translation. **Zōngrén**, not “Tsung Ting,” is the securely reconstructed name for the four-star group.

This is an independent Chinese bureaucratic asterism, not an antecedent of Poczobut’s invention.

### Indian

The field is not the defining stellar group of a standard nakṣatra. Because nakṣatras are longitudinal lunar sectors, the stars can fall geometrically within a mansion sector depending on epoch and boundary convention; that does not make the V a named Indian bull.

### Arabic lunar mansions

Similarly, a longitude-based Arabic *manzil* can cross the region, but no classical Arabic lunar mansion is constituted by 66–73 Oph as a group. Assignments change between sidereal and tropical reconstructions. No direct Taurus Poniatovii mansion tradition was found.

### Alchemical and Hermetic interpretations

No premodern alchemical or Hermetic interpretation of Taurus Poniatovii is possible before 1777. Later occult writers sometimes assign it generic “bull” qualities, but these are modern extrapolations.

### Astrology

Vivian E. Robson’s 1923 *Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology* assigned the obsolete constellation qualities including obstinacy, changeability, emotion, honour, and renown.

**Modern invention/tradition:** This is an early-twentieth-century astrological attribution, not something found in Poczobut or Bode and not inherited from Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*. Ptolemy gave planetary “temperaments” to stars of the ancient constellations; he could not discuss Taurus Poniatovii.

No star of the group is a standard Behenian fixed star.

## Iconography

### Ancient monuments and manuscripts

- **Farnese Atlas:** no Poniatowski bull; the sculpture predates the invention by roughly sixteen centuries.
- **Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts:** no independent bull; the stars belong to the Ophiuchus charts.
- **Dürer’s 1515 maps:** no bull; the field remains part of the inherited Ptolemaic sky.
- **Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria*:** no bull.
- **Hevelius’s 1690 atlas:** no bull, although it contains Scutum Sobiescianum elsewhere.
- **Schiller 1627:** no bull; zodiacal Taurus is St Andrew, a separate region.

Claims placing Taurus Poniatovii on any of these are anachronisms.

### Poczobut and Vilnius

A representation of the Bull remains associated with the Vilnius University Astronomical Observatory. Its exact date and restoration history require architectural documentation before it can be treated as an unchanged 1777 image.

### Fortin’s revised atlas

The French **Taureau Royal de Poniatowski** introduced the image to the Flamsteed–Fortin atlas tradition in the late eighteenth century.

### Klinger globe, circa 1790

The Klinger globe documents the figure on a commercial Central European celestial globe before 1801.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s copperplate is the definitive monumental image: a full bull placed across a dense telescopic field, its V-shaped head turned into a miniature echo of Taurus. The juxtaposition with Scutum Sobiesii reinforced the Polish royal imagery. [Bode detail](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bode_pontowski.jpg), [complete atlas](https://doi.org/10.3931/e-rara-11148)

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson retained and simplified the bull for a less crowded naked-eye atlas.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824

The perforated card presents the bull with Serpentarius, Serpens, and Sobieski’s Shield. It is the best-known popular image after Bode and is responsible for much modern decorative reproduction.

### Burritt, United States, nineteenth century

Burritt’s maps and textbook transferred Jamieson-style constellation imagery into American education.

### Modern art

Alexandra Paperno included *Taurus Poniatovii* in her 2018 series **Abolished Constellations**, converting discarded scientific figures into a meditation on classification and historical erasure. [Paperno, *Abolished Constellations*](https://alexandrapaperno.com/abolished-constellations-paper/)

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

No securely verified historic coin, cathedral portal, or medieval calendar depicts Taurus Poniatovii. Images of ordinary Taurus, Apis, the evangelist’s ox, or zodiacal bulls should not be relabelled as it.

The Vilnius Observatory decoration is the principal architectural representation located. Further claims about royal palace ceilings require object-level provenance.

## People, events, and works

### Marcin Poczobut

Poczobut was an observer, educator, former Jesuit, royal astronomer, and director of the Vilnius Observatory. His Bull was simultaneously:

- a measured stellar group;
- an emblem of patronage;
- a bid for international scientific visibility;
- part of the Vilnius Observatory’s institutional identity.

### Stanisław August Poniatowski

The last king’s support made the dedication intelligible, while his abdication and death turned the figure into an unintended memorial.

### Andrew Strzecki

Strzecki’s 1777–78 scientific journey promoted the Vilnius work in Warsaw, Paris, and London and sought astronomical instruments. Modern research shows that promoting the constellation was one objective of the journey. [Study of Strzecki’s journey](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/372088706_The_Scientific_Wanderjahr_of_Vilnius_Astronomer_Andrew_Strzecki_in_1777-1778)

### Remigiusz Kossakowski

Kossakowski acted as scientific diplomat and publicist in Paris, assisting both the constellation’s publicity and Poczobut’s election as a correspondent of the French Academy.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode did not invent the constellation. His role was decisive reception: correspondence, Berlin ephemerides, the 1782 *Vorstellung*, and the 1801 *Uranographia*.

### Lalande, Méchain, and Fortin

Their revised French atlas tradition carried the *Taureau Royal* into European practical astronomy.

### William Herschel and binary astronomy

Herschel’s observations of 70 Oph helped establish it as a physical binary. This scientific afterlife vastly exceeded the formal life of the constellation.

### William Stephen Jacob

Jacob’s 1855 interpretation made 70 Oph central to the prehistory of exoplanet detection. The claim was erroneous, but historically consequential.

### E. E. Barnard

Barnard’s Star lies near the old bull and is commonly shown on modern finder charts with it. Barnard did not discover or observe it as part of Taurus Poniatovii.

### Navigation

No major explorer or voyage was found that demonstrably steered by “Taurus Poniatovii.” Its stars are modest, its lifetime was late, and navigators used brighter standard stars such as Altair. Statements that Polish explorers navigated by the Royal Bull remain unverified.

### Literature, music, buildings, ships, and missions

No historically significant ship, spacecraft mission, major musical composition, or canonical poem named specifically for Taurus Poniatovii was located.

The figure does appear in modern Polish historical essays, observing columns, atlas reproductions, visual art, and constellation catalogues. Its cultural presence is therefore chiefly antiquarian and national-historical rather than literary or navigational.

## Boundary history

### Ptolemy

The stars were assigned to the catalogue environment of Ophiuchus as unformed stars. There were no geometric constellation borders.

### Bayer

Bayer illustrated Ophiuchus and neighbouring constellations but did not define a Bull or statutory borders. The future V remained peripheral material.

### Hevelius

Hevelius added Scutum Sobiescianum nearby but not Taurus Poniatovii. His pictures overlapped or interlocked in the normal pre-boundary manner.

### Poczobut

Poczobut selected a measured stellar group inside the older Ophiuchus/Aquila interspace. He did not remove stars through a universally binding boundary system.

### Fortin and Bode

They reassigned the pictorial ownership of numerous stars to the Royal Bull. Bode’s dense rendering expanded the animal far beyond the modern V.

### Argelander and Heis

Their more conservative nineteenth-century atlases placed practical stellar identification ahead of multiplying patronal figures. The stars reverted in ordinary usage to Ophiuchus.

### Delporte

Delporte’s borders transformed constellations from overlapping pictures and star lists into exhaustive sky areas. The core went to Ophiuchus. The bull “lost” every star because no territory remained under its name.

No evidence was found that it transferred a principal core star to Aquila; statements that its stars “are now in Ophiuchus and Aquila” refer to the expanded Bode figure, not the compact V.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was it invented in 1773, 1777, or 1785?

- **1773:** year of the observations or notebook title.
- **1777:** year of publication and accepted introduction.
- **1785:** publication of the sixteen-star Latin determination.

Calling 1785 the invention date is incorrect. Calling 1773 the publication date confuses observation epoch with printing.

### 2. Poczobut or Bode?

Poczobut invented it; Bode adopted and elaborated it. “Bode’s constellation” is acceptable only in the limited sense “as depicted by Bode.”

### 3. Seven, sixteen, eighty, or five stars?

All numbers refer to different scopes:

- seven emblematic stars;
- sixteen measured by Poczobut;
- about eighty plotted by Bode within the animal;
- five in the surviving modern asterism.

A source should specify which it means.

### 4. 72 or 73 Oph?

Both enter plausible reconstructions. 72 Oph is the brighter horn star and appears in Ptolemaic/historical lists; 73 Oph is frequently chosen in modern five-star asterism lists. The dispute is created largely by modern reduction of a larger figure.

### 5. Which star was “alpha”?

67 Oph is often called the would-be alpha because it dominates the head. This is **modern counterfactual nomenclature**. Bode’s lower-case Roman letters are not equivalent to Bayer Greek letters.

### 6. Was French Academy permission required or granted?

Allen says formal permission had been obtained. The better-documented sequence shows Poczobut introduced the figure at Vilnius, then supporters sought French endorsement and publicity. No primary Academy resolution granting constitutive permission was located.

### 7. Did the king oppose it?

A contemporary or near-contemporary English review reportedly said the king refused the honour; Polish historical writing says he criticized its panegyrical character. Yet the surviving October 1777 royal letter thanks and praises Poczobut. The evidence supports royal reserve more readily than a public repudiation.

### 8. Did the partitions kill it?

**Disputed causal claim:** They deprived the dedication of a living monarchy and contributed to later symbolic obscurity. But astronomically the decisive forces were inconsistent adoption, overlap with Ophiuchus, and nineteenth-century standardization. Scutum’s survival disproves a simple anti-Polish explanation.

### 9. Did the IAU “abolish it in 1922”?

This is reasonable shorthand but procedurally imprecise. The IAU adopted a list that omitted it. Delporte then allocated every sky position to accepted constellations. No individual anti-Taurus resolution was found.

### 10. Is it an open cluster?

The V is an **asterism**. Some stars overlap Collinder 359/Melotte 186, whose physical coherence is itself questioned. 70 Oph is a nearby foreground binary. Calling the complete Bull a star cluster is misleading.

### 11. Is Barnard’s Star a member?

No. It is nearby in angular position, much too faint for the historical figure, and not one of Poczobut’s naked-eye defining stars.

### 12. Is Phorbaceus an ancient name belonging to the Bull?

No secure evidence supports that formulation. It is a later traditional name for 72 Oph derived from Ophiuchus-associated Phorbas lore.

### 13. Did it replace the Tigris?

Only partially and pictorially. Bartsch’s Tigris crossed the region but extended vastly farther. The Bull reused some stellar territory after Tigris had declined.

### 14. “Tsung Ting or Tsung Jin”

Allen’s wording is unreliable by modern sinological standards. The relevant Chinese asterism is **Zōngrén 宗人**. “Tsung Ting” probably conflates it with another celestial office.

### 15. 70 Oph’s planet

Jacob made a genuine published claim, but there was no confirmed planet. Later See and Berman claims were separate and unsuccessful. Popular histories sometimes present them as accumulating evidence; historically they were incompatible orbit solutions.

### 16. *Taurus Poniatouii*

The spelling *Poniatouii* seen in some English books is a typographical substitution of **u** for **v** in *Poniatovii*, not a distinct Latin form.

### 17. *Taurus Poniatowskii*

This hybrid Latin/Polish spelling occurs in nineteenth-century English works. It is historical but not Bode’s label.

### 18. “Poniatowski’s Wormhole”

This is auction or OCR corruption of a card title, not a genuine historical synonym.

### 19. Astrology and alien lore

Astrological personality traits assigned to the Bull are twentieth- or twenty-first-century constructions. Claims linking Barnard’s Star, the Bull, extraterrestrial races, or contactee narratives are modern fringe mythology without astronomical evidence.

### 20. “Bode collection on chain: 830 stars in 109 groups”

No source consulted connected these numbers securely with Bode’s Taurus Poniatovii, *Uranographia*, or its companion catalogue. It should remain an unverified lead.

## Open questions

1. Where is the most accessible complete copy of Poczobut’s 1777 *Cahier*, and does its plate or prose explicitly number the seven primary stars?
2. Can the sixteen entries in the 1785 Berlin ephemerides be cross-identified star by star with modern HIP/HD numbers?
3. Does a French Academy minute document approval, or did later writers inflate favourable reception into “formal permission”?
4. What is the full wording of the 1778 *Critical Review* notice concerning the king’s alleged refusal?
5. Which edition of Fortin first inserted the *Taureau Royal*: a separately issued revised state, the 1776 atlas as altered, or a later edition?
6. What are the exact dates of the Vilnius Observatory wall image and its restorations?
7. Can Allen’s “Tsung Ting” be traced to a particular nineteenth-century Chinese star catalogue?
8. Which precise Borgia globe stars did Allen mean, and is the alleged triangle a constellation figure, geometric connector, or decoration?
9. Does Bode’s companion catalogue define the Bull by an explicit closed star list, or must its approximately eighty stars be reconstructed from typography and plate artwork?
10. What source lies behind the numbers “830 stars in 109 groups”?
