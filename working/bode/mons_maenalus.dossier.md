# Mons Maenalus: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Status — documented fact:** **Mons Maenalus was not retained by the International Astronomical Union.** It has no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, boundary, or area. At the IAU’s first General Assembly in Rome in May 1922, the Commission on Notations and Units adopted the list of “88 principal constellations”; Mons Maenalus was absent. It is therefore better to say that it was **excluded from the official list in 1922**, rather than that the IAU passed a resolution specifically “abolishing” it. Eugène Delporte’s boundaries, proposed in 1925, approved at Leiden in 1928, and published in 1930, distributed its former territory chiefly between **Boötes** and **Virgo**. [IAU history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [original-list discussion](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html); [Delporte bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ).

**Modern disposition — documented fact:** The compact version drawn by Johann Elert Bode lies in southern **Boötes**; the more extensive Hevelian mountain included stars now in **Boötes and Virgo**, and Hevelius’s surrounding “unformed” stars extended toward **Serpens Caput**. The stars most consistently associated with the surviving asterism are 14, 15, 18, and 31 Boötis and 71 Virginis. Reconstructions of Hevelius’s broader figure also include stars near modern HIP 68498 and HIP 70327 and identify 109 Virginis as its brightest associated star.

**Important 2026 development — documented fact, but not a restoration:** On 15 April 2026 the IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Maenalus** as the proper name of **109 Virginis**. That decision commemorates the obsolete figure; it does **not** revive Mons Maenalus as a constellation. Because some derivative pages still say “approved in 2025,” the date should be taken from the current IAU catalogue rather than older cached summaries. [IAU star-name catalogue](https://www.iau.org/public/themes/naming_stars/); [109 Virginis record and references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/109_Virginis).

### Etymology

**Documented linguistic fact:** Latin *Mons Maenalus* means “Mount Maenalus.” Latin *Maenalus* transcribes Greek Μαίναλος/Μαίναλον.

**Ancient or medieval etymological tradition:** A scholastic gloss cited in later Virgilian commentary derives *Maenalus* “ἀπὸ τῶν μήλων,” from Greek *mēla*, sheep or flocks, because the mountain abounded in them. This is an ancient-style explanatory etymology, not accepted proof of the word’s historical formation.

**Scholarly caution:** No secure Indo-European derivation was established in the consulted historical dictionaries. The mountain-name may be pre-Greek. Similarity to Greek *mainomai*, “to rage/be mad,” sometimes invites popular association with wildness or maenads, but no reliable source was found establishing that derivation.

### No Babylonian or Egyptian name

**Negative finding:** Mons Maenalus is a seventeenth-century addition, so it has no Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek astronomical, Arabic, Persian, or Hebrew constellation-name antecedent. Ancient cultures certainly catalogued or interpreted stars in the same area, but no evidence traces an ancient “mountain” figure there. Claims that attach Babylonian, Egyptian, or prehistoric Hindu mountain symbolism directly to Mons Maenalus are retrospective analogies.

## Stars and objects

### What stars made the mountain?

There is no single timeless membership list. Before Delporte, constellations were figures or catalogue headings, not mutually exclusive bounded sky areas. A star could lie in the drawing of the mountain while being catalogued under Boötes, Virgo, Serpens, or among *informes*—stars outside the main figure.

#### The compact Bode asterism

The most defensible reconstruction of Bode’s compact mountain is:

| Modern identification | Historical role | V magnitude, approximately | Notes |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **31 Boötis** | Bode’s brightest mountain star; designated **z** by Bode | 4.86 | G-type evolved star; no old Arabic proper name was found. |
| **15 Boötis** | Part of the slope/outline | 5.29 | K-type star. |
| **18 Boötis** | Part of the slope/outline | 5.41 | F-type star. |
| **14 Boötis** | Part of the slope/outline | 5.53 | F-type star. |
| **71 Virginis** | Included in broader modern reconstructions | 5.65 | K-type star; lies in Virgo under IAU boundaries. |

A Royal Astronomical Society of Canada historical-asterism reconstruction gives a bent oval of **14, 18, and 31 Boötis, 71 Virginis, HIP 68498, and HIP 70327A**. It notably omits 15 Boötis, illustrating the uncertainty inherent in reading a pictorial hill as a modern line-asterism. [RASC World Asterisms Project](https://www.rasc.ca/sites/default/files/World%20Asterisms%20Project%20Handbook%20V%202024.4.pdf).

#### Hevelius’s broader mountain

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Hevelius drew a larger landform than Bode. The stars were drawn on or around the hill but not necessarily treated as an independent catalogue class. Modern cross-identifications place the mountain across southern Boötes and northern Virgo, with surrounding stars extending toward Serpens Caput. In this broad reading **109 Virginis**, magnitude about 3.72, is the brightest star.

This explains the apparently contradictory statements that:

- 109 Virginis was “the brightest star of Mons Maenalus”; and
- 31 Boötis was “the brightest star of Mons Maenalus.”

The former describes the broad Hevelian reconstruction; the latter describes Bode’s reduced southern-Boötes figure.

### Maenalus, 109 Virginis

**Documented modern fact:** 109 Virginis is now officially named **Maenalus**. It is a naked-eye A-type star, approximately magnitude 3.72 and roughly 134 light-years away. Modern classifications include A0 V and the alternative A0 IIInn; the latter notation indicates broadened or “nebulous” lines caused in part by rapid rotation. It has no ancient Arabic proper name traceable in al-Ṣūfī.

**Etymology:** The modern proper name derives from the obsolete constellation, which derives from the Arcadian mountain.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

No Bayer Greek letters were assigned specifically to Mons Maenalus. The surviving principal identifiers are Flamsteed numbers in Boötes and Virgo. “Bode’s z” for 31 Boötis is a lower-case Latin letter from Bode’s expanded letter system, not a Bayer Greek letter and not an IAU proper name.

### Internal asterisms

The mountain itself is the asterism. No separately named historical sub-asterism inside it could be documented. Modern hobbyist line drawings vary and should not be projected backward onto Hevelius.

### Deep-sky objects

**Negative finding:** No Messier object, NGC object, nebula, cluster, or galaxy was found whose established name derives from Mons Maenalus. Because the figure never acquired official boundaries, claims that an object was “in Mons Maenalus” require a specified atlas and epoch. Modern catalogues place objects only in Boötes or Virgo.

Bode plotted numerous nebulae and clusters across his atlas, but no evidence was found that he assigned a named deep-sky object to the mountain as such.

### Variables, novae, and supernovae

**Negative finding:** No historical nova, supernova, or variable-star class is securely recorded under the name Mons Maenalus. Delporte was instructed to preserve existing variable-star constellation designations when drawing the official boundaries; none bore a *Maenali* designation because no standardized variable-star genitive for the abandoned figure existed.

The principal reconstructed stars are not famous historical variables. 109 Virginis is not classified as a significant naked-eye variable in the sources consulted.

### Meteor showers

**Negative finding:** There is no recognized Maenalid or Mons-Maenalid meteor shower. The nearby Quadrantids preserve the name of another obsolete northern constellation, Quadrans Muralis, but are unrelated to Mons Maenalus.

### Boundaries and transfers

**Ptolemy, second century CE:** No Mons Maenalus. Relevant stars were assigned to Boötes, Virgo, Serpens, or treated as unformed stars outside the figures.

**Bayer, 1603:** No mountain. Bayer’s *Uranometria* retained the classical figure of Boötes without an Arcadian platform.

**Schiller, 1627:** No mountain. The corresponding Boötes field was Christianized as St Sylvester.

**Hevelius, engraved title dated 1687; publication 1690:** Added the mountain beneath Boötes. It was primarily a landscape adjunct or sub-constellation, not a securely bounded autonomous region.

**Flamsteed, 1725/1729:** Flamsteed’s influential catalogue and atlas did not establish Mons Maenalus as an independent catalogue constellation. Its weak institutional footing is one reason it faded.

**Bode, 1782 and 1801:** Retained the name but contracted the recognizable mountain into southern Boötes. Bode’s plotting gave it its best-known form.

**Nineteenth century:** It persisted decoratively in Senex, Doppelmayr, Jamieson, *Urania’s Mirror*, Smyth, and popular lists, while scientific atlases increasingly omitted it. Argelander and Heis belonged to the movement toward utilitarian figures and standardized catalogue constellations; no evidence was found that either gave Mons Maenalus enduring independent status.

**IAU/Delporte:** Its former stars went chiefly to Boötes and Virgo. Hevelius’s far eastern fringe was absorbed into the modern surrounding constellations, including the Serpens Caput vicinity. It did not “lose” stars through one discrete Delporte transfer: the old figure had never possessed a legally defined polygon.

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity: the terrestrial mountain, not a constellation

**Fifth century BCE — documented geography:** Thucydides refers to the Maenalians, inhabitants of the Arcadian district (*History of the Peloponnesian War* 5.64, 5.67, 5.77). This is geographical and ethnographic testimony, not astronomy.

**Third century BCE — poetic-religious tradition:** Theocritus invokes Pan wandering “great Maenalus” in *Idyll* 1.123–126. Maenalus is already a canonical mountain of Pan, pastoral music, and Arcadian wildness.

**First century BCE — Virgil:** In *Eclogue* 8.21–24 the refrain calls for a Maenalian song; the mountain has “speaking pines,” hears shepherds’ loves, and hears Pan, who first made reeds musical. *Eclogue* 10.14–15 makes Maenalus and Lycaeus mourn the lovesick Gallus. *Georgics* 1.16–18 invokes Pan as guardian of sheep: *tua si tibi Maenala curae*, “if your own Maenalus is your care.” These passages made Maenalus a durable emblem of literary Arcadia. [Virgil, *Eclogues*](https://www.theoi.com/Text/VirgilEclogues.html); [*Georgics* I](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/VirgilGeorgicsI.php).

**Late first century BCE–early first century CE — Ovid:** Ovid repeatedly uses *Maenala* as shorthand for Arcadian terrain and the sphere of Pan/Diana. In *Metamorphoses* 1 the Arcadian setting belongs to the Lycaon story; in Book 2 Maenalus forms part of Callisto’s hunting country. In *Fasti* 5, Maenalus, Arcadia, and the Ladon worship the Arcadian god. Ovid’s testimony supports the mountain’s mythic associations, but not a celestial mountain.

**Second century CE — Pausanias:** *Description of Greece* 8.3.4 says Maenalus, son of Lycaon, founded the city named Maenalus. At 8.9.3–4 the mountain contains the tomb of Arcas. At 8.36.7–8 Pausanias describes the Maenalian country and reports that Mount Maenalus is especially sacred to Pan; people say they can hear Pan piping there. This is the fullest surviving ancient topographical testimony. [Pausanias, Book VIII](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias8B.html); [8.9.4 Greek and translation](https://pausanias.symmachus.org/translation/8/9/4.html).

**First–second century CE, date disputed — Pseudo-Apollodorus:** *Library* 3.8.1 lists Maenalus among Lycaon’s fifty sons and makes the elder Maenalus the instigator of the cannibal banquet offered to Zeus. Zeus overturns the table and destroys Lycaon and most sons with thunderbolts. This is a myth about the eponym, not an ancient catasterism. [Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.8](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DLibrary%3Abook%3D3%3Achapter%3D8).

### Ancient astronomical silence

**Documented negative evidence:** Aratus, Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* and its Epitome, Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica*, Hyginus’ *Fabulae*, Germanicus’ Aratea, the scholia to Aratus and Germanicus, Manilius, and Ptolemy know Boötes but not Mons Maenalus as a celestial figure.

Nonnus, Pausanias, Ovid, Pseudo-Apollodorus, and later mythographers preserve Arcadian persons or places relevant to the name, but none says that a mountain called Maenalus was placed in the sky. Thus there is no ancient answer to “who catasterized Mons Maenalus and why.” **Hevelius did.**

### 1515 — Dürer

Dürer’s printed hemispheres, prepared with Conrad Heinfogel and Johannes Stabius from Ptolemaic stars, show the traditional Boötes. No mountain constellation appears. This is important negative iconographic evidence.

### 1603 — Bayer

Bayer’s *Uranometria* shows Boötes but no Mons Maenalus. The future mountain’s stars remained attached to classical constellations or outside their principal outlines.

### 1627 — Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Augsburg, replaced Boötes with **Sanctus Silvester Pontifex Maximus**, St Sylvester, Supreme Pontiff. Mons Maenalus did not yet exist; therefore it was not independently Christianized. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/); [ETH record and facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720).

### 1687–1690 — invention by Hevelius

**Documented modern invention:** Johannes Hevelius designed Mons Maenalus for his *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia*. The atlas’s engraved title bears 1687; the complete work was published posthumously at Danzig/Gdańsk in 1690 by his widow and collaborator Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius.

The complete *Prodromus Astronomiae* comprised an introduction, a catalogue of 1,564 entries, and the atlas. The title-page/publication-date distinction explains why sources alternately date Mons Maenalus to **1687** or **1690**. [Hevelius digital catalogue discussion](https://pbarbier.com/hevelius/hevelius.html); [Verbunt and van Gent’s catalogue study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.3841); [Vienna historical catalogue](https://hwastro.univie.ac.at/rare/1690_Hevelius.htm).

**Purpose — strong scholarly reconstruction:** Hevelius gave Boötes an Arcadian ground to stand upon. Since one ancient identity of Boötes was **Arcas**, son of Callisto and eponymous ancestor of the Arcadians, Mount Maenalus created a coherent Arcadian tableau. The literary fame of Maenalus as Pan’s mountain, especially through Virgil, made it a natural emblem of pastoral Arcadia.

**Caution:** Hevelius’s known text does not explicitly say, in the sources located, “I chose Maenalus because Boötes is Arcas.” That causal link is therefore a persuasive reconstruction from placement and mythology, not a quoted declaration.

### 1721–1722 — John Senex

Senex’s celestial planispheres label and depict Mons Maenalus beneath Boötes. This is early evidence that the invention circulated beyond the Hevelius atlas.

### 1742 — Doppelmayr

Johann Gabriel Doppelmayr’s Nuremberg *Atlas Coelestis* shows Boötes standing on the mountain. Different charts reportedly spell it **Mons Mænalus** and **Mons Menalus**. [Linda Hall exhibition](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/22-the-innovations-of-hevelius-1690-1731/26-doppelmayr-johann-gabriel-atlas-coelestis/).

### About 1750 — Lotter

Tobias Conrad Lotter’s *Planisphaerium Coeleste* marks **M. Maenalus** as a mountain peak. This demonstrates continuity in German commercial celestial cartography.

### 1752–1763 — not Lacaille

**Disputed attribution rejected:** Some later popular handbooks attribute Mons Maenalus to Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille in 1752. This is demonstrably false because it appears in Hevelius’s work dated 1687/published 1690 and in Senex before Lacaille’s southern catalogue. Lacaille invented southern instrument constellations and **Mensa**, but not Mons Maenalus.

### 1782 — Bode’s German presentation

Bode included it in *Vorstellung der Gestirne* as **Berg Mänalus/der Berg Maenalus**. This vernacular form helped place it in German popular astronomy.

### 1801 — Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode printed **Mons Maenalus** beneath Boötes in his monumental *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio*, Berlin, 1801. The atlas has 20 large engraved plates and plotted 17,240 stars in the accompanying catalogue, plus double stars, nebulae, and clusters. [ETH complete facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [Bode bibliographic description](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035); [Linda Hall discussion](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/36-bode-s-uranographia-and-its-aftermath-1801-1851/).

**Bode’s treatment — documented from the chart:** He reduced Hevelius’s sprawling landscape to a smaller hill beneath the feet of Boötes, effectively a subordinate asterism. The recognizable stellar core became 14, 15, 18, and 31 Boötis; 31 Boötis bore Bode’s letter **z**.

**Textual caution:** The atlas title and prefatory material describe the global project, but no separate extended Bode mythological essay on Mons Maenalus was found. Claims that Bode supplied a detailed textual catasterism should therefore be treated as unverified.

**“830 stars in 109 groups” lead:** No support was found for this phrase as a description of Mons Maenalus or Bode’s *Uranographia*. Bode’s accompanying catalogue gives 17,240 stars. The “830 stars in 109 groups” wording appears to belong to another collection, index, or transmission chain and should not be repeated as a fact without a shelfmark or catalogue citation.

### 1818–1820 — Bode’s supplement

Bode’s *Nachtrag zu seiner Anleitung zur Kenntniss des gestirnten Himmels* continued to list **Berg Maenalus**.

### 1822 — Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson’s London *Celestial Atlas*, map VII, is explicitly titled “Bootes and Mons Maenalus, Asterion and Chara, or Canes Venatici, Coma Berenices, and Quadrans Muralis.” It shows Boötes standing on the colored mountain. [David Rumsey record and high-resolution image](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~34111~1170845:-Bootes-and-Mons-Maenalus,-Asterion).

### Circa 1825 — *Urania’s Mirror*

The anonymous constellation cards known as *Urania’s Mirror* borrowed closely from Jamieson. The Boötes card retained Mons Maenalus as the herdsman’s mountain platform. Research by Peter Hingley attributes the set probably to the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam. The attribution is strong but remains phrased probabilistically. [Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror* study](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e88349382fdcfa294ff6ccf80007dbf2e9311237&repid=rep1&type=pdf).

A Cary miniature globe of about 1825 omitted Mons Maenalus even while including most Hevelian figures, evidence that reception was never universal. [Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-19753).

### 1844 — Smyth

Admiral William Henry Smyth’s *Bedford Catalogue* mentions Mons Maenalus and correctly attributes it to Hevelius. Smyth also discussed the erroneous *Mons Menelaus* form.

### 1845 onward — decline

The scientific catalogue tradition increasingly preferred a smaller stable set of constellations. Mons Maenalus had no conspicuous outline, no bright distinctive star in Bode’s reduced form, no important catalogue nomenclature depending upon it, and no consensus boundaries. Argelander’s and Heis’s practical charts exemplify the broader shift away from ornamental micro-constellations.

### 1889 — Allen

Richard Hinckley Allen’s *Star-Names and Their Meanings* records it as a Hevelian figure, notes German **Berg Maenalus**, Italian **Menalo**, and the corrupt **Mons Menelaus**, and repeats a highly speculative Hindu-origin theory. [Allen, p. 290](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Mons_Maenalus%2A.html).

### 1922–1930 — exclusion and redistribution

Mons Maenalus was absent from the 1922 IAU list. Delporte subsequently enclosed its sky within Boötes and Virgo when creating exhaustive boundaries for the retained constellations. His boundaries were based on equatorial coordinates for epoch B1875.0, so precession makes them appear slightly tilted on modern-coordinate maps.

### 1923 — Robson’s astrological afterlife

Vivian E. Robson’s *Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology*, p. 52, assigned Mons Maenalus the influence: pride, dignity, and ability, mixed with destructiveness and a life beset by difficulties. This is a **modern astrological invention**, not a doctrine from Ptolemy. [Robson excerpt and citation](https://astrologyking.com/mons-maenalus-constellation/).

### 2016 and later — artistic afterlife

Alexandra Paperno included *Mons Maenalus* in her *Abolished Constellations* series, treating abandoned astronomical figures as works on paper. [Paperno project](https://alexandrapaperno.com/abolished-constellations-paper/).

### 2026 — Maenalus becomes a star name

The IAU Working Group on Star Names transferred the historical memory of the obsolete constellation to 109 Virginis. The name now has official astronomical standing only as a stellar proper name.

## Myths and legends by culture

## Greek and Roman material

### Arcas and Callisto

**Ancient myth, but only indirectly connected to the mountain constellation:** One major catasterism identifies Boötes with **Arcas**, son of Zeus and Callisto. Callisto is transformed into a bear; Arcas later pursues or nearly kills her, and Zeus places them in heaven. Versions differ over whether Arcas becomes Boötes, Ursa Minor, or another figure and whether Hera, Zeus, or Artemis causes Callisto’s transformation.

The Arcas–Boötes identification is found in the Eratosthenic catasterism tradition and Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* 2.1 and 2.4. Ovid gives the most influential Latin narrative in *Metamorphoses* 2.401–530. Germanicus and the scholia transmit variants.

**Relation to Mons Maenalus:** Since Arcas is Arcadia’s eponymous hero and Pausanias places his tomb on Maenalus, later interpreters reasonably understand Hevelius’s mountain as Arcas’s Arcadian ground. But no ancient source places a celestial mountain under him.

### Arcas’s tomb on Maenalus

**Local tradition documented by Pausanias, second century CE:** Pausanias 8.9.3–4 reports Arcas’s tomb on Mount Maenalus at a meeting of roads. The Mantineans later claimed to have moved his bones to Mantinea on Delphic instructions. This makes the mountain a particularly apt base for Boötes-as-Arcas.

### Maenalus, son of Lycaon

**Ancient eponymic myth:** Pausanias 8.3.4 makes Maenalus one of Lycaon’s sons and founder of the city Maenalus. Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.8.1 makes him one of fifty sons and specifically the elder brother who instigated the serving of human flesh to Zeus. Zeus overturns the table at Trapezus and destroys the impious family, sparing Nyctimus in that version.

**Variant:** Pausanias’ account of Lycaon’s crime at 8.2.3 focuses upon Lycaon sacrificing a human infant to Zeus Lycaeus and becoming a wolf. It does not give Maenalus Pseudo-Apollodorus’ instigating role. The versions should not be conflated.

**Later extension:** Tzetzes on Lycophron 481 repeats material linking Maenalus to Lycaon’s family. This is late mythographic transmission, not independent archaic evidence.

### Pan of Maenalus

**Ancient cult and poetic tradition:** Theocritus’ *Idyll* 1 invokes Pan at Maenalus. Virgil makes Maenalus’ pines sing and its landscape listen to Pan’s pipe. Pausanias 8.36.8 says the mountain is especially sacred to Pan and records the local report that his piping can be heard there.

This is the strongest ancient mythic identity attached to the mountain. Hevelius’s figure can therefore be called “Pan’s Arcadian mountain,” although Hevelius drew a mountain beneath Boötes, not Pan himself.

### Diana/Artemis and the Arcadian hunt

**Tradition:** Ovid’s Callisto hunts in Arcadia as a companion of Diana; Maenalus forms part of that wilderness. Later summaries consequently call it one of Diana’s hunting grounds. This is a literary-geographical association. No ancient cult text found in this research calls the celestial Mons Maenalus Diana’s mountain.

### Virgil’s singing mountain

**Literary tradition, first century BCE:** In *Eclogue* 8 Maenalus is personified: it has speaking pines and perpetually hears shepherds and Pan. The recurring *Maenalios … versus* makes “Maenalian” virtually synonymous with Arcadian pastoral song. In *Eclogue* 10 Maenalus mourns Gallus. These are poetic personifications, not distinct myths or catasterisms.

### Ovid’s Maenalus

**Literary tradition:** Ovid repeatedly employs the plural *Maenala* for the Arcadian highlands. It supplies the landscape for Lycaon, Callisto, Diana, Pan, and Arcadian antiquity. Ovid never says that Maenalus became a constellation.

### What the classical catasterism sources do not say

**Negative evidence:** No Maenalus mountain appears in Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or Epitome, Hyginus’ astronomical or mythographical works, Aratus or his scholia, Germanicus or his scholia, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, Pausanias, or Ovid as a celestial figure. Their Boötes stories are relevant background only.

There are therefore no ancient competing identities, divine installer, reason for stellar placement, or ancient “every myth of the constellation” beyond those belonging independently to Boötes.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Mesopotamian and Egyptian

**Negative finding:** No Mesopotamian or Egyptian mountain corresponding specifically to Mons Maenalus could be traced. Proposed equations between the broad Boötes region and Mesopotamian figures concern Boötes or neighboring constellations, not Hevelius’s faint southern hill.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* follows the Ptolemaic constellations. He could not have named Mons Maenalus, which was invented about seven centuries later.

The southern-Boötes stars would have been treated within **al-ʿAwwāʾ/Boötes-related stellar material** or as stars outside the classical figure depending on the catalogue entry. Boötes itself acquired Arabic descriptions such as **al-Rāmiḥ**, “the lancer,” because of its staff or spear. That name belongs to Boötes/Arcturus traditions, not the mountain.

**Bedouin asterisms:** No securely identified Bedouin asterism consisting specifically of 14, 15, 18, and 31 Boötis or 71/109 Virginis was found. Assigning a generic Arabic Boötes name to Mons Maenalus would be misleading.

### Persian

Persian astronomical catalogues largely transmitted the Greek–Arabic Ptolemaic framework. No independent Persian Mount Maenalus figure was found. Persian names attached to the greater Boötes/Virgo regions do not establish a Persian antecedent of the mountain.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mapping

**Negative finding:** No premodern Hebrew constellation called Mount Maenalus was traced. Biblical, rabbinic, and medieval Hebrew attempts to identify constellations concern recognized ancient figures and zodiacal or planetary schemes.

No documented Kabbalistic mapping specifically assigns these faint stars to Maenalus. Modern occult diagrams that do so would necessarily postdate Hevelius and require individual provenance.

### Chinese astronomy

The relevant sky does not form a Chinese “Mount Maenalus.” Chinese astronomy divided the region among asterisms associated with the **Heavenly Market Enclosure** and the broader Virgo–Boötes sector. Exact identifications of faint stars differ among reconstructions, and no sufficiently authoritative cross-identification was found for every Hevelian hill star.

**Conclusion:** A Chinese equivalence should not be invented from geographic overlap. At most, individual modern stars can be cross-indexed to Chinese asterism positions where historical charts permit.

### Indian astronomy and lunar mansions

Mons Maenalus itself has no Indian antecedent. Its stars lie well north of the ecliptic except for the more southerly Virgo members; they are not the defining stars of a classical nakshatra.

Richard Hinckley Allen repeated a conjecture, ultimately associated with J. F. Hewitt, that the mountain might recall a prehistoric Indian “mother-mountain” climbed by a sun-god, with imagery transferred from Hercules to neighboring Boötes. **This is unsupported speculation.** No textual chain from an Indian source to Hevelius has been produced, and Hevelius’s plainly Arcadian name and placement supply a sufficient explanation.

### Julius Schiller’s Christian sky

In Schiller’s 1627 *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, the Boötes field became **St Sylvester, Supreme Pontiff**—*Sancti Silvestri Pontificis Maximi*. Because Mons Maenalus had not yet been invented, no saint or object “replaced” it separately. After Hevelius added the hill, Schiller’s system was not revised to accommodate it.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

**Negative finding:** No early alchemical or Hermetic text was found treating Mons Maenalus as a symbolic constellation. Bode did show such explicitly modern figures as *Officina Typographica* and *Machina Electrica*, but the mere presence of Mons Maenalus in Bode’s crowded atlas does not make it an alchemical emblem.

Later occult writers may analogize the mountain to ascent, initiation, Pan, or Arcadian nature. Without a dated source these are modern interpretations, not historical tradition.

### Astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* assigns planetary natures to stars of ancient constellations, but naturally says nothing about Mons Maenalus.

Robson’s 1923 claim that it gives “pride and dignity with great abilities,” mixed with destructiveness and difficulty, is the earliest explicit constellation-wide astrological delineation found in this research. It should be classified as **twentieth-century astrological synthesis**, not ancient doctrine.

The sometimes-published “Mae” abbreviation, “Montis Maenali” genitive, one-degree star orbs, and tropical zodiac positions are modern astrologers’ apparatus. They have no IAU or Hevelian authority.

No Behenian fixed star belonged to Mons Maenalus. None of its principal faint stars is among the canonical fifteen Behenian stars.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas, second century CE

The Farnese celestial globe preserves a Greco-Roman constellation scheme. It shows Boötes but no Mount Maenalus. The omission is expected and is evidence against an ancient mountain constellation.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, tenth century onward

Al-Ṣūfī’s paired inside/outside constellation drawings preserve Boötes under the Ptolemaic system. No mountain appears. Later manuscript variation does not provide a Maenalus antecedent.

### Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

Dürer’s northern hemisphere shows Boötes in the inherited classical scheme without the mountain.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* shows Boötes but no landscape platform.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

Schiller replaces the Boötes image with St Sylvester. His plates view the heavens in globe-style reversal, described as a divine or exterior view. No Mons Maenalus exists yet.

### Hevelius, Danzig/Gdańsk, 1687/1690

This is the first secure celestial depiction. Boötes stands upon a substantial engraved Arcadian mountain. Hevelius’s figures are reversed left-to-right, following the celestial-globe or external view. The mountain functions visually as ground and geographically as Arcadia.

### Senex, London, 1721–1722

Planispheres retain and label the mountain peak.

### Doppelmayr, Nuremberg, 1742

Boötes stands on the peak; spelling varies between plates. This is an important stage in the spread of Hevelian iconography through German atlas making.

### Lotter, Augsburg, circa 1750

The planisphere labels it *M. Maenalus*.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s plate redraws the entire scene in his own style. The mountain is smaller and more schematic than Hevelius’s, beneath the feet of a Boötes equipped with staff/sickle. Bode’s atlas is the source of the widely reproduced monochrome detail. [Bode image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bode_mons.jpg).

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Hand-colored map VII gives the mountain a strong pictorial presence. It is explicitly named in the map title.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, circa 1825

The translucent constellation card derived from Jamieson retains the mountain. This is likely the best-known popular depiction after Bode.

### Later art

Landseer—identified by Allen only by surname—made a “striking representation” of the Husbandman standing on the figure. The exact artist, work, date, and present location were not established from the sources consulted; Allen’s statement should not be expanded without further provenance.

Alexandra Paperno’s 2016 *Mons Maenalus* treats the lost constellation as contemporary conceptual art.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

**Negative finding:** No securely documented ancient coin, cathedral portal, monumental ceiling, or pre-Hevelian calendar depicts Mons Maenalus as a constellation. Images of Boötes, Arcas, Pan, or an Arcadian mountain are not automatically images of the Hevelian figure.

## People, events, and works

### Johannes and Elisabeth Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius conceived and engraved the atlas program. The completed *Prodromus Astronomiae* appeared after his death through Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius, his wife, observer, collaborator, and editor. Modern scholarship notes that the precise extent of her observational and editorial contribution remains uncertain, though surviving images and records show it was substantial.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* and *Uranographia* prolonged the mountain’s life. His atlas gathered nearly every old and new figure then circulating; that inclusiveness preserved minor constellations but also demonstrated the overcrowding that later reformers sought to cure.

### Alexander Jamieson and Richard Rouse Bloxam

Jamieson made the mountain accessible to a British popular audience in 1822. *Urania’s Mirror* then copied much of his iconography. Peter Hingley’s research makes Bloxam the probable designer or author of the cards, replacing an older unsupported attribution to “a young lady.”

### Literary Arcadia

Virgil’s Maenalus became part of the Western pastoral tradition. The mountain’s speaking pines and Pan-pipes underlie later poetic and visual Arcadias, including Renaissance and early-modern pastoral painting and literature. This reception explains why “Maenalus” was culturally intelligible to Hevelius’s learned audience.

### Navigation and exploration

**Negative finding:** No explorer or voyage was found that navigated specifically by “Mons Maenalus.” Its stars are faint and the figure was neither ancient nor nautically standard. Navigators used prominent stars such as Arcturus and Spica, not this decorative hill.

### Ships, missions, buildings, music

No historically important ship, astronomical mission, or building securely named for the obsolete constellation was found. The modern Greek mountain and municipalities generate unrelated uses of *Mainalo/Maenalus*, which should not be counted as constellation reception.

A modern Florida company called “Mons Maenalus Property LLC” exists, but no evidence connects it culturally to the astronomical figure beyond use of the name; it is not historically significant to the constellation.

## Controversies and disputes

### Was it invented in 1687 or 1690?

**Resolution:** Both dates describe different stages. The engraved atlas title bears 1687, the year Hevelius died and the material was printed/prepared; the complete posthumous publication appeared in 1690. “Invented in 1687; published in 1690” is the clearest formulation.

### Was it invented by Lacaille?

**False later attribution:** No. A nineteenth- or twentieth-century popular table assigns it to Lacaille in 1752, but the dated Hevelius atlas and earlier Senex maps refute this. Lacaille created **Mensa**, the other commonly cited celestial mountain or table-mountain figure.

### Is it the only mountain constellation besides Mensa?

**Qualified tradition:** Mons Maenalus and Mensa are the two best-known constellations explicitly named for terrestrial mountains—Arcadian Maenalus and Table Mountain. But “the only two mountains in the sky” depends on definitions: other figures can contain hills, rocks, or geographical features without being officially named *Mons*. Mensa survives; Mons Maenalus does not.

### Was it an independent constellation or part of Boötes?

**Genuine classificatory ambiguity:** Hevelius labeled and drew it, and later atlases listed it among new constellations; yet it functioned as landscape beneath Boötes and lacked a stable independent catalogue population. Bode’s reduced form is best described as a **sub-constellation or asterism within southern Boötes**. Calling it simply an autonomous constellation exaggerates its historical status; calling it mere decoration ignores its label and reception.

### Which was its brightest star?

**Resolved by version:** 109 Virginis in a broad reconstruction of Hevelius; 31 Boötis in Bode’s contracted asterism. Statements that give either without naming the reconstruction are incomplete.

### Does 109 Virginis belong to the figure?

**Reconstruction, now commemorated officially:** It is outside the compact Bode core but appears in broader Hevelian reconstructions. The IAU’s 2026 choice of the name *Maenalus* for 109 Virginis endorses the historical association as a naming commemoration, not a retroactive boundary ruling.

### “Mons Menelaus”

**Documented error:** This form confuses Maenalus with Menelaus. Smyth suggested influence from Menelaus of Alexandria; Allen repeated the warning. No evidence shows that Hevelius intended either the Homeric king Menelaus or the Alexandrian mathematician.

### Personal genealogy of Maenalus

**Ancient variants and modern confusion:** Pausanias and Pseudo-Apollodorus make Maenalus a son of Lycaon. Online retellings sometimes call him a son of Arcas or a brother of Callisto. The “brother of Callisto” formulation follows the broad Lycaonid genealogy only if Callisto is also treated as Lycaon’s daughter. “Son of Arcas” was not verified in the primary sources consulted and should be marked disputed unless a specific ancient text is supplied.

### Did Hevelius choose it because Boötes was Arcas?

**Plausible scholarly reconstruction, not documented declaration:** The Arcadian connections are unusually tight—Arcas, his tomb on Maenalus, Pan’s mountain, and Boötes standing on it—but no explicit Hevelius sentence explaining the choice was located.

### Hindu “mother-mountain” origin

**Unsupported speculative claim:** Allen asks whether Hevelius’s figure might preserve an ancient Indian solar myth discussed by Hewitt. No transmission evidence connects that theory to Hevelius. It is a late nineteenth-century diffusionist conjecture, not an origin account.

### Astrological antiquity

**Popular error:** Robson’s delineation is sometimes presented alongside ancient fixed-star lore in a way that implies classical authority. Neither Ptolemy nor any ancient astrologer could have described Mons Maenalus. The interpretation first appears, so far as this research found, in Robson’s 1923 compilation.

### Was it “abolished in 1922”?

**Terminological qualification:** In ordinary language, yes. Strictly, the IAU adopted a list from which it was absent. The formal boundaries followed in 1928/1930. There was no individual sentence declaring “Mons Maenalus is hereby abolished.”

### Why did it disappear?

**Scholarly synthesis:** There is no known IAU debate devoted exclusively to rejecting it. It had already become functionally obsolete: faint stars, unstable extent, overlap with Boötes/Virgo, little catalogue use, and a chiefly decorative purpose. The IAU standardized established scientific practice rather than suddenly destroying a vigorous constellation.

### Hoaxes

The contested-material search found no hoax specifically involving Mons Maenalus. The principal problems are misattribution, spelling corruption, genealogical conflation, overconfident star membership, and speculative ancient origins.

## Open questions

1. **Exact Hevelian membership:** A complete entry-by-entry reconstruction should be made directly from the 1690 catalogue’s *informes* and the plate geometry, cross-matched to the Verbunt–van Gent machine-readable catalogue. Existing popular lists mix Hevelius and Bode.

2. **Bode’s textual wording:** The atlas and catalogue were located, but a separate explanatory paragraph by Bode devoted solely to Mons Maenalus was not found. Page-level examination of the *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* may establish whether he described its origin beyond labeling it.

3. **The “M. Menalis” readings:** Reported spelling variants should be checked against specific original leaves to distinguish authorial Latin, engraver’s abbreviation, and modern transcription.

4. **Landseer image:** Allen’s reference requires identification by full artist name, title, publication, and repository.

5. **Chinese cross-identifications:** Specialist comparison of the Hevelian stars with Sun Xiaochun and Kistemaker’s reconstructions could identify which individual Chinese asterisms overlap, but no single Chinese equivalent of the mountain should be expected.

6. **109 Virginis naming record:** The IAU’s downloadable current catalogue should be preserved with version/date because cached pages variously report 2025 and 15 April 2026.

7. **“830 stars in 109 groups”:** This phrase could not be reconciled with Bode’s catalogue of 17,240 stars. Its originating “Bode collection on chain” record needs a catalogue identifier or URL.

8. **First use before the final Hevelius atlas:** It remains possible that manuscript proofs or correspondence used the name before the engraved 1687 title. No earlier printed attestation was found.
