# Coma Berenices: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — directly attested by a primary text, surviving historical object, official catalogue, or modern scientific publication.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion inferred by modern specialists from fragmentary texts, star positions, manuscript transmission, or historical context.
- **Tradition** — a repeatedly transmitted identification whose historical origin may be imperfectly documented.
- **Disputed claim** — an identification or attribution for which competent sources disagree.
- **Legend/myth** — a narrated supernatural or catasteristic event, reported as such rather than as literal history.
- **Modern invention** — a recent name, symbolism, or interpretation not found in the older tradition.
- **Unverified claim** — a statement repeated in popular literature for which the requested research did not locate adequate evidence.

## Basic identification

### Status

**Documented fact — kept by the IAU.** Coma Berenices is one of the 88 modern constellations. Its official abbreviation is **Com** and its Latin genitive is **Comae Berenices**. The IAU adopted the three-letter abbreviation in 1922; Eugène Delporte’s equatorial boundaries, published for the IAU in 1930, permanently defined its modern territory.

It was therefore **not abolished**. Its stars did not have to be redistributed after 1930, although Delporte’s geometric border fixed which formerly disputed stars and deep-sky objects belonged to Coma Berenices rather than Leo, Virgo, Boötes, Canes Venatici, or Ursa Major.

The constellation covers about **386.5 square degrees**, approximately 0.94 percent of the sky, ranking 42nd in area. Its modern neighbors are:

- Canes Venatici to the north
- Boötes to the east
- Virgo to the south
- Leo to the west

Its modern boundaries are a twelve-segment polygon, approximately spanning right ascension 11h58m–13h36m and declination +13°.3–+33°.3 in Delporte’s adopted B1875 framework. Precession means those simple coordinate values do not remain simple in later equinoxes.

The constellation contains the **north galactic pole**, conventionally near J2000 RA 12h51m, Dec +27°08′, so sight lines through it leave the dusty plane of the Milky Way. This explains its exceptional richness in visible external galaxies; it does not mean those galaxies are physically associated with the nearby stars forming the outline.

### What the figure is

The ancient and early-modern figure is not the queen herself but a detached mass of hair: a lock, curl, braid, tress, or whole head of hair. Atlas artists variously drew:

- a small curl or lock;
- a tied braid;
- a long loose fall of hair;
- the back of a woman’s head with flowing hair;
- occasionally a wheatsheaf-like or leafy mass emphasizing the unresolved star cluster.

The naked-eye core of the traditional figure is the loose open cluster now called **Melotte 111**, especially the stars around γ Comae Berenices. The modern constellation is much larger and includes α and β Comae Berenices and extensive galaxy fields south and east of the ancient cluster.

**Etymology — documented linguistic history.** *Berenice* is Latin *Berenīcē*, from Macedonian Greek Βερενίκη, corresponding to classical Φερενίκη, *Pherenikē*: “bringer of victory,” from *pherein*, “to bring/carry,” and *nikē*, “victory.” The form is especially apt for a queen celebrated after a victorious war, but the name existed independently of the constellation.

### Bode’s labels

In Bode’s earlier **Vorstellung der Gestirne** (Berlin and Stralsund, 1782) the German heading is **“Das Haupthaar der Berenice.”** In the 1801 **Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio**, the mapped Latin figure is **COMA BERENICES**. Bode shows a luxuriant fall of hair behind the region of Boötes and Leo rather than a human queen standing bodily in the sky.

**Correction to a supplied lead.** The phrase “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be reconciled with Bode’s *Uranographia*. Bode’s 1801 atlas and accompanying catalogue contain far more than 830 plotted stars—commonly reported as more than 17,000—and over 100 old and proposed constellation figures. “830 stars in 109 groups” may describe a separate collection, database subset, chain record, or digitization project, but it should not be treated as Bode’s own census without a specific collection record.

## Stars and objects

### The traditional naked-eye figure

Ptolemy’s catalogue does not give Coma an independent constellation chapter. In the “unformed” stars outside Leo’s canonical image, he describes the northernmost point of the **νεφελοειδὴς συστροφή**, *nepheloeidēs systrophē*, the “nebulous/cloud-like mass,” between the extremities of Leo and the Great Bear, called the **Πλόκαμος**, “Lock.”

Modern reconstructions commonly identify Ptolemy’s three frame stars as:

- γ Comae Berenices = Flamsteed 15 Com
- 7 Comae Berenices
- 23 Comae Berenices

Ptolemy additionally says the grouping resembles an ivy leaf, **φύλλον κισσίνου**, *phyllon kissinou*. The historical proper-name form **Kissin/Phyllon Kissinou** has consequently been attached inconsistently to stars in this area.

**Disputed identification.** Precise identifications of faint Ptolemaic “unformed” stars depend on converting ancient coordinates and interpreting whether a catalogue entry denotes one star, a cluster, or a pictorial boundary. The three-star frame above is persuasive but should not be mistaken for an uncontroversial ancient Bayer-style identification.

#### Diadem

**Modern/traditional name of uncertain origin.** “Diadem” means a royal headband or crown and is symbolically appropriate to Berenice. It is not an ancient proper name securely traceable to Greek or Arabic catalogues. The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Diadem** for α Comae Berenices A in 2017. Older sources also call the star *Al Zafirah* or *Al Dafirah*, but those assignments reflect the common modern conversion of an asterism-name into a single-star name.

#### Al Dafirah

Arabic **al-Ḍafīrah**, “the braid,” originally described the group. Ulugh Beg’s catalogue tradition has been interpreted as restricting it to two stars, often identified as 7 and 23 Com. R. H. Allen assigned “Al Dafirah” to γ/15 Com. This is a classic example of a collective Arabic name being redistributed in European star-name literature.

#### Phyllon Kissinou and Kissin

Greek *phyllon kissinou* means “ivy leaf.” It is a description of the shape of the cloudy group in the *Almagest*, not necessarily a personal proper name for 23 Com. Later catalogues turned the phrase into the star name **Kissin**, and it has also been misplaced onto 21 Com/UU Com. The competing assignments are products of later catalogue transmission.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

Coma has an unusual designation history. Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria* depicted the figure, but the familiar α, β, γ sequence cannot safely be treated as a straightforward original Bayer ranking like that of the classical constellations. Later cataloguers regularized Greek-letter usage. The order is not by brightness: β is marginally brighter than α and γ.

Flamsteed numbers run through a much larger selection of stars, including the traditional cluster. Historical texts often enumerate 7, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 21, and 23 Com among the hair or tuft.

### Melotte 111: the Coma Star Cluster

**Documented astronomical fact.** The visible “hair” is substantially the nearby open cluster:

- Melotte 111
- Collinder 256
- the Coma Berenices Cluster
- distance about 85–87 parsecs, roughly 280 light-years;
- apparent diameter roughly 4.5–7.5 degrees depending on membership and limiting magnitude;
- age estimates commonly about 600–800 million years.

P. J. Melotte listed it as no. 111 in his 1915 cluster catalogue. It was absent from Messier’s and the NGC’s cluster lists because its great angular size and sparse appearance made its physical nature difficult to establish. Robert Julius Trumpler’s 1938 proper-motion, radial-velocity, and photometric study identified a coherent cluster population.

Modern Gaia work has expanded the membership, identified low-mass members and tidal tails, and shown that some conspicuous projected stars—especially γ Com—are probably foreground or background objects rather than members.

### The Coma Cluster and dark matter

**Documented scientific history.** The Coma Cluster, Abell 1656, lies about 100 Mpc or roughly 320–330 million light-years away and contains more than a thousand catalogued galaxies. Its central giant ellipticals include NGC 4874 and NGC 4889.

In 1933 Fritz Zwicky applied the virial theorem to radial velocities in the cluster and concluded that the system required vastly more gravitating mass than the luminous galaxies supplied. In his German paper he invoked **dunkle Materie**, “dark matter.” His 1937 English paper developed the cluster-mass analysis further.

**Qualification.** Zwicky’s numerical mass-to-light discrepancy was exaggerated by the distance scale and mass estimates then available, but the fundamental discrepancy survived better data. It is therefore fair to call the Coma calculation the first famous inference of dark matter on extragalactic scales, not a finished modern measurement of the dark-matter fraction.

### Variable stars and transients

Notable variables include:

- **FK Comae Berenices**, prototype of the FK Com class: rapidly rotating, magnetically active, apparently single G/K giants, probably formed by binary merger. It shows starspots, chromospheric emission, flares, and strong X-ray/ultraviolet activity.
- **FS Comae Berenices**, an eruptive/emission-line variable associated with the FS CMa class; the class name is easily confused typographically with the constellation’s genitive.
- **R Comae Berenices**, a Mira-type long-period red variable.
- **UU Comae Berenices = 21 Com**, a chemically peculiar rotational variable.
- **AI Comae Berenices**, a pulsating variable of the RR Lyrae family.
- α Com has historically been suspected of eclipse-like variability, but predicted eclipses in 2014–15 failed because the visual binary orbit had been calculated incorrectly. This is a useful modern example of a well-publicized astronomical prediction corrected by new astrometry.

Numerous supernovae have been recorded in Coma’s galaxies, particularly in the Virgo and Coma galaxy-cluster fields. Historically important examples include **SN 1960R in M100**, followed by later M100 supernovae such as SN 1979C, SN 2006X, and SN 2019ehk. The very large number of transient designations makes an exhaustive all-survey list impractical; no securely documented ancient “guest star” or naked-eye nova uniquely assigned to Coma Berenices was found.

### Meteor showers

The **Comae Berenicids**, IAU shower no. 20, code COM, are a minor December shower with a high geocentric velocity, commonly quoted near 65 km/s. The official grammatical form is now frequently “Comae Berenicids,” using the constellation’s Latin genitive; “Coma Berenicids” remains common in older literature.

The IAU Meteor Data Center also lists proposed or minor radiant groups in this region, including the **April α Comae Berenicids**. A shower’s name indicates the apparent radiant, not a physical origin in the constellation’s stars.

## Chronology of attestation

### Possible Bronze Age antecedents

#### Babylonian/Akkadian material

**Disputed scholarly reconstruction.** A star or star group written **MUL.ḪÉ.GÁL-a-a** has been tentatively placed in or near modern Coma Berenices. The Sumerogram ḪÉ.GÁL conveys abundance or luxuriance, but translations and placement vary. It is not evidence for a Babylonian “Berenice’s Hair.”

A more recent argument compares Ptolemy’s cloud-shaped group with a **MUL.APIN** expression involving the spadix or frond of the date palm of the goddess Erua. The similarity of a leafy or tufted shape is suggestive, but cultural continuity into the Greek Berenice story has not been demonstrated.

**Finding:** Babylonian antecedents are positional or morphological candidates, not an early version of the Hellenistic queen’s lock.

#### Egyptian material

**Disputed scholarly reconstruction.** Some archaeoastronomical work identifies the Ramesside star-clock expression **sbꜣw ꜥšꜣw**, “many stars,” with the Coma cluster. Since that designation is generic and Egyptian stellar coordinates are indirect, the identification is possible but not secure.

Popular claims that Coma appears on the Dendera zodiac as “a woman and child” are **unverified or erroneous**. Dendera is late Ptolemaic/Roman, not “2000 BC,” and the figure identifications in its non-zodiacal fields are contested. No evidence was found that its supposed woman-and-child is a recognized predecessor of Coma Berenices.

### 246–245 BCE: historical setting

**Documented history.** Ptolemy III Euergetes became king in 246 BCE and departed on the Third Syrian or Laodicean War following the murder of his sister Berenice Syra and her child.

Berenice II was queen of Egypt and daughter of Magas of Cyrene and Apama. Hyginus incorrectly or confusingly calls her a daughter of “Ptolemy and Arsinoe” and Ptolemy III’s sister. She was his cousin, not his full sister. Later retellings often reproduce Hyginus’s dynastic error.

### About 245 BCE: Conon and Callimachus

**Documented ancient literary attribution; supernatural event is legend/myth.** Conon of Samos identified a cluster of faint stars near Leo’s tail as Berenice’s vanished hair. Callimachus composed the **Lock of Berenice**, later incorporated at the close of Book IV of the *Aetia*.

The exact date and choreography are reconstructed. A date around 245 BCE fits Ptolemy’s return and the composition’s court setting. Some scholars propose a public ceremony in which Conon announced the celestial identification and Callimachus supplied the poem. The sources do not provide an eyewitness narrative adequate to prove that reconstruction.

### Callimachus, *Aetia* fr. 110 Pfeiffer/Harder

Fragments preserved on papyrus, especially **PSI IX 1092**, contain part of the Greek original. The lock speaks in the first person. It swears its reluctance to leave Berenice’s head; Zephyrus carries it through the air, and Aphrodite/Arsinoe places it as a new star among the ancient stars.

The poem probably closed *Aetia* Book IV. Its publication history is disputed: Pfeiffer thought an occasional poem may have been revised for inclusion in the collected *Aetia*; later papyrological work has complicated the idea of multiple editions.

### Third or second century BCE: Pseudo-Eratosthenes

The surviving *Catasterismi* is not Eratosthenes’ autograph but an epitomized and interpolated work drawing on Hellenistic material.

Under **Leo**, the tradition identifies seven unfigured stars by the Lion’s tail as the lock of Berenice Euergetis. Under **Corona Borealis**, another tradition calls the nearby or associated hair **Ariadne’s hair**.

**Disputed interpretation.** “Ariadne’s Hair” need not prove that the same seven stars had an older, independent constellation-name. The epitome may preserve overlapping explanatory traditions, and later summaries sometimes conflate Ariadne’s crown, Ariadne’s hair, and Berenice’s lock.

### First century BCE: Catullus 66

**Documented fact.** Catullus translated or freely adapted Callimachus’s poem in Latin, probably in the 50s BCE. It is the fullest surviving ancient account.

The opening praises Conon, who mapped the risings and settings of stars and “saw me, a lock from Berenice’s head, shining brightly in celestial light” (*Catullus*, 66.1–8). Berenice vowed the hair to all the gods for her husband’s return from war in “Assyrian” territory and fulfilled the vow with sacrificial blood.

The lock insists:

> *invita, o regina, tuo de vertice cessi, / invita*  
> “Unwillingly, O queen, I departed from your head—unwillingly.”  
> (*Catullus* 66.39–40)

A winged Zephyrus carries it; Venus places it beside Virgo, Leo, Callisto/Ursa Major, and Boötes. Yet the lock ends by wishing it could return to Berenice’s head even if the stars themselves should fall: celestial immortality is inferior to bodily reunion.

The poem also preserves courtly and sexual themes: Berenice’s grief at separation, bridal tears, consummation, dynastic marriage, perfume offerings, and exhortations to marital fidelity.

### First century BCE/CE: Hyginus

*Poetica Astronomica* 2.24 gives the clearest prose narrative:

- Seven stars lie in a triangle above Leo near Virgo.
- Conon of Samos and Callimachus call them Berenice’s hair.
- Berenice vowed to cut her hair if Ptolemy returned victorious.
- She placed it in the temple of Venus Arsinoe Zephyritis.
- It disappeared the following day.
- The king was distressed.
- Conon, wishing to gain royal favor, pointed to seven stars “empty of a figure” and **pretended/represented** that they were the lock: *quas esse fingeret crinem*.

That skeptical verb matters. Hyginus does not simply report a miracle; he allows courtly invention or flattery.

Hyginus then preserves otherwise rare biographical traditions:

1. **Horse breeding.** Some writers, “with Callimachus,” said Berenice bred horses and sent them to Olympia.
2. **The warrior princess.** When her father’s army fled, Berenice allegedly mounted a horse, rallied the troops, killed many enemies, and drove the remainder away; Callimachus therefore called her **magnanimam**, great-souled.
3. **Dowries for Lesbian women.** Eratosthenes allegedly said she ordered unpaid parental dowries restored to maidens of Lesbos and instituted a legal petition or claims procedure.

These are traditions about Berenice, not additional catasterisms.

### Pliny the Elder, first century CE

*Natural History* 2.178 states that Italy cannot see Canopus “nor that which they call Berenice’s Hair”: *nec Canopum Italia et quem vocant Berenices crinem*.

This passage later generated a famous mistranslation discussed below.

### Ptolemy, about 150 CE

**Documented astronomical treatment.** Ptolemy did not list Coma among the 48 constellations. He placed its stars among the **ἀμόρφωτοι**, the stars outside Leo’s formal image, and called the cloud-like condensation the *Plokamos*.

This shows that the name survived technically while autonomous constellation status did not.

### Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*

**Documented astrological tradition.** In *Tetrabiblos* I.9, Ptolemy gives the cluster beneath the Bear’s tail—the cluster of Coma Berenices—the combined temperament of the **Moon and Venus**. This is the principal ancient astrological characterization.

No Coma star belongs to the later canonical set of fifteen **Behenian fixed stars**. Modern occult lists that imply otherwise are conflating Ptolemy’s general fixed-star qualities with the medieval Behenian talismanic canon.

### Late antiquity and the authors named in the brief

- **Aratus, *Phaenomena***: written before the Berenice episode, so it cannot name her lock. “Ungrouped, unnamed” stars near the region have been retrospectively connected with it, but that is not an attestation.
- **Scholia to Aratus**: later scholia do mention the Berenice tradition, but their dates and layers vary; they transmit post-Callimachean learning rather than an independent pre-245 BCE myth.
- **Germanicus, *Aratea***: no secure independent Berenice narrative was located in the poem itself. Later scholia and illustrated manuscript apparatus can import Hyginian material.
- **Ovid**: no extended independent catasterism of Berenice’s lock was found in the authentic works. General references to consecrated hair or Alexandrian queens should not be substituted for one.
- **Manilius, *Astronomica***: does not name Coma Berenices, despite its existence by his time.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus**: no Berenice II or Lock catasterism.
- **Pausanias**: no Lock narrative.
- **Nonnus**: no securely identified independent version of this catasterism.
- **Eratosthenes/Pseudo-Eratosthenes and Hyginus** are therefore the principal ancient mythographic witnesses; Callimachus and Catullus are the principal poetic witnesses.

Claims that all standard classical mythographers narrated the story are false. Most do not.

### Islamic Middle Ages

Al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* (964) retained Ptolemy’s organization. The stars remained outside the image of Leo, associated with its tail-region tuft rather than promoted to an independent queen’s-hair constellation.

Arabic terminology—*al-Hulbah*, *al-Dhuʾābah*, *al-Ḍafīrah*—translated or reinterpreted the tuft/lock shape. Al-Ṣūfī’s importance here is observational and transmissional; he did not originate the Berenice myth.

### Medieval Latin astronomy

Latin *Almagest* traditions rendered the group with phrases such as:

> *Convolutio nubilosa quae cincinnus vocatur*  
> “The cloudy convolution which is called the curl.”

Renaissance marginalia glossed Greek *plokamos* as Latin *cincinnus*, *caesaries*, or *coma virginis*, sometimes adding that this was perhaps Berenice’s lock celebrated by Callimachus.

This “perhaps” illustrates the humanist recovery process: a Ptolemaic technical phrase was reconnected with classical poetry.

### 1515: Dürer’s northern star map

Albrecht Dürer’s 1515 woodcut follows the classical Ptolemaic constellation system. It does **not** provide a fully autonomous pictorial Coma Berenices. Claims that Dürer depicted the later constellation should distinguish between stars in the region and a named figure.

### 1536: Caspar Vopel

**Documented cartographic innovation.** Vopel’s printed celestial globe of 1536 is the earliest presently documented graphic promotion of Coma Berenices into an independent pictured constellation. He placed a tiny personification or lock-like image among the stars.

Elly Dekker’s study shows that Vopel was inspired by humanist reading of Ptolemy’s catalogue and its glosses. Thus he did not invent the name or story; he transformed a named ancient asterism into a mapped constellation figure.

His 1536 globe influenced:

- Jacques de la Garde’s 1552 globe;
- Jean Naze’s 1560 globe;
- Christoph Schniepp’s globe around 1600;
- the Nicolai and Oterschaden globes around 1603.

### 1551: Gerard Mercator

Mercator’s celestial globe shows **Cincinnus**, though with only one star and two nebulous marks. Mercator is sometimes credited with inventing or first separating Coma, but Vopel’s 1536 globe precedes him.

### 1598–1602: Tycho Brahe

Tycho completed his larger catalogue in 1598. The shorter 777-star version appeared in the 1602 *Astronomiae Instauratae Progymnasmata*. He catalogued **Coma Berenices separately**, making the figure operationally independent in high-precision European astronomy.

Hence two statements must be distinguished:

- Vopel first securely **pictured** it independently in 1536.
- Tycho established it as a separately headed **catalogue constellation** in 1602.

### 1603: Johann Bayer

Bayer’s *Uranometria* received Coma as a distinct figure, spreading it through the most influential early-modern star atlas. The constellation’s modern Greek-letter history is more complicated than the simple popular statement “Bayer named α, β, γ”; later cataloguers standardized the letters.

### 1627: Julius Schiller

**Verified lead.** In the Christianized *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Schiller replaced Coma Berenices with the **Flagellum Christi Domini**, “the Scourge/Whip of Christ the Lord,” or pictorially the **Flagellation of Christ**.

The brief’s “Scourge of Christ?” is therefore substantially correct. More precisely, the stars symbolized the instrument or emblem of Christ’s scourging within Schiller’s Passion-related northern sky. Andreas Cellarius reproduced Schiller’s Christianized system in *Harmonia Macrocosmica* in 1660/61, but it never displaced the classical constellations.

### 1679–1690: Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius included Coma independently in the catalogue and plates published posthumously by Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius in 1690 as the *Prodromus Astronomiae* and *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia*. He shows the hair as a luxuriant detached tress.

### Flamsteed and the eighteenth century

John Flamsteed’s catalogue and posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* (1729) retained the figure and supplied the numbered stars now called Flamsteed designations. Jean-Baptiste Fortin’s reduced French edition of 1776, supervised in the Parisian astronomical milieu associated with Le Monnier and Messier, was an important basis for Bode’s 1782 popular atlas.

### 1782: Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne*

Bode titled it **“Das Haupthaar der Berenice.”** His atlas corrected and enlarged the reduced Flamsteed/Fortin tradition for educated amateurs. He emphasized that telescopes reveal the region to be rich in nebulae.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact.** Bode’s immense Berlin atlas—the plates about 103 by 67 cm—mapped more stars than earlier pictorial atlases and retained many historical and newly proposed constellations. On the plate containing Boötes, Canes Venatici, and the surrounding sky, **COMA BERENICES** appears as flowing hair.

Bode did not invent it and did not treat it as an experimental figure. By 1801 it belonged to the established European atlas canon.

### Nineteenth-century reception

- **Alexander Jamieson, *A Celestial Atlas* (London, 1822):** shows a long fall of hair viewed largely from behind.
- **Urania’s Mirror** (London, 1824/25), card 10: combines Boötes, Canes Venatici, Coma Berenices, and the now-obsolete Quadrans Muralis. The cards copy Jamieson closely and use perforations so stars shine when held to light.
- **Elijah H. Burritt, *Geography of the Heavens* (1833 and later):** describes the beautiful cluster between Cor Caroli and Denebola and repeats the Conon story in accessible American textbooks.
- **Argelander:** retained Coma as a practical constellation in the *Uranometria Nova* era, while reducing reliance on elaborate human figures.
- **Eduard Heis, *Atlas Coelestis Novus* (1872):** retained Coma and pictured the hair.
- Progressive nineteenth-century atlases increasingly privileged star coordinates and outlines over mythological engraving.

### 1922 and 1930: IAU standardization

At the IAU’s first General Assembly in Rome in 1922, the organization regularized 88 constellation names and three-letter abbreviations. Coma Berenices was retained as **Com**.

Delporte’s *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* (1930) supplied exact boundaries along hour circles and declination parallels for epoch B1875. The lines converted a historically elastic figure into a jurisdictional sky area.

## Myths and legends by culture

### The vow and celestial lock

**Legend/myth, first extant in Callimachus, about 245 BCE.** Berenice vowed her hair for Ptolemy III’s safe and victorious return from war. After his return she cut it and dedicated it at the coastal sanctuary of Arsinoe-Aphrodite at Zephyrium. The lock disappeared. Conon declared that Aphrodite had accepted and translated it into the heavens.

Callimachus makes the lock itself the narrator. Catullus preserves its reluctance, its journey on Zephyrus, its consecration by Venus, and its desire to return to Berenice.

### Which deity received it?

Callimachus/Catullus uses a complex Alexandrian syncretism:

- Aphrodite/Venus is the celestial agent.
- The sanctuary was dedicated to **Arsinoe Zephyritis**, the deified Arsinoe II assimilated to Aphrodite.
- Some popular retellings simplify this to “a temple of Venus.”
- Others say “the gods” because Catullus says Berenice promised the lock to all the gods before locating its dedication in the Arsinoe-Aphrodite cult.

These are differences of emphasis, not necessarily contradictory versions.

### Who took the hair?

- **Callimachean myth:** Zephyrus bears it into the sky; Aphrodite places it among the stars.
- **Hyginus:** it disappeared, and Conon claimed the unfigured stars represented it.
- **Later legend:** Jupiter took it.
- **Rationalizing tradition:** a priest stole it, the wind blew it away, or the disappearance was staged as court ritual.

No early source located in this research says a hostile Egyptian priest stole it because it was offered to a Greek deity. That colorful story is a **modern or late rationalizing embellishment** unless an earlier source can be produced.

### Berenice the reluctant bride and devoted wife

Catullus 66 stresses that she wept when her new husband left, not because she regretted marriage but because lovers hate separation. The poem also alludes to their wedding night and treats the vow as both conjugal devotion and dynastic public performance.

### Berenice the warrior

**Tradition preserved only through Hyginus 2.24.** When Berenice’s father supposedly fled before superior enemies, she mounted a horse, rallied the army, killed many foes, and drove the others away. Callimachus called her “great-souled.”

The exact historical event is uncertain. It may preserve court encomium connected with Berenice’s Cyrenean background rather than independent historiography.

### Berenice’s Olympic horses

Hyginus says some writers, together with Callimachus, claimed she raised horses and sent them to Olympia. Ptolemaic royal women did participate in prestigious equestrian victories, but Hyginus’s wording and the loss of his sources prevent a precise reconstruction of Berenice’s victories.

### Berenice and Lesbian dowries

Again through Hyginus, Eratosthenes reportedly said Berenice ordered dowries owed to maidens of Lesbos to be paid and instituted a claims process. The story portrays her as royal benefactor and lawgiver. Its original context is lost.

### Ariadne’s competing hair

Pseudo-Eratosthenes’ Corona tradition refers to **Ariadne’s hair**, while Leo’s stars are Berenice’s lock. Later writers have treated Ariadne as the group’s earlier owner.

**Disputed.** The overlap may result from epitomization or from a broader association between hair, crowns, and bridal apotheosis. It does not prove that Coma was a stable pre-Berenice constellation called Ariadne’s Hair.

### The comet version

Some later versions say the lock became a **hair-star**, that is, a comet. This depends on the ancient and early-modern metaphor of a comet as a long-haired star—Greek *komētēs*, Latin *stella crinita*. It is a verbal and iconographic variant, not the mainstream astronomical identification.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Arabic and Bedouin astronomy

The strongest Arabic names are the tuft/braid terms already listed. Al-Ṣūfī preserved the stars as unfigured stars of Leo.

The specific names are often called “Bedouin” in modern summaries, but the evidence is mixed: some are Arabic translations of Ptolemy rather than demonstrably independent desert asterisms. **Al-Ḍafīrah** is semantically transparent Arabic, but its exact pre-Islamic status is not established by the sources consulted.

### Chinese astronomy

The modern Western constellation cuts across several traditional Chinese asterisms, especially within the **Supreme Palace Enclosure**, 太微垣, *Tàiwēi Yuán*. These include stars assigned to:

- 太微左垣, *Tàiwēi Zuǒ Yuán*, Left Wall of the Supreme Palace;
- 郎位, *Lángwèi*, Seats/Positions of Court Gentlemen or Officers;
- 郎將, *Lángjiàng*, Captain of the Bodyguard;
- 五諸侯, *Wǔ Zhūhóu*, Five Feudal Lords;
- 幸臣, *Xìngchén*, Favored Courtier;
- 周鼎, *Zhōudǐng*, the Tripod of Zhou.

α Com forms the fifth star of the Supreme Palace Left Wall and represented the **First Eastern General**, 東上將, *Dōngshàngjiāng*. γ Com is associated with the first star of *Lángwèi*.

Modern Chinese **后髮座/后发座**, “the Queen’s Hair constellation,” is a translation of the Western constellation and must not be projected backward onto traditional Chinese astronomy.

### Indian astronomy

The IAU region lies north of the principal ecliptic belt, so it is not itself one of the classical 27/28 nakṣatras. The nearest mansion framework is that of **Hasta** and neighboring Virgo/Leo mansions, but no standard Sanskrit nakṣatra corresponds to Melotte 111.

The Malayalam name **Sītāvēṇi**, “Sita’s braid,” is reported in modern regional star-lore lists for Coma Berenices. Its antiquity and precise stellar membership require better primary ethnographic documentation; it should currently be labeled **regional tradition, incompletely verified**, not a pan-Indian Vedic identification.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic material

The modern Hebrew astronomical name is **שערות ברניקי**, “Berenice’s hair.”

No securely traceable ancient Jewish constellation occupying precisely these stars was found, nor a standard rabbinic identification of the group. Likewise, no established medieval Jewish Kabbalistic mapping of Coma Berenices was located.

Claims that Coma is the woman-and-child or “Desired One of the Nations” derive principally from nineteenth-century Christian “gospel in the stars” literature, especially Frances Rolleston and E. W. Bullinger. These are **modern Christian inventions**, based on speculative Hebrew etymologies and retrojection into Egyptian imagery, not evidence for an ancient Hebrew constellation.

### Christianized sky

Schiller’s **Flagellum Christi Domini**, the Scourge of Christ, is the historically documented Christian replacement. It is distinct from later attempts to interpret the hair as Veronica, Berenice/Beronice, or the Veil.

A later devotional association linked the names **Berenice/Beronice/Veronica** and the *vera icon*, suggesting that the saint who wiped Christ’s face was connected with the constellation. This is a word-driven devotional speculation, not Schiller’s identification and not an ancient constellation tradition.

### Hermetic and alchemical readings

No stable classical or medieval alchemical doctrine specifically centered on Coma Berenices was found. General Hermetic systems correlate hair, Venus, lunar moisture, royal femininity, or the north galactic region, but those associations are later applications.

The defensible historical astrological datum is Ptolemy’s **Moon–Venus** nature for the cluster. Modern claims that Coma signifies “the perfected alchemical bride,” “Isis’s severed lock,” or a Kabbalistic sephirah are **modern esoteric constructions** unless supported by a cited historical text.

### Lunar mansions and Behenian stars

- No Coma star belongs to the standard fifteen Behenian stars.
- Because the constellation lies well north of the ecliptic, no standard Arabic *manzil al-qamar* or Indian nakṣatra is defined by the Coma cluster.
- Some mansion sectors, extended as great lunes from the ecliptic, can geometrically cross the modern IAU territory. That is a modern geometrical overlay, not evidence that Coma itself was a mansion.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas

**Documented absence.** The Farnese Atlas, a Roman marble copy of a Hellenistic celestial globe, lacks Coma Berenices as an autonomous figure. It also lacks other late or noncanonical figures such as Antinous and Equuleus in reconstructions. This supports, but does not alone prove, its dependence on a constellation system predating or excluding the Hellenistic Lock.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts illustrate Leo twice—outside and inside views of a celestial globe. Coma’s stars occur as unfigured stars near the Lion rather than as a portrait of Berenice’s hair. Manuscripts vary in star placement and captions, so a particular folio must be dated and localized before using it as evidence.

### Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

The northern celestial map by Dürer, Stabius, and Heinfogel follows the classical system. The region is present, but an autonomous hair figure is not securely depicted.

### Vopel, Cologne, 1536

Vopel’s surviving printed globe gores show a small personification within the cluster. This is the decisive surviving iconographic change from a textual Ptolemaic tuft to an autonomous image.

### Mercator, 1551

Mercator renders **Cincinnus** minimally: one star and two nebulous marks. The restrained image remains close to Ptolemy’s description.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer incorporates Coma in *Uranometria* among the northern figures. The hair is visibly independent, cementing Vopel and Tycho’s innovation in atlas culture.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

Lucas Kilian’s engraving replaces the pagan/dynastic lock with Christ’s scourging emblem. Schiller maps the sky as seen from outside the celestial sphere, reversing the orientation familiar from many geocentric charts.

### Hevelius, Danzig, 1690

Published by Elisabeth Hevelius after Johannes’s death, the plate shows an elegant mass of flowing hair. Hevelius’s globe-view orientation also reverses familiar left/right relationships.

### Flamsteed, London, 1729

Flamsteed retains the figure in the standardized telescopic atlas tradition. The star numbering later gave the constellation its familiar Flamsteed numbers.

### Bode, Berlin, 1782 and 1801

Bode’s 1782 hair is a long, human-looking fall of tresses. The 1801 *Uranographia* enlarges and refines the figure amid dense telescopic star fields and nebular symbols. The contrast is characteristic of Bode: elaborate classical artwork overlaid by unusually rich positional data.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s woman’s hair, seen from behind, is directly ancestral to *Urania’s Mirror*.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/25

Card 10 shows Boötes, his hunting dogs, Coma’s hair, and Quadrans Muralis. Hand coloring varies by copy. The cards were engraved by Sidney Hall and published by Samuel Leigh. Although attributed to “a lady,” archival work points to the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam; possible participation by his wife remains speculative.

### Coins, buildings, ceilings, portals, and calendars

Ptolemaic coins and seals depict Berenice II, sometimes with abbreviated or styled hair and royal/divine attributes, but no securely identified ancient coin was found that depicts the seven-star Lock as a constellation.

Berenice and Ptolemy appear in Egyptian temple reliefs, including the Khonsu precinct at Karnak, in dynastic-divine contexts. These are not depictions of Coma Berenices.

No securely catalogued medieval cathedral portal or monumental ceiling was found in which Coma Berenices can be positively distinguished. Zodiac cycles commonly omit it because it is non-zodiacal and outside the 48 canonical Ptolemaic figures. Assertions that a vague tuft near Leo on a church ceiling is Coma require case-by-case iconographic proof.

## People, events, and works

### Historical persons

- **Berenice II of Cyrene**, queen and eventual divine dynastic figure.
- **Ptolemy III Euergetes**, whose Syrian campaign motivated the vow.
- **Conon of Samos**, court astronomer credited with the stellar identification.
- **Callimachus**, author of the Greek poem.
- **Catullus**, whose Latin adaptation preserved most of it.
- **Caspar Vopel**, first securely documented independent visual cartographer.
- **Tycho Brahe**, first major catalogue authority to list it independently.
- **Johann Bayer, Hevelius, Flamsteed, Bode, Jamieson, and Delporte**, successive agents in its cartographic standardization.
- **P. J. Melotte and R. J. Trumpler**, classifiers of the physical open cluster.
- **Fritz Zwicky**, whose Coma Cluster calculation inaugurated the modern extragalactic dark-matter problem.

### Literature

#### Catullus 66

The foundational Latin literary afterlife and principal surviving witness to Callimachus.

#### Alexander Pope, *The Rape of the Lock*

Pope’s mock-epic title and apotheosis of Belinda’s lock deliberately echo the Berenice tradition. The poem ends with the lock translated into the skies, while Pope explicitly contrasts it with Berenice’s:

> “Not Berenice’s locks first rose so bright…”

The first version appeared in 1712 and the enlarged five-canto version in 1714.

#### Ugo Foscolo, *La chioma di Berenice* (1803)

Foscolo published an Italian treatment of Catullus/Callimachus with critical and literary apparatus, contributing to the Lock’s Romantic and neoclassical reception.

#### William Cullen Bryant, “The Constellations”

Bryant refers to the “streaming tresses of the Egyptian queen,” an example of the constellation in nineteenth-century English-language celestial poetry.

#### Gunnar Ekelöf

The Swedish poet invoked Berenice’s hanging hair together with a comet and the Leonids in a 1933 poem, demonstrating the continuing play between hair, comets, and meteor imagery.

#### Eudora Welty

Welty used **“The Bride of the Innisfallen”** and other celestial allusions, but an often-assumed major Welty work titled *Coma Berenices* could not be substantiated. This is included as an example of a plausible literary attribution that should not be repeated without a bibliographic citation.

### Music and visual art

Numerous modern compositions, albums, paintings, and installations use “Coma Berenices” or “Berenice’s Hair,” but no single work has become canonical enough to justify treating it as part of the ancient tradition. Such uses usually evoke femininity, sacrifice, separation, starry dispersal, or the galaxy cluster.

### Navigation and exploration

Coma contains no first-magnitude navigational star and does not appear among the standard bright-star navigation sets. No well-documented voyage was found that depended specifically on α, β, or γ Com.

Its practical naked-eye roles were more often:

- locating the spring sky between Leo, Boötes, and Canes Venatici;
- judging transparency because the cluster disappears under haze or moonlight;
- guiding telescopic sweeps through the Virgo/Coma galaxy fields.

Claims that ancient explorers “steered by Berenice’s Hair” are therefore unverified and intrinsically less plausible than navigation by Polaris, Arcturus, or stars of Ursa Major.

### Scientific legacy bearing its name

- Coma Berenices open cluster/Melotte 111
- Coma Cluster/Abell 1656
- Coma Supercluster
- Coma Wall/Great Wall terminology
- Coma Berenices dwarf galaxy
- FK Comae Berenices variables
- Comae Berenicid meteor showers

These names refer to sky location; the physical objects have no necessary relationship to one another.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Who “invented” Coma Berenices?

- **Conon, about 245 BCE:** named or identified the asterism.
- **Callimachus:** gave it its surviving poetic catasterism.
- **Vopel, 1536:** first securely documented independent graphic constellation.
- **Tycho, 1602:** first major separate catalogue heading.
- **Mercator, 1551:** influential but later than Vopel.
- **Bayer, 1603:** popularized it in the first great printed star atlas, but did not invent it.

The dispute disappears once “named asterism,” “pictured constellation,” and “catalogued constellation” are distinguished.

### 2. Was it an ancient constellation?

It was an **ancient named asterism**, but not one of Ptolemy’s 48 autonomous constellations. Calling it simply an “ancient constellation” is understandable but imprecise.

### 3. Ptolemy’s “nebula”

Ptolemy’s “nebulous mass” is Melotte 111 seen as unresolved or semi-resolved starlight, not an emission nebula and not the Coma Cluster of galaxies. Translation as “nebula” can mislead modern readers.

### 4. Berenice’s relationship to Ptolemy III

Hyginus makes her his sister and gives her Ptolemaic parentage. Historically she was the daughter of Magas of Cyrene and Apama and was Ptolemy III’s cousin. Later mythographers copied Hyginus’s error.

### 5. When was the hair offered?

Catullus’s vow is conditional upon safe return, so fulfillment follows Ptolemy’s return. Some reconstructions place the cutting or public ceremony during the campaign or treat the sources’ chronology loosely. Exact months in 245 BCE are scholarly reconstruction, not explicit ancient dating.

### 6. Theft, wind, or staged disappearance

The earliest poetry tells of divine transport; Hyginus simply says the hair was missing. Theft by priests, removal by a court agent, wind, and planned ritual disappearance are rationalizations. None is demonstrable.

### 7. “Seven stars”

Hyginus specifies seven stars in a triangle. Ptolemy catalogues a more complicated combination of individual stars and a cloudy mass. Modern Melotte 111 has dozens of members visible optically. Therefore “the constellation consists of seven stars” is a mythographic convention, not a modern astronomical count.

### 8. Ariadne’s Hair

The Pseudo-Eratosthenic evidence is genuine but difficult. It may preserve an alternate name, an association with Corona Borealis, or textual contamination. “Coma was originally Ariadne’s Hair” is stronger than the evidence permits.

### 9. Canopus called Berenice’s Hair

**Documented mistranslation and resulting error.** Pliny wrote that Italy sees neither Canopus nor “the one they call Berenice’s Hair.” Philemon Holland’s 1601 English translation made the syntax imply Canopus itself was also called Berenice’s Hair. Later writers inferred a second southern Coma.

The Latin does not support this. Canopus and Berenice’s Hair are two separate difficult-to-see objects in Pliny’s sentence.

### 10. Diadem and the Arabic names

Diadem’s royal symbolism is clear, but its historical origin is obscure. *Al Dafirah* belonged to the braid/asterism and was later shifted to γ or α Com. Treating every historical group-name as an ancient proper name for one IAU star produces false precision.

### 11. Kissin and the ivy leaf

*Phyllon Kissinou* describes the group as an ivy leaf. “Kissin” subsequently migrated among 23 Com, 21 Com, and other stars through catalogue copying. It is not a securely ancient personal star name.

### 12. Bayer-letter expectations

β is brighter than α. This is not evidence that a star’s brightness changed dramatically; Bayer-style letters do not always encode a strict magnitude ranking, and Coma’s letters were regularized through a complicated post-Bayer catalogue history.

### 13. M91, the “missing Messier object”

Messier’s position was long thought irrecoverable. NGC 4548 is now the generally accepted identity after recomputing Messier’s offset from M89, but older books identify other galaxies. This is a catalogue-coordinate controversy, not a vanished object.

### 14. M64’s discoverer

Edward Pigott, Bode, and Messier independently found M64. Older accounts often credited Bode or Messier because Pigott’s priority was overlooked. NASA’s historical summary gives Pigott’s observation first, followed twelve days later by Bode.

### 15. Coma and the discovery of dark matter

Zwicky’s 1933 result is sometimes called “the discovery of dark matter.” More precisely, it was an early quantitative inference of missing gravitating matter in a galaxy cluster. Earlier stellar-dynamics discussions used “dark matter” or invisible matter in other senses, and the modern particle concept developed later.

### 16. Schiller’s Scourge

Verified, but “Coma became the Flagellation of Christ” and “Coma became Christ’s whip” are slightly different. Schiller’s Latin title **Flagellum Christi Domini** names the scourge/instrument; pictorial and secondary descriptions may call the scene or theme the Flagellation.

### 17. Egyptian woman-and-child claims

No adequate evidence connects a Dendera woman-and-child figure to Coma Berenices. The claim is characteristic of nineteenth-century biblical-constellation schemes and internet repetition.

### 18. Hebrew and Kabbalistic antiquity

No primary ancient Hebrew or medieval Kabbalistic source was found for “Berenice’s Hair,” “the Desired One,” or a messianic mother-and-child in precisely these stars. Modern Hebrew uses a translation of the Western name.

### 19. Hoaxes and commercial star names

Commercially assigned names within Coma are not recognized by the IAU. The IAU approves traditional or culturally researched proper names for stars; it does not sell names. No constellation-specific historical hoax comparable to the modern star-selling business was found.

## Open questions

1. **Conon’s original medium.** Did he publish the identification in a written work, demonstrate it on a globe, announce it ceremonially, or merely supply astronomical authority for Callimachus? No original work survives.

2. **The actual disappearance.** Whether the hair was stolen, blown away, ritually removed, or never materially deposited cannot be recovered.

3. **Callimachus’s editions.** Papyrus discoveries confirm parts of the Greek poem but leave unresolved whether there was an independent court-performance version followed by a revised *Aetia* version.

4. **Ariadne’s Hair.** The relationship among Ariadne’s crown, her hair, and Berenice’s lock in the Pseudo-Eratosthenic text remains philologically difficult.

5. **Babylonian placement.** Proposed identifications of ḪÉ.GÁL or Erua’s palm-frond with Coma require improved reconstruction of Babylonian star geography.

6. **Egyptian star clocks.** The identification of *sbꜣw ꜥšꜣw*, “many stars,” with Melotte 111 is plausible but not decisive.

7. **Arabic indigenous versus translated names.** More manuscript comparison is required to distinguish independent Bedouin lore from Arabic translations of Ptolemy’s *Plokamos*.

8. **Early letter designations.** The precise printed path by which α, β, and γ became fixed for Coma deserves direct collation of Bayer, Tycho, Flamsteed, Baily, and nineteenth-century catalogues.

9. **Bode’s accompanying description.** The atlas scan securely establishes the map and labels, while accessible OCR for all passages in the accompanying German/French explanatory volume remains imperfect. A diplomatic transcription should be made from the physical pages before quoting more than the verified heading and general description.

10. **“830 stars in 109 groups.”** No consulted Bode catalogue or atlas description substantiated this number. Identification of the referenced “Bode collection on chain” is needed before it can be evaluated.
