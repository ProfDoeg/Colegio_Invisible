# Equuleus: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact** — directly attested by a primary text, catalogue, surviving atlas, institutional record, or modern astronomical database.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference by modern historians from positional, textual, or iconographic evidence.
- **Tradition** — a claim repeated in historical or modern reference literature but not necessarily traceable to the constellation’s origin.
- **Disputed claim** — supported and challenged in scholarship, or resting on ambiguous testimony.
- **Myth** — an ancient narrative; “myth” describes its literary category, not its truth value.
- **Legend** — later folklore or a retrospective story lacking an ancient constellation text.
- **Modern invention** — a recent name, association, or interpretation.

## Basic identification

### Status

**Documented fact. Equuleus was kept by the International Astronomical Union.** It is one of the 88 official constellations, with the abbreviation **Equ** and genitive **Equulei**. It was included in the IAU’s canonical list at Rome in 1922. Eugène Delporte’s rectilinear boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930.

It therefore was **not abolished**, and its stars did not pass wholesale to another constellation. Its modern territory borders only:

- Aquarius
- Delphinus
- Pegasus

The constellation occupies approximately **72 square degrees**, making it the **second-smallest IAU constellation**, after Crux, and the smallest in the northern celestial hemisphere. Its brightest star, α Equulei, is only about visual magnitude 3.9; no Equuleus star is brighter than fourth magnitude. The official IAU account gives **Equuleus — Equ — “the Little Horse” — Equulei** and supplies its modern boundary file. [IAU constellation list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### What the figure is

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy did not call it a “foal” in Greek. In the *Almagest* star catalogue, book VII–VIII, constellation 18 of the northern group, the heading is:

> Ἵππου προτομή — *Híppou protomḗ*

Literally, this is the **forepart, bust, or frontal portion of a horse**. *Protomē* can denote a head-and-chest representation, familiar from sculptural busts and animal protomes. Ptolemy described four faint stars:

1. the preceding of the two in the head — α Equulei;
2. the following of the two in the head — β Equulei;
3. the preceding of the two in the mouth — γ Equulei;
4. the following of the two in the mouth — δ Equulei.

His conclusion is *asteres d amauroi*, “four faint stars.” A modern transcription and identification of the Greek catalogue is available through the [Ptolemaeus Arabus et Latinus edition](https://ptolemaeus.badw.de/edition/1/19/text/1); the four-star mapping is conveniently tabulated in the [All Skies Encyclopaedia entry](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Equuleus).

### Bode’s Equuleus

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode did not invent Equuleus. In *Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio* (Berlin, 1801), it occupies **plate X**, “Pegasus, Delphinus, Equuleus.” The original sheet is about 66 × 100 cm. Bode shows a compact horse’s head and forequarters immediately east of Delphinus and west of Pegasus. The engraving labels it in Latin **EQUULEUS**.

The figure is therefore one of the ancient constellations in Bode’s atlas, not one of his experimental modern creations. His atlas contained twenty enormous copperplates and a catalogue of 17,240 stars; the surviving ETH copy describes it as twenty copperplates, while Bode’s accompanying catalogue supplied the textual and positional apparatus. [ETH digital copy](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [NYPL plate record](https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/510d47dc-8dc2-a3d9-e040-e00a18064a99); [plate index](https://nzdr.ru/data/media/biblio/edu/astro/%21%D0%90%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%8F/1/John%20Chiravalle%2C%20Pattern%20Asterisms-%20Springer%20%282006%29%28ISBN%29%28PDF%20%28scan%29%29%20English.pdf)

The prompt’s description of the “Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” appears to be **collection-platform metadata**, not a statistic from Bode’s *Uranographia*. Bode’s atlas and accompanying catalogue contain far more than 830 stars. I found no passage in the 1801 work describing Equuleus as “830 stars in 109 groups.”

## Stars and objects

## The classical four and the modern figure

The naked-eye pattern is normally drawn from α, β, γ, and δ Equulei, sometimes with ε Equulei. Ptolemy’s four formed two pairs: head and mouth. Bayer preserved essentially this quartet.

| Star | Flamsteed | V mag., approximately | Historical/modern note |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| α Equulei | 8 Equ | 3.92 | Kitalpha; brightest star; spectroscopic binary. |
| δ Equulei | 7 Equ | 4.47 | Close binary; formally Tepennekhet since 2026. |
| γ Equulei | 5 Equ | 4.7 variable | Magnetic chemically peculiar, rapidly oscillating Ap star. |
| β Equulei | 10 Equ | 5.16 | A-type main-sequence star; despite β, not second brightest. |
| ε Equulei | 1 Equ | about 5.3 | Multiple system, commonly included in modern line figures. |
| η Equulei | 9 Equ | about 5.8 | Red giant; Bode-era letter survives in some catalogues. |
| λ Equulei | 2 Equ | about 6.7 | Faint lettered star; the letter remains occasionally used. |
| 3 Equulei | 3 Equ | about 5.6 | Orange giant. |
| 4 Equulei | 4 Equ | about 5.9 | F-type dwarf, double. |
| 6 Equulei | 6 Equ | about 6.1 | Formally named Protome in 2026. |

Bayer’s *Uranometria* (1603) assigned **α through δ**. Bode extended Greek lettering beyond Bayer’s quartet. Modern usage retained ε securely and occasionally η and λ, while several of Bode’s additional letters became obsolete or inconsistent. The Flamsteed sequence 1–10 is more stable for the ten traditional numbered stars.

### Kitalpha — α/8 Equulei

**Documented etymology.** *Kitalpha* is a contraction or Latinized corruption of Arabic **قطعة الفرس, qiṭʿat al-faras**, “the piece/section of the horse.” The Arabic expression originally named the entire constellation, not specifically α. European star-name catalogues later transferred it to the brightest star.

The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Kitalpha** for α Equulei A on 21 August 2016. [IAU star-naming history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/naming_stars/); [Kitalpha etymology](https://www.star-facts.com/kitalpha-alpha-equulei/)

**Contested/malformed forms.** *Kitalphar*, *Kital Phard*, and especially Elijah Burritt’s **Kitel Phard** are corrupt expansions, not separate Arabic names. Allen explicitly calls Burritt’s version a strange transformation. Riccioli’s **Elmac Alcheras** is another garbled learned form whose exact Arabic pathway is not recoverable.

### Tepennekhet — δ/7 Equulei

**Modern official name based on reconstructed Egyptian heritage.** In July 2026 the IAU adopted **Tepennekhet** for δ Equulei A. The name means “Head of the Giant” and refers to a body part of the Egyptian constellation **Nekhet**, reconstructed from Ramesside star-clock material.

This does **not** demonstrate that Egyptians called the four-star Equuleus figure a horse, nor that δ Equulei can be identified beyond dispute in every Egyptian diagram. It is a modern heritage name selected by the IAU from an Egyptian sky tradition overlapping the present Equuleus territory. The WGSN specifically avoided naming a single star simply *Nekhet*, because Nekhet was a much larger figure. [IAU 2026 announcement](https://www.iau.org/IAU/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx); [Tepennekhet dossier](https://ase.exopla.net/index.php/Tepennekhet)

### Protome — 6 Equulei

**Modern official name drawn from Greek terminology.** Also in July 2026, the IAU adopted **Protome** for 6 Equulei A. It deliberately preserves the ancient constellation word *protomē*, “bust/forepart.” It is not an ancient proper name of that particular star: the transfer from constellation title to stellar name is a modern WGSN act. [Protome history](https://ase.exopla.net/index.php/Protome); [IAU catalogue presentation](https://exopla.net/star-names/modern-iau-star-names/)

### γ Equulei

**Documented astronomical fact.** γ Equ is a magnetic, strontium–chromium–europium peculiar A star and a rapidly oscillating Ap variable. Its visual light change is small; its rapid pulsations are measured on a scale of minutes, while magnetic/rotational variation occurs over a much longer and still difficult period. It has visual companions, but not all apparent components are necessarily gravitationally bound.

Older popular summaries often give “12½ minutes” as though that were a single stable cycle. In reality, roAp stars show multiple pulsational frequencies, so that number is an observational shorthand, not the whole variability.

### δ Equulei

**Documented astronomical fact.** δ Equ is a nearby close visual and spectroscopic binary with an orbital period of about 5.7 years. It was historically notable because its short visual-binary period permitted relatively rapid determination of a complete orbit. Claims that it has “the shortest known binary period” are obsolete unless explicitly limited to a historical class and date.

### ε Equulei

**Documented astronomical fact.** ε/1 Equulei is a multiple system. Descriptions oscillate between “triple” and “quadruple” because they count different resolved or physical components. This is a classification issue, not evidence that the star’s identity changed.

### Variables, novae, and supernovae

**Documented fact.** γ Equ is the constellation’s best-known named variable. Modern catalogues contain numerous fainter variables in the IAU area, including eclipsing, pulsating, rotational, and long-period objects. Catalogue counts change as surveys add faint sources; the statement sometimes repeated that Equuleus has “about 25 variables” is now badly dated.

**Negative finding.** I found no securely documented historical naked-eye nova or Galactic supernova assigned to Equuleus, and none of the canonical historical supernovae lies inside its modern boundary.

Modern extragalactic supernovae have inevitably been recorded in its background galaxies, but these transient survey designations do not amount to an “Equuleus supernova” in the traditional historical sense. A definitive current enumeration would require a live query against the Transient Name Server, whose contents change continuously.

### Exoplanet systems

Notable hosts include:

- **HD 200964**, a subgiant with a multi-planet system whose orbital solution has prompted dynamical studies;
- **HAT-P-65**, host of a transiting hot Jupiter;
- **WASP-90**, likewise a transiting-planet host.

These stars belong to Equuleus only by their position inside the Delporte boundary; they play no role in the traditional horse figure.

## Deep-sky objects

Equuleus lies away from the main Milky Way band and contains no conspicuous Galactic nebula or open cluster.

### Messier objects

**Documented fact:** **none**.

### Principal NGC and IC objects

- **NGC 7015** — faint spiral galaxy, the constellation’s most frequently recommended deep-sky target.
- **NGC 7040** — faint galaxy.
- **NGC 7046** — faint spiral galaxy.
- **NGC 7045** — not a true nebula or galaxy; a multiple star recorded by John Herschel as nebulous. This is a useful historical misidentification.
- **IC 1360**
- **IC 1361**
- **IC 1364**
- **IC 1365** — interacting or grouped galaxies, often split into NED components.
- **IC 1367**

A modern catalogue compilation lists fifteen NGC/IC entries or components in the boundary, nearly all faint galaxies. [Deep-sky list](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/equuleus-deepsky-objects)

No major nebula, cluster, or galaxy has an established proper name “Equuleus Nebula,” “Equuleus Cluster,” or “Equuleus Galaxy.” Such phrases encountered in commercial star-map material are descriptive, not recognized object names.

## Meteor showers

Older references correctly stated that Equuleus had no established shower. That became outdated in 2025.

**Documented provisional discovery.** Global Meteor Network data revealed a weak shower active roughly 15–30 June 2025, with 46 selected meteors and a radiant near RA 319.4°, Dec +9°, inside Equuleus. It was entered in the IAU Meteor Data Center working list as **M2025-O1**, subsequently called the **delta-Equuleids**, code **DQU**, with geocentric speed about 56.9 km/s.

Its status must be phrased carefully: it is a newly detected, weak working-list shower, not yet comparable in security or visibility to the Perseids or Geminids. [Vida, Šegon and Roggemans, “New meteor shower in Equuleus”](https://www.emeteornews.net/2025/08/27/new-meteor-shower-in-equuleus/); [paper PDF](https://www.emeteornews.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/eMetN2025_5.pdf)

Commercial pages using names such as “beta-Equuleids” without a traceable IAU MDC identification should not be treated as authoritative.

## Boundary history

### Before formal boundaries

Ancient and early-modern constellations were figures and catalogue headings, not mutually exhaustive sky polygons. Asking for a Ptolemaic “boundary” therefore risks anachronism.

### Ptolemy, ca. 150 CE

Ptolemy assigned exactly four faint stars to the formed figure. The territory between those stars and neighboring Delphinus, Pegasus, and Aquarius was not divided by plotted legal boundaries. His four are conventionally identified with α, β, γ, and δ Equ.

The fact that both Equuleus and Pegasus were incomplete horses caused later terminological awkwardness: Equuleus was explicitly a *protomē*, while Pegasus too was normally depicted only from the forequarters forward.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* retained the four-star core and assigned α–δ. The figure remained a head and short forepart rather than a full horse. Bayer’s letter ordering was not a strict brightness sequence, which explains why β Equulei is fainter than γ and δ.

### Flamsteed, 1729

Flamsteed’s *Atlas Coelestis* retained Equuleus alongside Pegasus and Delphinus and numbered stars in right-ascension order. The canonical Equuleus sequence became 1–10 Equulei.

### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius retained the classical figure in *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*, plate “Sagitta, Delphinus et Equuleus.” His charts were presented as an external celestial globe view and are consequently mirror-reversed relative to direct sky maps. He did not abolish or materially enlarge Equuleus.

### Lacaille

Lacaille did not reinvent Equuleus. His significance lies primarily in the southern sky and in systematic stellar lettering. Claims that Equuleus was a Lacaille constellation confuse it with **Equuleus Pictorius**, Lacaille’s “painter’s easel,” now Pictor.

### Bode, 1782 and 1801

Bode’s 1782 *Vorstellung der Gestirne* was a German revision and expansion of Jean Fortin’s reduced Flamsteed atlas. In 1801, Bode’s much larger *Uranographia* placed Equuleus on plate X with Pegasus and Delphinus and supplied more faint stars and Greek letters than Bayer.

Bode drew figure outlines across an increasingly dense stellar field, but still did not create exclusive legal borders. Apparent “taking” of stars from neighbors on such plates can reflect where the engraver laid an animal outline, not a formal catalogue reassignment.

### Nineteenth century: Argelander and Heis

Friedrich Wilhelm Argelander and Eduard Heis increasingly subordinated pictorial monsters and animals to practical line figures and catalogued star positions. Equuleus survived because it was Ptolemaic and already embedded in Bayer–Flamsteed nomenclature, despite its faintness. It did not depend on continued acceptance of Bode’s decorative form.

### IAU and Delporte

The IAU standardized three-letter abbreviations in 1922. Delporte subsequently drew borders along right ascension and declination for epoch B1875.0, attempting to preserve established Bayer, Flamsteed, and known variable-star assignments. The boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in 1930.

This transformed “Equuleus” from a horse-shaped star group into an exact celestial area. Precession makes the transformed J2000 coordinate segments appear slightly oblique even though Delporte’s original B1875 segments followed hour circles and parallels.

**Neighbor exchange.** No famous Bayer or Flamsteed Equuleus star was transferred away. The modern polygon chiefly formalized ambiguous empty space formerly shared pictorially with Aquarius, Delphinus, and Pegasus. Delporte’s guiding principle was to avoid moving established lettered, numbered, or known variable stars. [IAU history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [1931 review of Delporte](https://www.nature.com/articles/128690a0)

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the first century BCE: no secure Equuleus

**Negative evidence.** Equuleus is absent from Aratus’ *Phaenomena* (early third century BCE), which versified the sphere of Eudoxus. It is also absent as an independent entry from the surviving *Catasterismi* attributed to Eratosthenes.

No secure Babylonian or Egyptian small-horse predecessor has been identified in precisely these four stars.

### First century BCE: Geminus

**Earliest secure literary attestation.** Geminus’ *Introduction to the Phenomena* includes the “Forepart of the Horse” and attributes the addition to Hipparchus. The work is usually dated to the first century BCE.

The exact implication of Geminus’ wording is debated: it may mean that Hipparchus introduced the constellation, or merely that the constellation was recognized in Hipparchan astronomy. The surviving *Commentary on Aratus and Eudoxus* by Hipparchus does not list Equuleus as a normal independent figure.

### First century BCE–first century CE: Roman silence

Vitruvius, Manilius, and the extant Aratean tradition do not give Equuleus an independent mythic dossier. This is significant because these authors readily discuss better-established constellations.

### ca. 150 CE: Ptolemy

**Secure canonical establishment.** Ptolemy catalogued the four faint stars as constellation 18, *Hippou protomē*. From this point the figure entered the standard scientific catalogue tradition.

### Late antiquity

Late Greek and Latin epitomes transmitted the constellation primarily as a positional object, with little or no mythology. The lack of an early catasterism created the vacuum later filled by borrowing myths from Pegasus and from famous mythological horses.

### Ninth century: Carolingian manuscripts

The **Leiden Aratea**, made around 816, belongs to the Aratean manuscript tradition. Because Equuleus was absent from Aratus’ poem, its appearance in derivative diagrams or appended star lists is not evidence for an Aratean myth; it reflects the accommodation of post-Aratean catalogue material.

### ca. 964: al-Ṣūfī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita* retained the Ptolemaic constellation as **Ṣūrat Qiṭʿat al-Faras**, the figure of the horse’s section. Al-Ṣūfī’s manuscript tradition normally portrays each constellation twice: as seen on a celestial globe and as seen in the sky, producing mirrored images.

Al-Ṣūfī says, in effect, that four faint stars form a quadrilateral resembling the horse’s face or forepart. He did not supply a classical catasterism for it. [Al-Ṣūfī constellation list](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_constellations.htm); [Hafez study](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/28854/14/JCU_28854_Hafez_2010_thesis.pdf)

### Thirteenth–fifteenth centuries

Islamicate globes and manuscripts continued to depict *Qiṭʿat al-Faras*. Muʾayyad al-ʿUrḍī’s thirteenth-century globe tradition shows a horse bust. Latin Almagest translations generated such expressions as *Sectio Equi* and *Praecisio Equi*.

### 1515: Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s paired celestial woodcuts, prepared with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, are landmark printed maps. Equuleus appears as a small horse head/forepart in the northern map tradition. It should not be sought on the southern sheet catalogued separately by the Louvre. [Louvre Dürer record](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl020580218)

### 1551: Mercator

Gerardus Mercator’s celestial globe preserved Equuleus as a compact horse protome. It sits between the Dolphin and the greater Horse, reinforcing the spatial relationship familiar from Ptolemy.

### 1603: Bayer

Bayer’s *Uranometria* gave the constellation its durable α–δ scheme. This is the point at which the four stars acquired the Greek-letter designations still used.

### 1627: Schiller

Julius Schiller replaced the pagan horse with a Christian emblem, discussed below.

### 1675: Sherburne

Edward Sherburne’s annotated English Manilius lists Equuleus and records Schiller’s replacement as *Mystica Rosa*. [Sherburne digital text](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A51768.0001.001/1:10?rgn=div1&view=fulltext)

### 1690: Hevelius

Hevelius retained the horse’s head and forequarters in *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*.

### 1729–1776: Flamsteed and Fortin

Flamsteed’s posthumous atlas and Fortin’s reduced French edition show *Le Petit Cheval* between Pegasus and Delphinus.

### 1782: Bode’s *Vorstellung*

Bode incorporated it into his expanded German adaptation of Fortin/Flamsteed.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

The large plate X is the culmination of the decorative scientific atlas tradition: dense stellar plotting beneath a classically modeled horse protome. [NYPL plate](https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/510d47dc-8dc2-a3d9-e040-e00a18064a99)

### 1822–1825: Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* portrayed Equuleus with Pegasus. The anonymous 1824/1825 cards *Urania’s Mirror*, engraved by Sidney Hall and now attributed to Richard Rouse Bloxam, copied Jamieson closely. Card 15 is “Pegasus and Equuleus.” [Library of Congress card](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2002695508/); [RAS account](https://ec2-18-175-4-31.eu-west-2.compute.amazonaws.com/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror)

### 1922–1930: official standardization

The IAU retained Equuleus and standardized **Equ**. Delporte’s boundary converted it into a 72-square-degree region.

### 2016

The IAU formally approved **Kitalpha** for α Equulei A.

### 2022–2023

The spacecraft **EQUULEUS** launched aboard Artemis I and completed a planned lunar flyby before communications ceased in May 2023.

### 2025

The weak meteor shower M2025-O1/delta-Equuleids was detected and added to the working list.

### 2026

The IAU adopted **Protome** for 6 Equulei A and **Tepennekhet** for δ Equulei A, expanding the constellation’s official proper-name inventory from one star to three.

## Classical myths and their transmission

## The central finding

**Documented negative evidence:** there is no surviving classical catasterism written specifically for Equuleus.

- Aratus does not mention it.
- Pseudo-Eratosthenes has no Equuleus chapter.
- Hyginus’ *Astronomica* has no separate Equuleus chapter.
- Manilius does not develop it.
- Ovid supplies no Equuleus catasterism.
- Germanicus’ Aratean poem cannot independently explain a constellation absent from Aratus.
- Pseudo-Apollodorus and Pausanias provide horse myths but do not identify this four-star figure with one of them.
- Nonnus contains many equine transformations and Dionysiac horses but no secure Equuleus identification.

Consequently, modern lists of “Equuleus myths” are collections of **later candidate identities**, usually borrowed from Hyginus’ Pegasus chapter or from unrelated horse traditions.

### Celeris, brother or offspring of Pegasus

**Ancient myth; later Equuleus attribution. First surviving astronomical appearance: Pseudo-Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.18, probably first–second century CE.**

In the chapter **Equus**—the large Horse, Pegasus—Hyginus reports a version in which Mercury gave Castor a horse called **Celeris**, whose name means “swift.” Celeris was said to be either Pegasus’ brother or offspring, with both horses sprung from Medusa’s blood.

This source attaches the story to **Pegasus/Equus**, not to Equuleus. The transfer to the Little Horse is a later interpretive move, encouraged by the need to find a second mythological horse for a second horse constellation.

- **Documented:** Hyginus preserves Celeris in the Horse chapter.
- **Tradition:** Celeris represents Equuleus.
- **Not documented in antiquity:** that Zeus, Mercury, Castor, or another god placed Celeris specifically among the four Equuleus stars.
- **Etymology:** Latin *celer*, “swift”; *Celeris* is “the swift one.”

The statement “Celeris, brother of Pegasus, was given by Mercury to Castor” is therefore genuine ancient mythic material but not a genuine ancient Equuleus catasterism.

### Hippe/Melanippe, daughter of Chiron

**Ancient myth; constellation originally the large Horse. First surviving catasterismic form: Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 18; repeated by Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.18.**

Hippe—also called Melanippe or Euippe—was Chiron’s daughter. Aeolus seduced her; when she became pregnant she hid, fearing her father’s discovery. In variants, she prayed to the gods or Artemis transformed her into a mare. She was then placed among the stars where Chiron could not see her, or where her hindquarters remained concealed.

Pseudo-Eratosthenes cites Euripides’ lost **Melanippe** drama as part of the narrative background. Hyginus retells it under **Equus/Pegasus**.

Again, this is not originally Equuleus. The ancient figure concerned is the large Horse, conventionally Pegasus, whose incomplete body could explain Hippe’s concealment. Later popular astronomy reassigned Hippe to Equuleus because:

1. Equuleus needed a myth;
2. Hippe was female and equine;
3. the small protome could be understood as a transformed mare or her foal;
4. Chiron’s daughter provided a convenient link to another constellation.

The variant sometimes saying that the **foal born to Hippe** is Equuleus is even later and lacks a cited classical source.

### Cyllarus/Cylarus

**Ancient horse name; later Equuleus attribution; attribution unsupported by the ancient constellation texts.**

Cyllarus appears in classical literature in more than one equine context:

- Virgil praises a horse named Cyllarus in *Georgics* 3.89–94.
- Pollux’s famous horse is called Cyllarus in later mythographic and literary tradition.
- Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 12.393–428 describes **Cyllarus the centaur**, beloved of Hylonome, who dies in the battle with the Lapiths.

These figures should not be conflated automatically. The centaur Cyllarus is certainly not a little horse placed in the sky. The idea that Equuleus is **Cyllarus, Pollux’s horse given by Juno/Hera**, appears in modern star-lore compilations, but I could not trace it to Pseudo-Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Ovid, Pseudo-Apollodorus, Pausanias, or the ancient Aratean scholia as a catasterism.

- **Documented:** Cyllarus is an ancient equine/centaur name.
- **Tradition:** Equuleus represents Pollux’s horse.
- **Disputed/unsupported:** that Hera gave this horse to Pollux and it became Equuleus.
- **Popular error:** identifying Ovid’s centaur Cyllarus with the constellation without qualification.

### Poseidon’s first horse

**Ancient mythic complex; late attribution to Equuleus.**

Poseidon was strongly associated with horses and bore the epithet Hippios. Several ancient traditions credit him with producing the first horse:

- in the contest with Athena for Attica, he struck the Acropolis and produced the salt spring; later retellings sometimes substitute or add a horse;
- other accounts place the creation of the horse in Thessaly;
- Poseidon fathers extraordinary horses including Pegasus in some genealogical schemes and Arion in others.

Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 1.24.3 and 1.26.5, describes the Athenian contest and the marks of Poseidon’s trident, but does not say that Equuleus is the resulting horse. Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 3.14.1, likewise gives the contest without an Equuleus catasterism.

The claim that Equuleus is “the first horse struck from the earth by Neptune’s trident” is a Renaissance or modern mythographic identification, not a surviving Greek astronomical story.

### Philyra and Cronus/Saturn

**Ancient transformation myth; later loose association.**

Cronus, surprised while coupling with the Oceanid Philyra, assumed the form of a stallion; their child was Chiron, half man and half horse. In some versions Philyra herself was changed into a linden tree after seeing her child. The story is ancient and is reflected in Hellenistic and Roman literature, including Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 2.1231–1241, and later mythographers.

No ancient source located during this research makes the four stars of Equuleus commemorate Cronus’ equine disguise, Philyra, or the infant Chiron. The association probably grew through Chiron’s daughter Hippe and the general horse imagery.

### Castor and horsemanship

Castor is repeatedly distinguished as a horse-tamer: Homer, *Iliad* 3.237 calls him *hippodamos*, “horse-subduing.” The Dioscuri became patrons of cavalry and appeared as horsemen in Greek and Roman religion.

That broad tradition explains why a mythographer could give Celeris to Castor, but it does not independently identify Equuleus. Statements that the constellation honored Castor’s equestrian skill are interpretive expansions.

### The absence from Eratosthenes and Hyginus

The frequent references “Eratosthenes 18” and “Hyginus 2.18” beside Equuleus in modern websites are potentially misleading. Both chapter numbers concern **the large Horse**, now Pegasus. They are relevant to the *reception* of Equuleus mythology only because later writers borrowed those narratives.

This is the dossier’s most consequential source-critical correction.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Babylonian

A Mesopotamian constellation **MUL ANŠE.KUR.RA**, “the Horse,” is attested in *MUL.APIN* I i 30. Modern positional reconstructions generally place it in or around **Cassiopeia and Cepheus**, not Equuleus. [Study of the Babylonian Horse](https://www.sciengine.com/doi/pdf/05B3A95E80984B63AE3FA9E3C30F2EB7)

Therefore:

- **Documented:** Babylonia had a constellation called the Horse.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** its stars were in the far northern sky.
- **Unsupported:** that it was the ancestor of Equuleus.
- **Possible broad influence:** Greek horse imagery may belong to a much larger Near Eastern transmission history, but no continuous chain links the four Equuleus stars to MUL ANŠE.KUR.RA.

No Equuleus-specific omen series or cuneiform name has been securely identified.

## Egyptian

Older writers found no Egyptian antecedent for Equuleus. The situation changed in official nomenclature in 2026, but not because an Egyptian “little horse” was discovered.

**Scholarly reconstruction/modern naming:** δ Equulei was associated with **Tep-en-Nekhet**, “the head of Nekhet/the Giant,” in a reconstruction of Ramesside star clocks. Nekhet was a large Egyptian figure, not a horse and not coterminous with Equuleus.

Thus the Egyptian material is an overlapping sky tradition, not an antecedent of the Greek figure.

## Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī’s learned Arabic figure follows Ptolemy:

- **Qiṭʿat al-Faras** — the section of the horse;
- four faint stars in a quadrilateral;
- a horse head or frontal portion in the illustrations.

**Al-Faras al-Awwal**, “First Horse,” reflects its earlier rising relative to Pegasus. **Al-Faras al-Thānī**, “Second Horse,” also occurs in the transmission; the numbering depends on whether sequence, size, or catalogue order is being emphasized.

### Bedouin sky in the same region

The indigenous Arabian sky did not simply reproduce the Greek horse. Equuleus lies near the long belt of **saʿd** asterisms—paired “lucky” stars running through Capricornus, Aquarius, and Pegasus. α Equulei and β Aquarii form the traditional Chinese Xū pair, but they are not securely one of the canonical Bedouin *saʿd* pairs by themselves.

Claims that all four Equuleus stars formed a particular Bedouin foal could not be traced to al-Ṣūfī. His value is precisely that he distinguishes Greek-derived constellation figures from indigenous Arabian star lore. [Ancient Arabic sky-culture documentation](https://github.com/Stellarium/stellarium-skycultures/blob/master/arabic_ancient/description.md); [study of the *saʿūd*](https://baas.aas.org/pub/2022n6i349p01/release/1)

## Chinese

Traditional Chinese astronomy placed the region in the **Black Tortoise of the North** and, more specifically, the lunar mansion **Xū**, “Emptiness.”

- **α Equulei + β Aquarii** formed **虛, Xū**, the eponymous asterism of the Xū mansion.
- λ Equulei, 4 Equulei, and 3 Equulei were later catalogue additions, enumerated as *zēng* or “added” stars associated with Xū.

The modern Chinese name **小馬座, Xiǎomǎzuò**, is merely the translation of Western Equuleus. It must not be projected backward onto the Han or Tang sky. [Equuleus in Chinese astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equuleus_in_Chinese_astronomy); [IAU account of Chinese nomenclature](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/naming_stars/)

## Indian

Allen claimed that Equuleus was “another of the Açvini, the Horsemen.” This is problematic.

The **Aśvins** are Vedic twin horsemen, and **Aśvinī** is the first lunar mansion, conventionally marked by β and γ Arietis—not by Equuleus. I found no early Sanskrit siddhānta or nakṣatra list making the four Equuleus stars an independent Aśvin figure.

- **Documented:** Aśvins/Aśvinī are ancient Indian equine celestial concepts.
- **Unsupported generalization:** Equuleus itself was an Indian Aśvin constellation.
- **Probable source of confusion:** iconographic similarity and the eagerness of nineteenth-century comparativists to equate horse figures across cultures.

## Persian

No independent ancient Iranian or medieval Persian constellation confined to α–δ Equulei was found. Persian astronomers working in the Islamic scientific tradition used Arabic forms derived from the *Almagest*, such as *Qiṭʿat al-Faras*. Modern Persian *Asb-e kūchak* is a translation of the Western constellation.

The famous “four royal stars” of Iranian/Persian astrological lore do not include an Equuleus star.

## Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No secure biblical, rabbinic, medieval Hebrew, or classical Kabbalistic constellation corresponding specifically to Equuleus could be traced.

Later attempts to map the twelve tribes, Hebrew letters, biblical animals, or the *Sefer Yetzirah* onto all modern constellations are generally **modern esoteric constructions**. They should not be treated as evidence that ancient Hebrew observers recognized Equuleus.

The biblical “horse” passages and the “Rose of Sharon” in Song of Songs 2:1 are literary images, not ancient Hebrew names for these four stars.

## Julius Schiller’s Christian sky

**Documented fact.** In *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* (Augsburg, 1627), Julius Schiller replaced Equuleus with constellation XVIII:

> **Rosæ Mysticæ sive frondis rosæ**

“The Mystic Rose, or a branch/frond of the rose.”

This was Marian symbolism. It is sometimes summarized as “Rose of Sharon,” but Schiller’s printed Latin is **Mystical Rose/rose branch**, not the Hebrew phrase *ḥăḇaṣṣeleṯ ha-Šārôn*. “Rose of Sharon” is therefore an interpretive gloss, not his precise title.

Schiller’s atlas, produced with assistance from Johann Bayer and engraved by Lucas Kilian and others, replaced pagan northern figures with New Testament saints and symbols. The system was reproduced on Andreas Cellarius’ Christianized hemispheres but never displaced the classical constellations. [Library of Congress original](https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbc0001.2017rosen1317/?sp=1&st=gallery); [ETH study](https://astrorara.library.ethz.ch/en/coelum-stellatum); [constellation table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coelum_Stellatum_Christianum)

## Astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* I.9 assigns planetary “temperaments” to prominent stars and portions of many constellations, but **does not assign an Equuleus nature**. [Tetrabiblos I.9](https://astrolibrary.org/library/tetrabiblos/tetrabiblos-11/)

None of Equuleus’ classical four is among the canonical **fifteen Behenian fixed stars**.

The constellation is north of the zodiac and is not itself a zodiac sign. Tropical zodiacal longitudes of its stars change through precession; modern fixed-star astrologers sometimes interpret Kitalpha individually, but such delineations are modern additions rather than preserved Ptolemaic doctrine.

### Lunar mansions

- **Chinese:** α Equulei participates directly in Xū, one of the 28 mansions.
- **Arabic:** the mansion system follows ecliptic stars; Equuleus’ stars are not the defining pair of a standard Arabic lunar mansion.
- **Indian:** not a defining part of Aśvinī or another canonical nakṣatra.
- **European Picatrix tradition:** no distinctive Equuleus mansion identity was found.

## Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No premodern alchemical emblem or Hermetic planetary doctrine specifically attached to Equuleus could be documented. General claims that the Foal represents “untamed Mercury,” “the prima materia,” or initiation are products of modern occult writing unless accompanied by a dated text.

Schiller’s rose is Christian-Marian, not evidence that Equuleus had an earlier Rosicrucian or alchemical meaning.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas

**Documented negative evidence.** Equuleus is absent from the Farnese Atlas. Bradley Schaefer’s analysis identifies Equuleus, Coma Berenices, and Antinous among the later figures missing from the globe. This absence is one argument that the sculpted constellation set reflects an older, broadly Aratean tradition rather than Ptolemy’s complete list. [Schaefer, Farnese Atlas study](https://www.phys.lsu.edu/farnese/JHAFarneseProofs.htm)

A competing observation is that a small unassigned area near Delphinus may have been absorbed into the Dolphin’s tail. That is an iconographic interpretation, not a labeled Equuleus.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

From the tenth century onward, copies of al-Ṣūfī depict the constellation as a small horse’s head and neck. Each pair of images usually offers:

- the external/globe view;
- the direct sky view.

The mirrored pair is integral to the work’s astronomical method, not an illustrator’s error.

### Islamicate globes

Thirteenth-century and later metal globes show a compact horse bust. The small scale and faint stars occasionally make the figure schematic, but its position between Pegasus and Delphinus is stable.

### Dürer, 1515

Dürer’s northern celestial map participates in the Renaissance recovery of classical uranography. Equuleus is a small subsidiary horse figure, easily overshadowed by Pegasus. Its diminutive treatment influenced later printed atlases.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer depicts a naturalistic head and forequarters, oriented according to the internal sky view. His four Greek letters anchor the figure. The constellation is visually separate from Pegasus even though their bodies nearly confront one another.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

The animal disappears and is replaced by a rose or rose branch—**Rosa Mystica**—on the same stellar framework.

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Hevelius presents a compact horse in the reversed globe-view convention. Equuleus shares a plate with Sagitta and Delphinus.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s plate X, engraved at monumental scale, shows Equuleus between Delphinus and Pegasus with dense faint-star plotting. It is a horse’s head, neck, and forequarters, not a complete pony. The scientific grid and catalogue stars coexist with a lightly engraved mythological animal. [NYPL object record and image](https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/510d47dc-8dc2-a3d9-e040-e00a18064a99)

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* simplifies the Bode-era grandeur for a popular audience. Equuleus remains a small horse protome beside Pegasus.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/1825

Card 15, “Pegasus and Equuleus,” copies Jamieson with hand coloring and pierced star holes. The set was long attributed to “a lady”; Peter Hingley identified the probable author as the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam. The attribution and copying history are documented by the Royal Astronomical Society. [RAS account](https://ec2-18-175-4-31.eu-west-2.compute.amazonaws.com/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror); [Library of Congress image](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2002695508/)

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

No ancient coin type, cathedral portal, or monumental ceiling could be securely identified as **Equuleus rather than Pegasus, a generic horse, or a centaur**. This distinction matters: Pegasus is common in ancient coinage and later decorative art, while the tiny four-star protome is rarely independently recognizable.

Medieval calendars based on Aratus usually omit it or include it only through supplementary catalogue traditions. Claims identifying any small carved horse as Equuleus require a constellation label, neighboring figures, or stellar markings.

## People, events, and works

### Navigation

Equuleus has little documented navigational importance. Its stars are faint, and the nearby Great Square of Pegasus, Altair, and Delphinus are more useful landmarks. No major explorer could be verified as steering specifically by Kitalpha or the Equuleus quadrilateral.

Statements that “ancient sailors navigated by Equuleus” should be regarded as unverified unless a logbook, pilot book, or navigational treatise is produced.

### Astronomers

- **Geminus** supplies the earliest surviving notice and the disputed Hipparchan attribution.
- **Ptolemy** canonized the four-star figure.
- **al-Ṣūfī** transmitted and illustrated it in the Islamicate world.
- **Bayer** fixed α–δ.
- **Flamsteed** numbered its stars.
- **Hevelius** and **Bode** gave it influential atlas images.
- **John Herschel** observed the object later catalogued as NGC 7045; it proved to be stellar rather than nebulous.
- Modern stellar observers used γ Equulei and δ Equulei as laboratories for magnetic pulsation and binary dynamics.

### Literature

Marlene van Niekerk’s poem **“Rock Painting”**, written in connection with a Cederberg foal image, calls it “Equuleus of the Cederberg” and later “Celeris.” This is an explicit modern poetic fusion of the constellation and the later Celeris tradition, not ancient evidence. [Poem publication](https://www.poemhunter.com/poem/rock-painting/)

### Music

Soon Hee Newbold composed **Equuleus** in 2008 in memory of Kamila Lemanowicz, whose love of horses prompted the constellation theme. Program notes describe musical movement from a trot to a wild race home. [Program-note source](https://nvso-webmaster.squarespace.com/s/Planets.pdf)

Laurie Larson released an album titled **Pegasus and Equuleus**, with cover art by Marijke Koger. [Album page](https://www.laurielarson.net/pegasus-and-equuleus)

### Spacecraft EQUULEUS

**Documented fact.** **EQUULEUS** is an acronym for **EQUilibriUm Lunar-Earth point 6U Spacecraft**, a JAXA/University of Tokyo CubeSat. It was launched on NASA’s Artemis I mission on 16 November 2022.

Its objectives included:

- low-energy trajectory control in the Earth–Moon system;
- imaging Earth’s plasmasphere;
- detecting lunar impact flashes;
- measuring micrometeoroid impacts;
- demonstrating water-resistojet propulsion.

JAXA reported a successful lunar flyby and the first orbit control beyond low Earth orbit using a water-propellant system. Communications ended in May 2023 before the complete intended science-orbit campaign. Its name deliberately alludes to the Little Horse constellation while fitting the engineering acronym. [JAXA mission result](https://global.jaxa.jp/press/2022/11/20221126-1_e.html); [NASA mission sheet](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180003498/downloads/20180003498.pdf); [University of Tokyo/JAXA overview](https://www.isas.jaxa.jp/feature/eq_om/eq_om_02.html)

Its instruments included the similarly constellation-themed **PHOENIX** and **DELPHINUS**, plus **CLOTH**; its water propulsion system was **AQUARIUS**.

### Ship

**EQUULEUS LEADER** is a documented vehicle carrier, IMO 9342906, registered in classification records. The available register confirms the name but not whether the owner deliberately chose it from the constellation rather than a broader horse-themed naming system. [ClassNK register](https://www.classnk.or.jp/register/regships/one_dsp.aspx?imo=9342906)

### Namesakes not necessarily astronomical

Modern businesses and products use “Equuleus” for its associations with agility, horses, rarity, or the EQUULEUS spacecraft. Such uses are namesakes, not additions to constellation tradition.

## Controversies and disputes

### Did Hipparchus invent Equuleus?

**Disputed.**

The usual claim comes through Geminus, who associates the Horse’s Forepart with Hipparchus. Supporting considerations are:

- Equuleus is absent from Aratus/Eudoxus;
- it appears before Ptolemy;
- Hipparchus worked between those authors;
- Ptolemy’s catalogue drew heavily on earlier Hellenistic star cataloguing.

Against a confident attribution:

- Hipparchus’ surviving *Commentary on Aratus and Eudoxus* does not clearly list it as an independent constellation;
- his full catalogue is lost;
- Equuleus is absent from the Farnese Atlas even in reconstructions that associate that globe with Hipparchan data;
- Geminus’ wording may record use rather than invention.

The most defensible statement is: **Equuleus was first securely attested by Geminus in the first century BCE; Geminus associates it with Hipparchus, but Hipparchus’ personal invention of the figure cannot be independently verified.** [All Skies critical summary](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Equuleus); [recent Hipparchus catalogue research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00218286221128289)

### Was it Ptolemy’s invention?

**Incorrect as a strict claim.** Ptolemy is the first author whose surviving full catalogue establishes the four stars, but Geminus predates him. “Introduced by Ptolemy” is acceptable only in the loose sense “introduced into the canonical catalogue inherited by later astronomy.”

### Is it one of the “classical myth constellations”?

**Astronomically yes; mythographically no.** It is one of Ptolemy’s 48, but it lacks a surviving independent classical catasterism. Popular books commonly erase this distinction.

### Celeris and Hyginus 2.18

**Source-transfer problem.** Hyginus 2.18 is about Pegasus/Equus. Celeris is ancient, but its application to Equuleus is later. Websites citing “Hyginus 2.18” as direct proof that Equuleus is Celeris misstate what the chapter explains.

### Hippe and Eratosthenes 18

The same problem applies. Pseudo-Eratosthenes 18 concerns the Horse/Pegasus, not a separate Little Horse. Hippe’s transfer is reception history.

### Cyllarus

No classical constellation text supports Cyllarus as Equuleus. Furthermore, Cyllarus can denote a horse or Ovid’s centaur. Popular summaries conflate them and sometimes swap the divine gift:

- Celeris to Castor by Mercury;
- Cyllarus to Pollux by Juno.

The balanced conclusion is that this is a **later harmonizing tradition with unstable details**.

### Poseidon’s trident horse

Ancient Poseidon horse-creation myths are genuine. Their attachment to Equuleus is not attested in the surviving classical astronomical sources. In the Athenian contest, the earliest central object is Poseidon’s salt spring/sea-water sign; the horse version is a competing or later elaboration.

### “Rose of Sharon”

Schiller’s name is *Rosa Mystica sive frons/frondis rosae*, the Mystical Rose or rose branch. Calling it the **Rose of Sharon** blends Marian symbolism with Song of Songs 2:1. It is evocative but textually imprecise.

### Kitalpha: star name or constellation name?

Originally, *qiṭʿat al-faras* named the whole Ptolemaic figure. Only later was its contracted form fixed upon α Equulei. Treating Kitalpha as an ancient individual name of α is anachronistic.

### “Kitalpha means the horse’s head”

Not exactly. The Arabic means **piece, section, or portion of the horse**. “Head” describes the picture but narrows the lexical meaning.

### *Kitel Phard* and *Elmac Alcheras*

These are corruptions, not evidence for lost independent Arabic horse myths. Burritt spread *Kitel Phard* in nineteenth-century American popular astronomy. Riccioli transmitted *Elmac Alcheras*, but its morphology does not yield a convincing independent Arabic expression.

### Babylonian antecedent

The Babylonian Horse is real but probably far north in Cassiopeia/Cepheus. Identifying it with Equuleus ignores the positional reconstruction and is not accepted evidence of descent.

### Indian Aśvins

The resemblance “Horsemen → little horse” encouraged comparison, but the Aśvinī mansion lies in Aries. Allen’s equation lacks an early Indian source tying the Equuleus stars to the Aśvins.

### NGC 7045

John Herschel’s nebulous classification entered the NGC, but modern observation shows a multiple star. Calling it a galaxy or nebula is a surviving catalogue-derived error.

### Does Equuleus have no meteor shower?

That was true of established lists before 2025. The discovery of M2025-O1/delta-Equuleids makes the unqualified present-tense claim obsolete, although the shower remains weak and provisional.

### “Second-smallest constellation”

Correct by modern IAU area. It was not meaningfully “second-smallest” before Delporte because constellations did not possess exclusive measured polygons.

### Confusion with Equuleus Pictorius

Lacaille’s **Equuleus Pictorius**, the “Painter’s Easel,” is the modern southern constellation **Pictor**. It has no historical identity with the northern Equuleus. Indexes that say “Equuleus was created by Lacaille” have confused these two names.

### No hoax found

The dedicated searches for “Equuleus hoax,” “controversy,” “obsolete,” and “abolished” produced no documented historical hoax centered on the constellation. The recurrent problems are misattribution, etymological corruption, myth transfer, and confusion with Pictor—not a deliberate fabrication.

## Open questions

1. **Geminus’ exact relationship to Hipparchus.** Does the relevant passage mean that Hipparchus invented the figure, formalized an existing small asterism, or merely employed it?

2. **Pre-Ptolemaic material evidence.** No securely labeled globe or manuscript predating Geminus identifies Equuleus. Future recovery of Hipparchan catalogue fragments could change this.

3. **The faint stars on ancient globes.** Small unlabeled marks near Delphinus on objects such as the Farnese globe remain vulnerable to overinterpretation. Are they incidental, part of Delphinus, or evidence of an embryonic Equuleus?

4. **Exact genealogy of medieval Latin names.** Forms such as *Praesegmen*, *Equi Praesectio*, and *Praecisio Equi* should ideally be tied manuscript by manuscript to Arabic–Latin Almagest translations.

5. **Bode’s extended Greek letters.** A definitive account requires collating the 1801 plate, Bode’s accompanying catalogue, and later *Bonner Durchmusterung*-era usage. Modern compilations agree that Bode extended the sequence to λ, but not all reproduce every intermediate assignment consistently.

6. **Egyptian identification.** Tepennekhet is now an official name, but the correspondence between Ramesside clock positions and δ Equulei remains a scholarly reconstruction rather than an ancient label written beside that modern star.

7. **Bedouin asterisms.** A star-by-star comparison of al-Ṣūfī manuscripts and the indigenous Arabian *anwāʾ* literature may reveal whether any faint Equuleus stars belonged to a named local pair overlooked by modern summaries.

8. **M2025-O1.** Additional years of observation are needed to establish the delta-Equuleids’ recurrence, orbital dispersion, parent body, and permanent status.

9. **Transient history.** A complete list of extragalactic supernovae in modern Equuleus requires a dated database extraction; it cannot be made permanently final in prose.

10. **Bode collection metadata.** The “830 stars in 109 groups” phrase could not be reconciled with Bode’s published star count. Its source collection record should be identified before treating it as historical data.
