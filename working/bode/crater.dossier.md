# Crater: Research Dossier

## Status and evidentiary conventions

**Status: retained.** Crater is one of the 88 constellations recognized by the International Astronomical Union. The IAU adopted the name and three-letter abbreviation in 1922; Eugène Delporte’s boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in 1930. Its stars therefore did **not** pass to another constellation, although modern boundaries separated Crater cleanly from Hydra after centuries in which cataloguers sometimes treated the two together.

**Official name:** *Crater*  
**Abbreviation:** **Crt**  
**Genitive:** **Crateris**  
**English:** the Cup, more exactly a mixing bowl  
**IAU area:** approximately 282 square degrees, 53rd of the 88 constellations  
**Brightest star:** δ Crateris, visual magnitude about 3.56  
**Adjacent IAU constellations:** Leo, Virgo, Corvus, Hydra, and Sextans  
**Position:** mostly right ascension 10h 51m–11h 57m and declination approximately −6.7° to −25.2° in modern coordinates.

The following labels are used:

- **Documented fact:** stated or depicted in a primary source or secure institutional record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern identification or historical inference supported by specialist work but not stated by the ancient source itself.
- **Tradition:** a transmitted identification whose historical existence is documented, though its subject is mythic or symbolic.
- **Myth/legend:** a narrative presented as such in ancient or later literature.
- **Disputed:** attribution, derivation, or identification on which the evidence is inconclusive.
- **Modern invention:** a recent symbolic or occult interpretation without evidence of ancient transmission.
- **Unverified claim:** found in popular or tertiary circulation but not traced to the proposed source.

## Basic identification

### What the figure is

**Documented fact.** The Greek constellation was Κρατήρ, *Krātēr*, a large, normally two-handled vessel in which wine was mixed with water before drinking. It was not originally a small personal goblet. Greek *krātēr* is related to the verb κεράννυμι, “mix,” ultimately to an Indo-European root meaning “mix” or “mingle.” Latin borrowed it as *crater* or *cratera*. The modern geological sense “bowl-shaped depression” arose by metaphor from the vessel.

Aratus, in the early third century BCE, places the vessel on the middle coils of Hydra, with Corvus at the serpent’s farther end: “On its middle coil lies the Bowl” (*Phaenomena* 443–449). This is the earliest surviving continuous literary description securely naming the constellation. [Greek text and translation of Aratus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0483); [Mair translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AratusPhaenomena.html).

Ptolemy catalogued seven stars under Κρατήρ in the *Almagest*, VIII.1, c. 150 CE. His anatomical descriptions make a two-handled mixing bowl unmistakable: base, body, north and south rims, and two handles. [Ptolemy’s seven entries](https://ecliptiqc.ca/Almageste_Livre8.php).

### Bode’s labels and presentation

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode’s *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* was published at Berlin in 1801. The atlas has twenty immense copper plates and a companion German/French catalogue containing 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. Crater appears on the southern-sky sheet commonly described as **Cauda Hydrae, Centaurus, Crater, Argo Navis and Hydra**, among Hydra, Corvus, Centaurus, Antlia, Argo, and Robur Caroli. Bode shows it as a decorated two-handled vessel standing on Hydra.

The plate uses the classical Latin **CRATER**; Bode’s smaller German *Vorstellung der Gestirne* (1782; enlarged edition 1805) calls it **Becher/Der Becher**. It was not invented by Bode and is not among his proposed constellations. [ETH’s complete 1801 *Uranographia*](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [Bode plate description and image](https://www.georgeglazer.com/archives/maps/archive-celestial/bodecentaur.html); [Bode’s German atlas history](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/einl.html).

**Qualification concerning the brief’s “830 stars in 109 groups.”** I could not trace that wording to Bode’s printed text or a standard bibliographical description of the *Uranographia*. It appears to describe a collection, database, or exhibition grouping rather than Crater itself. Bode’s whole 1801 companion catalogue contains 17,240 entries; Crater plainly does not contain 830 stars in the historical naked-eye catalogues.

## Stars and objects

## The historical seven-star bowl

Ptolemy’s seven Crater stars, with the most widely accepted modern identifications, are:

| Ptolemy’s description | Modern identification | Later designation |
|---|---|---|
| Base of the bowl, common with Hydra | α Crateris | 7 Crt, Alkes |
| Southern of two in the middle | γ Crateris | 15 Crt |
| Northern of the pair | δ Crateris | 12 Crt |
| Southern rim of the mouth | ζ Crateris | 27 Crt |
| Northern rim | ε Crateris | 14 Crt |
| Southern handle | η Crateris | 30 Crt |
| Northern handle | θ Crateris | 21 Crt |

This table exposes an important historical fact: Ptolemy’s figure is not identical to every modern stick figure. His “base common with Hydra” reflects overlapping pictorial constellations, not modern territorial boundaries. Moreover, modern β Crateris was catalogued by Ptolemy as a Hydra star immediately behind the bowl’s base. [Complete catalogue context](https://ecliptiqc.ca/Almageste_Livre8.php); [Peters and Knobel’s 1915 critical catalogue](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ptolemy%27s_catalogue_of_stars-_a_revision_of_the_Almagest_%28IA_cu31924012300491%29.pdf).

## Bayer and Flamsteed stars

Bayer’s *Uranometria* (Augsburg, 1603) assigned Greek letters through λ to prominent stars of the region. Letter order does not strictly follow brightness; α is only the second-brightest star, while δ is brightest.

The principal Bayer/Flamsteed stars are:

| Star | Flamsteed | V magnitude, approximately | Role/notes |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| δ Crt | 12 Crt | 3.56 | Brightest star; yellow-orange giant; northern interior/body in Ptolemy. |
| α Crt | 7 Crt | 4.07 | Alkes; orange giant; historical base of the bowl. |
| γ Crt | 15 Crt | 4.06 | White main-sequence star; close visual companion. |
| β Crt | 11 Crt | 4.46 | White main-sequence star; Ptolemy treated it as Hydra, not one of his seven Crater stars. |
| θ Crt | 21 Crt | 4.70 | Northern handle in Ptolemy. |
| ζ Crt | 27 Crt | 4.71 | Southern rim. |
| ε Crt | 14 Crt | 4.81 | Northern rim. |
| λ Crt | 13 Crt | 5.08 | Added to the enlarged Renaissance figure. |
| η Crt | 30 Crt | 5.17 | Southern handle. |
| ι Crt | 24 Crt | 5.48 | Later figure star. |
| κ Crt | — | 5.9 | Fainter Bayer star. |

Flamsteed’s numbering is a trap. In the posthumous *Historia Coelestis Britannica* and *Atlas Coelestis*, he used the combined heading **Hydra et Crater**, numbering stars in right-ascension order across both figures. Consequently a number such as 11 Crateris does not mean Flamsteed imagined a modern bounded Crater of thirty-one stars. Later cataloguers detached the vessel’s designations from the combined sequence. [Flamsteed catalogue cross-index](https://pbarbier.com/flamsteed/flamsteed.html).

### Proper names

#### Alkes — α Crateris

**Documented modern standard; historically transformed name.** The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Alkes** for α Crateris on 12 September 2016. It derives from Arabic **الكأس** (*al-kaʾs*), “the cup.” It was originally a name for the vessel/constellation rather than securely an indigenous name of α alone. Medieval and early modern forms include *Alhas*, *Alker*, and *Elkis*. Joseph Scaliger’s preferred **Alkes** ultimately prevailed in European literature. [IAU star-name list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/naming_stars/); [historical discussion in Allen](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Crater%2A.html).

**Documented late Arabic catalogue form.** In Muḥammad al-Akhṣāṣī al-Muwaqqit’s seventeenth-century Egyptian catalogue, α was called *Awwal al-Bāṭiya*, “the first [star] of the bowl,” Latinized as *Prima Crateris*. Spellings such as “Aoul al Batjna” are European transliteration artifacts, not a separate ancient name.

#### Labrum — δ Crateris

**Tradition, not IAU-approved.** *Labrum* is Latin for a lip, rim, basin, or bathing vessel. Modern popular lists sometimes give it to δ Crateris because of its position in the cup. It is absent from the present IAU official-name list. Calling it an internationally official proper name is therefore an error.

#### Al Sharas — β Crateris

**Unverified/poorly transmitted.** “Al Sharas” circulates in popular constellation pages, usually for β Crateris, but it is not IAU-approved and I found no persuasive derivation from al-Ṣūfī or a critical Arabic star-name study. It should not be presented as an established Arabic proper name.

## Asterisms

### The Bowl or Cup

The familiar modern naked-eye outline generally joins α, β, γ, δ, ε, and ζ, sometimes with η and θ as handles. This is an informal reconstruction, not an IAU-defined line figure.

### Ptolemy’s handled krater

A historically firmer asterism consists of α, γ, δ, ζ, ε, η, and θ. These are specifically assigned to the base, body, rims, and handles in the *Almagest*.

### Hydra–Crater–Corvus

**Documented ancient visual complex.** Aratus, pseudo-Eratosthenes, Hyginus, and Ovid treat the three figures as a connected tableau. This is not a single Ptolemaic catalogue heading, but it is a single mythographic scene.

### Arabic *al-Maʿlaf*

**Documented tradition through al-Ṣūfī.** Indigenous Arabic observers saw some or all of the bowl stars as a **manger or feeding trough**, *al-maʿlaf*. Exact membership varies in later reconstructions. The similarity to a rectangular trough is visual; it is not evidence that the Greek krater was borrowed from this Arabic figure.

## Deep-sky objects

Crater contains **no Messier object** and no conspicuous Galactic nebula or open cluster. Its most important catalogued objects are galaxies.

- **NGC 3511:** nearly edge-on spiral galaxy, about magnitude 11, paired on the sky with NGC 3513.
- **NGC 3513:** barred spiral approximately 11 arcminutes from NGC 3511.
- **NGC 3672:** spiral galaxy in the wider Crater galaxy aggregation.
- **NGC 3887:** barred spiral, one of the more accessible telescopic galaxies in Crater.
- **NGC 3892:** lenticular or barred lenticular galaxy.
- **NGC 3955:** inclined barred spiral.
- **NGC 3962:** elliptical galaxy and one of Crater’s brightest deep-sky objects, approximately magnitude 10.7.
- **NGC 3981:** large, loosely wound spiral, roughly 65 million light-years away.
- **Crater Cloud:** a loose nearby-galaxy concentration within the Virgo Supercluster; not a star cluster or nebula.
- **RX J1131−1231:** a quasar at redshift about 0.658, its host distorted into an Einstein ring and the active nucleus multiplied into four principal images by a foreground galaxy. ESO announced the lens in 2003; later Chandra work used it to constrain the central black hole’s spin. [ESO discovery account](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso0321/); [NASA/Chandra](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/chandra/distant-quasar-rx-j1131/).
- **Crater/Laevens 1:** discovered in 2014 in VST ATLAS data. Initially debated as either an unusually young outer-halo globular cluster or an ultra-faint dwarf galaxy; subsequent work generally favors a remote globular cluster. Its “Crater” name is positional.
- **Crater 2:** an extraordinarily diffuse Milky Way dwarf spheroidal discovered in VST ATLAS imaging by Gabriel Torrealba, Sergey Koposov, Vasily Belokurov, and Mike Irwin in 2016. It lies about 120 kpc away, has a half-light radius near 1.1 kpc, and an exceptionally low surface brightness. [Discovery paper](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/459/3/2370/2595158).

The name **Crater 2** does not mean that it is a physical second component of the classical constellation; it distinguishes the satellite from the earlier object Crater/Laevens 1.

## Variable and planetary-system stars

- **R Crateris:** semiregular SRb red giant, approximately magnitude 8.1–9.5, period near 160 days. Friedrich August Theodor Winnecke recognized its variability in 1861; Annie Jump Cannon’s 1907 catalogue fixed the designation R Crateris.
- **S Crateris:** semiregular SRb late-type giant.
- **SZ Crateris:** nearby binary and low-amplitude BY Draconis-type spotted variable, identified as variable in the late twentieth century.
- Other catalogued variables include eclipsing, rotational, pulsating, and long-period variables; modern boundary catalogues contain far more than the naked-eye figure.
- Several Crater stars have confirmed exoplanets, among them **HD 96167**, whose planet has a markedly eccentric orbit, and HATS survey systems. These systems take their constellation membership solely from Delporte’s boundaries.

## Novae and supernovae

**Negative finding.** I found no securely documented historical naked-eye nova or Galactic supernova assigned to Crater. That absence matters: the famous “new stars” of 1006, 1054, 1572, 1604, and 1670 belong elsewhere.

Numerous extragalactic supernovae have naturally been detected in Crater’s NGC and fainter galaxies, but no one event has become a standard historical object “of Crater.” Lists claiming a famous classical supernova in the Cup should supply a transient designation and discovery record.

## Meteor showers

- **Eta Craterids:** a weak, poorly characterized shower name appearing in modern compilations.
- **Daytime Craterids (426 DCR):** present in the IAU Meteor Data Center’s working list; a radio-detected daytime stream, not a prominent visual annual shower.
- **Theta Craterids** and **Alpha Craterids:** proposed or separated streams in meteor-orbit studies. Their status has changed among working, candidate, and established lists.

**Caution.** A shower named for a Crater star is named for the radiant’s apparent position; meteors do not originate in that star or in the constellation. [IAU Meteor Data Center working list](https://iaumdc.astro.sk/working).

## Boundaries and ownership of stars

### Before Ptolemy

Aratus supplies relative placement but no boundary: the bowl rests on Hydra’s middle coil. Hipparchus’s lost catalogue almost certainly treated it as a recognized figure, since his commentary on Aratus presupposes the constellation system, but the exact membership cannot be recovered completely.

### Ptolemy, c. 150 CE

Ptolemy assigned seven stars to Crater. α Crateris was explicitly “common with Hydra.” Modern β Crateris belonged to Hydra. Constellations could overlap pictorially, and empty sky had no official owner.

### Al-Ṣūfī, c. 964

Al-Ṣūfī retained Ptolemy’s 48 headings and essentially the same seven formal Crater stars, correcting magnitudes and positions and adding indigenous Arabic lore in prose. His two drawings—external globe view and internal sky view—made the vessel a distinct illustrated object while preserving its seat on Hydra.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer enlarged the working stellar figure and assigned Greek letters. His lettering brought β, ι, κ, and λ into the Renaissance Crater convention even though not all belonged to Ptolemy’s seven-star bowl. No border prevented adjoining maps from conceptually overlapping.

### Flamsteed, 1725–1729

Flamsteed catalogued the region as **Hydra et Crater**, producing the combined numerical sequence from which modern Crater Flamsteed numbers were extracted. This is the principal source of confusion about whether he “gave Crater thirty-one stars.”

### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius’s plate WW, **Crater et Corvus**, pictures both figures upon Hydra. His catalogue increased the number of plotted stars without creating a territorial boundary. [Hevelius plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Johannes_Hevelius_-_Prodromus_Astronomia_-_Volume_III_%22Firmamentum_Sobiescianum%2C_sive_uranographia%22_-_Tavola_WW_-_Crater_et_Corvus.jpg).

### Lacaille and Bode

Lacaille’s southern reforms did not abolish or rename Crater. His new **Sextans**-area neighbors and southern instrumental figures helped crowd the later atlas around Hydra. Bode plotted vastly more telescopic stars and nebulae, but retained the conventional classical vessel rather than redefining it as a new invention.

### Argelander and Heis

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) counted fourteen visible stars in Crater; Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* (1872) counted thirty-five. These totals express limiting magnitude and chart practice, not rival official boundaries.

### IAU and Delporte

**Documented fact.** The IAU standardized constellation names and three-letter abbreviations in 1922. Crater survived. Delporte drew its six-segment boundary in equatorial coordinates based on epoch B1875.0, preserving established variable-star nomenclature wherever practicable. The boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930. Precession makes them appear slightly slanted in modern J2000 plots, although the original segments followed right ascension and declination. [IAU history and boundary chart](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [Delporte bibliographical record](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ).

**What changed ownership:** β Crateris, treated as Hydra by Ptolemy, is now territorially in Crater. Conversely, several stars that old artists could place on the vessel, Hydra’s body, or the surrounding blank field were assigned exclusively to Hydra, Sextans, Virgo, Corvus, or Leo. No modern star can be “common” to two constellations.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before c. 275 BCE — possible prehistory

**Scholarly reconstruction, not documented identity.** Some writers have sought a Babylonian prototype for the Hydra–Corvus–Crater tableau. Mesopotamian star lists do contain a long serpent in roughly the Hydra region, but the proposed Babylonian complex does **not** securely contain a matching cup. A bowl-shaped Greek vessel may have been added after the serpent figure entered Greek uranography, or the whole arrangement may have developed within Greek practice.

No securely identifiable pharaonic Egyptian cup constellation using these exact stars has been demonstrated. Egyptian astronomical ceilings contain vessels and divine boats, but resemblance alone is insufficient. The Dendera zodiac is Ptolemaic/Roman-period evidence of a mixed Greco-Babylonian-Egyptian sky, not proof of an indigenous Old Kingdom Crater.

### c. 275 BCE — Aratus

**Documented fact.** Aratus’s *Phaenomena* 443–449 names the bowl and fixes it on Hydra, with the Raven apparently pecking the serpent. At lines around 519–521 he calls the bowl dim. He gives no myth explaining it.

This distinction is fundamental: the constellation’s existence is documented earlier than its surviving catasterism narrative.

### Later third or second century BCE — pseudo-Eratosthenes

**Tradition preserved in an epitome.** The surviving *Catasterismi* is not Eratosthenes’ original book in untouched form; it is an abridged and interpolated descendant. Chapter 41 treats Hydra, Crater, and Corvus together. Apollo sends the crow with a vessel for water needed in sacrifice. The crow waits for figs to ripen, then returns with a water snake and falsely blames it. Apollo exposes the lie and makes the three into adjacent constellations, while the thirsty bird is prevented from reaching the cup.

Hyginus explicitly attributes another version to Eratosthenes: the vessel was the one from which **Icarius gave wine to human beings**. That attribution may derive from a fuller Eratosthenic text now lost.

### c. 150 BCE onward — Hipparchus

Hipparchus’s commentary on Aratus confirms the inherited placement and rising/setting relations. His full catalogue is lost; Ptolemy’s later catalogue is related to it but is not demonstrably a verbatim copy. Claims that Hipparchus personally “invented” Crater are unsupported.

### First century BCE/CE — Roman poetic reception

**Manilius, *Astronomica* 1.** Manilius lists the “Crater pleasing to Iacchus” (*gratus Iaccho Crater*), linking the bowl generically with Bacchus/Dionysus. This is evidence for a Dionysiac reading, but not a full catasterism saying Dionysus personally placed this particular bowl in heaven.

**Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.40.** The composite surviving chapter is the richest ancient mythographic dossier: Apollo’s crow; Mastusius’s blood-filled bowl; Icarius’s wine vessel; and the jar in which Otus and Ephialtes confined Mars. [Latin text](https://latin.packhum.org/loc/899/1/49/515-517,912-914,2517-2519,2710-2712).

### c. 8 CE — Ovid

**Documented myth.** *Fasti* 2.243–266 gives the fullest polished Roman telling of Apollo’s crow. Apollo is preparing a solemn feast or sacrifice for Jupiter. The raven carries a gilded bowl, delays beside unripe figs, eats when they ripen, captures a water snake, and lies that it blocked the spring. Apollo condemns the bird to thirst while figs are unripe; snake, bird, and bowl become an eternal memorial in adjacent stars. [Latin text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Aabo%3Aphi%2C0959%2C007%3A2); [translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti2.html).

### c. 150 CE — Ptolemy

**Documented astronomical canonization.** The *Almagest* lists Crater among the 48 constellations and supplies seven stellar coordinates and magnitudes. All seven are fourth magnitude or qualified fourth magnitude in the received catalogue.

### Late antiquity

Latin translations and adaptations of Aratus—Cicero, Germanicus, Avienius, and the scholia—transmitted the picture of the vessel on Hydra. The scholia add mythographic explanations drawn from the same broad reservoir as pseudo-Eratosthenes and Hyginus.

**Negative finding.** I found no independent Crater catasterism in Nonnus, pseudo-Apollodorus, or Pausanias. Those authors preserve related figures and myths—Icarius and Dionysus, the Aloadae, Apollo’s birds—but should not be cited as witnesses to Crater’s placement unless a specific passage actually names the constellation. Much popular writing blurs “a myth about an object later associated with Crater” into “an ancient catasterism of Crater.”

### c. 964 — al-Ṣūfī

**Documented fact.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* (“Book of the Forms/Images of the Fixed Stars”) preserves Ptolemy’s formal bowl as **al-Bāṭiya**, while reporting the indigenous Arabic **al-Maʿlaf**, the manger or feeding trough. Each constellation was drawn twice, as seen on a globe and as seen in the sky. [Overview and manuscript link](https://www.ianridpath.com/startales/alsufi.html).

### Medieval Latin period

Gerard of Cremona’s twelfth-century Arabic-to-Latin *Almagest* and the Alfonsine tradition transmitted the constellation and Arabic nomenclature. *Al-kaʾs* suffered predictable changes when copied without knowledge of Arabic, creating *Alhas*, *Alker*, and *Elkis*. [Ptolemaeus Arabus et Latinus record](https://ptolemaeus.badw.de/work/3).

### 1515 — Dürer

Albrecht Dürer, working with Conrad Heinfogel and Johannes Stabius, printed the first European star maps, including the southern hemisphere with Crater. The figures are shown from the external or globe viewpoint, following the Ptolemaic descriptive tradition. [National Gallery of Art map](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/43182-southern-celestial-hemisphere).

### 1603 — Bayer

Bayer’s *Uranometria* gives Crater an individual engraved treatment with Bayer letters and the labels *Crater/Calix/Poculum*. The elaborate vessel follows Renaissance classicizing iconography.

### 1627 — Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, published at Augsburg with plates engraved by Lucas Kilian and assistance associated with Johann Bayer, replaced pagan southern figures with Old Testament subjects. **Crater and Corvus together became the Ark of the Covenant**, *Sancta Arca Foederis*. The pairing is documented on Schiller’s combined plate XLII–XLIII. This was a deliberate Christian remapping, not a claim that ancient Israelites called these stars the Ark. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/).

### 1690 — Hevelius

Elisabeth Hevelius published her late husband Johannes Hevelius’s *Prodromus Astronomiae* and *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* at Gdańsk. Plate WW presents **Crater et Corvus**, richly engraved and mirror-reversed relative to a direct sky view because Hevelius used the globe convention.

### 1729 — Flamsteed

The posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* retains the classical vessel but associates its catalogue numbering with Hydra under *Hydra et Crater*.

### 1782 and 1801 — Bode

Bode’s 1782 popular atlas calls the figure *Der Becher*. In 1801 the monumental *Uranographia* restores the international Latin *Crater* on an extraordinarily dense scientific-artistic plate. It plots telescopic stars and nebulae behind a faint classical image. The work’s title and catalogue are bilingual in Latin/German/French contexts, but Crater’s astronomical heading is Latin.

### 1822–1825 — Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* (London, 1822) presents Crater with Hydra and neighboring figures. *Urania’s Mirror* (London, 1824; cards dated 1825 in some issues) copied Jamieson’s compositions. Card 32 includes Hydra, Corvus, Crater, Sextans, Noctua, Felis, Lupus, Centaurus, Antlia, Argo, and Pyxis. The cards have star holes intended to be held to light. The anonymous author was identified in 1994 as the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam. [Royal Astronomical Society account](https://www.women.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror).

### 1843–1872 — scientific simplification

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* and Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* retain Crater but increasingly subordinate the picture to measurable star positions. The difference between fourteen stars in Argelander and thirty-five in Heis chiefly reflects visibility threshold.

### 1922–1930 — international standardization

Crater was retained in 1922, abbreviated **Crt**, and enclosed by Delporte’s boundary. It was never an “obsolete” or “abolished” constellation.

### Present

Crater remains scientifically useful as a coordinate region and culturally recognizable as the classical bowl. The IAU’s territorial constellation and the inherited seven-star figure are related but not identical concepts.

## Classical myths and legends

## 1. Apollo’s cup, crow, and water snake

**Myth; earliest surviving narrative:** pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 41, probably derived from Hellenistic material of the late third or second century BCE.

Apollo sends his sacred crow to bring water in a bowl for a sacrifice. The bird discovers unripe figs and waits until they ripen. Knowing its delay is culpable, it catches a water snake and claims the snake prevented it from reaching the spring. Apollo, as prophetic god, recognizes the lie. He sets crow, cup, and snake together in heaven and keeps the thirsty crow from the water.

Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.40, makes the punishment seasonal: while figs are ripening the crow cannot drink because its throat is “perforated” or dry; the celestial Hydra blocks it from the vessel. Ovid, *Fasti* 2.243–266, gives Apollo a feast for Jupiter, a gilded bowl, and an explicit closing catasterism. Ovid’s version is literary elaboration, not the first attestation.

**Who placed the figure:** Apollo, either by personally placing it among the stars or by causing the commemorative tableau. The texts do not consistently distinguish Apollo’s act from an impersonal catasterism.

**Related but different Apollo-raven myth.** In Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 2.531–632, Apollo turns the formerly white raven black after it reports Coronis’s infidelity. That narrative explains the bird’s color, not Crater. Conflating the Coronis episode with the fig-and-water story is a modern synthesis.

## 2. The cup of Icarius

**Tradition; surviving source:** Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.40, explicitly “some, with Eratosthenes, say” it is the bowl used by Icarius when he showed wine to humanity.

Dionysus taught the Athenian Icarius viticulture. Icarius offered wine to shepherds; mistaking drunkenness for poisoning, they killed him. His daughter Erigone found his body with the help of the dog Maera and hanged herself. In the conventional catasterism Icarius became Boötes, Erigone Virgo, and Maera Canis Minor or its star Procyon.

The attribution of **Crater** to Icarius’s first wine vessel ties the bowl into this constellation complex. It is not the same object as Apollo’s water cup, and Hyginus presents it as an alternative, not as a harmonized sequel.

Ancient narrative witnesses for the wider Icarius myth include pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 3.14.7; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 130; and Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 10.450 and *Fasti* passages concerning Erigone. Only the astronomical tradition explicitly equates Icarius’s vessel with Crater.

## 3. Dionysus’s mixing bowl

**Tradition with limited ancient specificity.** Manilius calls the constellation *gratus Iaccho Crater*, “the Bowl dear to Iacchus,” in *Astronomica* 1. Iacchus is a Bacchic/Dionysiac name. A krater is intrinsically Dionysiac because it is where wine and water are mixed at the symposium.

Later mythographers therefore call it “the mixing bowl of Dionysus.” That is reasonable iconographic interpretation, reinforced by the Icarius story, but I found no surviving early catasterism in which Dionysus simply places his personal cup in the sky under that wording. It should be distinguished from the documented Icarius variant.

## 4. The cup of Mastusius

**Myth; sole full surviving astronomical witness:** Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.40.

King Demophon ruled the Thracian Chersonese during a plague or calamity. An oracle required the annual sacrifice of a maiden. Demophon selected daughters by lot but withheld his own daughters. Mastusius, whose daughter was taken without a fair lot, concealed his anger. Later he invited the king and his daughters to a sacrifice, killed the daughters, mixed their blood with wine in a bowl, and served it to Demophon. On discovering the crime, the king ordered Mastusius and the bowl thrown into the sea. The sea was thereafter called Mastusian and the harbor Crater. Ancient astrologers, Hyginus says, fashioned the bowl in stars to teach that wrongdoing cannot safely profit anyone and enmity is not easily forgotten.

This is an aetiological tale for Thracian place-names as well as a catasterism. Its moral differs sharply from Apollo’s comic tale. It may preserve local material, but no earlier independent Greek version has survived.

**Possible textual complication.** Some editions print the Mastusius narrative as part of the Hydra/Crater chapter’s transmitted sequence; divisions and corrupt names vary. The form *Mastusius* is an editorial correction of manuscript spellings.

## 5. The jar of Ares, Otus, and Ephialtes

**Mythic identification; source:** Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.40: “Others say it is the jar into which Mars was thrown by Otus and Ephialtes.”

The Aloadae—Otus and Ephialtes—captured Ares and confined him in a bronze jar for thirteen months. Hermes rescued the exhausted god after their stepmother Eriboea revealed the imprisonment. The episode’s earliest surviving attestation is Homer, *Iliad* 5.385–391, eighth or seventh century BCE. Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.7.4, supplies a later narrative of the giants’ rebellion and deaths.

Hyginus’s brief notice equates Crater with that jar, but does not explain who placed it among the stars. It is an alternative antiquarian identification, not part of the Apollo–crow story.

## 6. The bowl and human blood

The Mastusius account is sometimes generalized in modern books as an “Asia Minor legend about human blood mixed with wine.” That compressed description derives from Hyginus; it is not an independently attested Anatolian bowl cult. Calling it simply “an ancient Asian blood-cup constellation” strips away the named Thracian characters and should be avoided.

## 7. Crater on Hydra’s back

**Documented iconography, not necessarily one myth.** Aratus already locates the bowl on Hydra. Ptolemy’s base star is common with Hydra. The placement allowed the Apollo story to join all three figures, but physical adjacency predates the surviving explanatory story. It is therefore unsafe to infer that the constellation was invented specifically to illustrate Ovid’s narrative.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Mesopotamian/Babylonian

**Scholarly reconstruction, disputed.** The long Greek Hydra has plausible parallels with Mesopotamian serpent figures. Some diffusion from Mesopotamian uranography into the Greek sky is highly probable in general. For Crater specifically, however, no cuneiform star list securely names a cup or krater occupying Ptolemy’s seven stars. Modern reconstructions that treat the whole Hydra–Crater–Corvus tableau as a translated Babylonian unit exceed the evidence.

Robert Brown’s nineteenth-century *Researches into the Origin of the Primitive Constellations* proposed extensive Euphratean origins and read the cup as part of an underworld/death complex. This is historically influential comparative mythology, but modern scholarship does not treat Brown’s one-to-one identifications as established.

## Egyptian

**Negative finding.** No pharaonic Egyptian name for precisely the Crater stars could be traced. The constellation’s first secure Egyptian-location attestation is Greek-language astronomy produced in or transmitted through the Hellenistic and Roman Mediterranean, culminating in Ptolemy at Alexandria. The Dendera zodiac’s Greek/Babylonian forms are late syncretic evidence, not a Bronze Age Egyptian Crater.

## Arabic and Islamic astronomy

Al-Ṣūfī’s formal Ptolemaic constellation is **al-Bāṭiya**, the wide vessel or bowl. In parallel, Bedouin observers called an asterism in the same region **al-Maʿlaf**, the manger/feed trough. Another Arabic name, **al-Kaʾs**, “the cup,” entered European star-name transmission and became Alkes.

The distinctions matter:

1. *al-Bāṭiya* is a learned translation/adaptation of the Greek figure.
2. *al-Maʿlaf* is indigenous Arabic star lore reported alongside it.
3. *al-Kaʾs* is a generic Arabic “cup” name whose later application narrowed to α Crateris.

## Chinese

**Documented independent tradition.** Most Crater stars belong to **翼宿, Yì Xiù, Wings**, the twenty-seventh of the Twenty-Eight Mansions and the sixth mansion of the Vermilion Bird of the South. It forms the Bird’s spread wings and spans modern Crater and Hydra, with additional stars in nearby regions in expanded catalogues.

A standard reconstruction gives the 22-star Wings chain beginning:

α Crt, γ Crt, ζ Crt, λ Crt, ν Hya, η Crt, δ Crt, ι Crt, κ Crt, ε Crt, and further Crater/Hydra stars, including θ and β Crt. α Crateris is the determinative or reference star in modern identifications.

The mansion acquired calendrical, court, meteorological, and astrological meanings. Texts associate it with music, theatrical or ceremonial performance, guests, and foreign peoples; ominous planetary or lunar trespass could signify war or court distress. Such interpretations belong to Chinese astral omenology, not Greek cup mythology. [Chinese Crater/Wings mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crater_in_Chinese_astronomy); [scholarly history of Chinese star maps](https://press.uchicago.edu/books/hoc/HOC_V2_B2/HOC_VOLUME2_Book2_chapter13.pdf).

The modern Chinese Western-constellation name **巨爵座** is a translation created within modern international astronomy and must not be substituted for ancient **翼宿**.

## Indian

**Negative/qualified finding.** Crater lies well south of the ecliptic, while the Indian *nakṣatra* system is defined by lunar stations close to the Moon’s path. No classical Vedic nakṣatra is made from the principal Crater stars. Modern star charts may place Crater south of the longitude occupied by late Virgo/early Libra nakṣatras, but that is geometric projection, not identity. Claims that Crater “is Hasta” confuse ecliptic longitude with stellar membership.

## Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

**Negative finding for antiquity.** The Hebrew Bible names or alludes to a few stellar groups—*Kimah*, *Kesil*, *ʿAyish*, and the obscure *Mazzarot*—but none is securely Crater. *Mazzarot* in Job 38:32 is a hapax legomenon probably referring to constellations, zodiacal stations, or seasonal stellar signs collectively. It is not an ancient Hebrew proper name for Crater.

**Modern invention/tradition.** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century “Gospel in the Stars” authors interpret Crater as the cup of divine wrath, citing Psalms, Isaiah, or Revelation. This scheme was popularized particularly through Frances Rolleston’s *Mazzaroth* (1862) and E. W. Bullinger’s *The Witness of the Stars* (1893). It is Christian typology imposed on the Greco-Roman constellation, not ancient Hebrew or Kabbalistic evidence.

I found no stable traditional Jewish Kabbalistic mapping that assigns the non-zodiacal constellation Crater a sefirah, Hebrew tribe, or biblical cup. Internet charts doing so are modern eclectic occultism unless an early manuscript can be produced.

## Schiller’s Christian sky

**Documented early-modern invention.** In 1627 Schiller joined Crater and Corvus as the **Holy Ark of the Covenant**, *Sancta Arca Foederis*. The chest, cherubim, and sacred fittings replace the cup and bird. This reflects Schiller’s larger rule: zodiacal figures became Apostles, northern figures New Testament subjects, and southern figures Old Testament subjects.

It is a Christianized atlas, not an assertion of ancient Israelite uranography.

## Alchemical and Hermetic readings

**Negative finding for a canonical ancient reading.** Greek and late-antique alchemy makes abundant use of vessels—kraters, bowls, furnaces, and mixing metaphors—but I found no primary Hermetic or alchemical text securely identifying the astronomical Crater as a specific alchemical vessel.

Later esoteric authors interpret it as:

- the vessel of transmutation;
- the Holy Grail;
- the cup of divine wrath;
- the womb or cosmic receptacle;
- the mixing of spirit and matter.

These are symbolically intelligible but are **modern or early-modern analogical readings**, not demonstrated continuations of an ancient Crater doctrine.

A tempting confusion is the **Krater** in the *Corpus Hermeticum* IV, where God sends down a mixing bowl filled with mind. That philosophical krater is a metaphor. No ancient passage establishes that it is the constellation.

## Astrology

### Ptolemaic qualities

Later fixed-star handbooks commonly assign Crater the nature of **Venus and Mercury**. The attribution is compatible with Ptolemy’s planetary-quality system, but direct claims that “Ptolemy says Alkes is Venus–Mercury” are suspect: Ptolemy normally discusses the qualities of constellation regions and did not know the medieval proper name Alkes.

### Manilius

In *Astronomica* 5, Manilius’s paranatellonta assign occupations and dispositions to people born as individual non-zodiacal figures rise. Later summaries associate Crater with wine, mixing, trade, and vessels. Textual reconstruction of Book 5 is difficult, and editions differ; modern deterministic lists should not be treated as word-for-word Manilius without an edition and line reference.

### Alkes in modern astrology

Twentieth-century fixed-star manuals describe Alkes as conferring love of knowledge, preservation, eminence, artistic or prophetic gifts, and sometimes concern with water or wine. These meanings are later astrological tradition, not astronomy and not a recoverable Babylonian doctrine.

### Behenian stars

**Negative finding.** Alkes and the other Crater stars are not among the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars transmitted in *De quindecim stellis* and Agrippa. Claims that Alkes is Behenian are false.

### Lunar mansions

Crater participates directly in the Chinese Wings mansion. It does not supply a principal star to the classical Arabic *manāzil al-qamar* or Indian nakṣatras because its stars lie too far from the ecliptic. Tropical occult systems may draw mansion sectors across the same longitudes, but that is not stellar membership.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas, Roman, c. 150 CE, Naples

The Farnese Atlas is the oldest surviving large-scale representation of the classical celestial globe: a Roman marble Atlas bearing a sphere with approximately 41 constellation reliefs. Identification of small, eroded figures is difficult. Crater has been claimed among the southern reliefs, but the globe has no individual star marks, and not every identification is equally secure. Bradley Schaefer’s claim that its constellation positions encode Hipparchus’s lost catalogue is influential but disputed; the sculpture cannot by itself prove the date or authorship of its prototype. [Schaefer’s study](https://www.phys.lsu.edu/farnese/JHAFarneseProofs.htm).

### Aratea manuscripts, ninth–fifteenth centuries

Latin Aratus manuscripts repeatedly show Crater as a two-handled cup sitting upon a long Hydra, with Corvus facing or pecking the serpent. Details vary: shallow basin versus tall chalice, symmetric versus asymmetric handles, bird facing forward or sideways. The image cycle is evidence of medieval transmission, not necessarily an exact copy of an ancient archetype. [Saxl Project Crater image survey](https://www.thesaxlproject.com/assets/Uploads/00-Crater-Master-R-6-Nov-2023.pdf).

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, from the eleventh century onward

The oldest surviving illustrated copies of al-Ṣūfī show the Ptolemaic vessel in paired mirror images. Manuscript date must not be confused with the work’s composition in c. 964. The image is an Islamic redrawing of the learned Greek constellation accompanied by Arabic observational lore.

### Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

Dürer’s southern celestial hemisphere shows the bowl from the globe perspective. Ptolemy, Aratus, Manilius, and al-Ṣūfī appear as authorities around the pair of maps, visually claiming a chain of ancient and Islamic transmission.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer gives Crater an ornate, monumental two-handled vessel. Star letters are laid across the cup rather than confined by borders. The composition became a major template for seventeenth-century atlas makers.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

Crater and Corvus disappear beneath the Ark of the Covenant. The Christian image is explicitly labeled as replacing constellations XLII and XLIII.

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Plate WW, *Crater et Corvus*, depicts a richly modeled urn on Hydra with the crow nearby. As a globe-view atlas, the image is reversed relative to the observer’s sky.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode depicts an elegant two-handled krater or vase on Hydra’s back, surrounded by far more plotted stars and nebulae than any ancient figure. The pale image allows the coordinate grid and stellar catalogue to dominate. The sheet embodies the final great synthesis of allegorical figure and precision stellar cartography.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson simplifies the classical figure for education, retaining the handled cup and adjoining serpent and bird.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/1825

Card 32 reproduces the Jamieson complex in hand-colored form with pinpricked stars. The cup is visually subordinate to the enormous Hydra. The cards’ attractive illustrations were long presented as anonymous; Richard Rouse Bloxam’s authorship was identified by Peter Hingley in 1994.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

**Negative finding.** Greek and Roman vessels, ravens, snakes, and Dionysiac kraters are extremely common on coins and pottery, but an object’s resemblance to Crater does not establish constellation iconography. I found no securely identified ancient coin whose type is explicitly the constellation Crater.

Crater appears in medieval astronomical manuscript calendars and celestial diagrams. General zodiac cycles on cathedral portals normally omit it because it is not zodiacal. Claims that a cup on a portal or church ceiling “is Crater” require contextual star marks, neighboring Hydra/Corvus, or an explanatory inscription.

## People, events, and works

### Observers and cataloguers

- **Aratus of Soli** supplied the earliest surviving literary placement.
- **Hipparchus** criticized and quantified Aratean constellation positions; his exact Crater catalogue is lost.
- **Ptolemy** fixed seven stars in the durable catalogue tradition.
- **al-Ṣūfī** compared Ptolemy with his own observations and Arabic folk astronomy.
- **Johann Bayer** supplied enduring Greek-letter designations.
- **John Flamsteed** numbered the combined Hydra–Crater region.
- **Johannes and Elisabeth Hevelius** transmitted its finest seventeenth-century engraving and catalogue.
- **Johann Elert Bode** placed it within the most extensive pictorial atlas of its era.
- **Eugène Delporte** converted the inherited figure into a precisely bounded sky area.
- **F. A. T. Winnecke** recognized R Crateris’s variability in 1861.
- **Torrealba, Koposov, Belokurov, and Irwin** discovered Crater 2 in VST ATLAS data.

### Navigation

Crater contains no first- or second-magnitude star and is poorly suited to oceanic navigation. Mariners could recognize it through nearby Spica, Corvus, Leo, and Hydra, but I found no explorer whose surviving log identifies Crater as a decisive steering constellation. Assertions that ancient sailors routinely “navigated by Alkes” are unsupported.

### Literature

- Aratus, *Phaenomena* 443–449 and approximately 519–521.
- pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 41.
- Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.40.
- Manilius, *Astronomica* 1 and the paranatellonta tradition of Book 5.
- Ovid, *Fasti* 2.243–266.
- Medieval *Aratea* and mythographic commentaries.
- Bode’s *Uranographia* and its companion *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*.

No major surviving ancient tragedy or epic centers on Crater as a personified character; it is an object constellation embedded in other myths.

### Music, paintings, buildings, ships, and missions

**Negative finding.** “Crater” is a common geological and astronomical word, making name association hazardous. I found no historically important ship or space mission demonstrably named for the constellation rather than an impact crater or volcanic crater.

Modern artworks and musical pieces titled *Crater* generally use the geological meaning. Likewise, buildings decorated with cups or kraters cannot be linked to the constellation without explicit astronomical context.

No spacecraft has been sent “to Crater,” since a constellation is a line of sight containing objects at unrelated distances. Crater 2 and RX J1131−1231 have been targets of telescope programs, not spaceflight destinations.

## Controversies and disputes

## Was Crater Babylonian?

**Disputed.** General Mesopotamian influence on Greek astronomy is well established, and Hydra has plausible serpent precursors. But the specific cup is missing from the best-known Babylonian reconstruction of these stars. Nineteenth-century diffusionist schemes, especially Robert Brown’s, are not primary Babylonian testimony.

**Finding:** a Babylonian antecedent for the precise Crater vessel is possible but unproved.

## Was it Egyptian?

**Unverified claim.** Popular “ancient Egyptian zodiac” websites sometimes assign every Ptolemaic constellation an Egyptian deity. No secure pharaonic Crater counterpart was found. Ptolemy’s residence in Egypt does not make each of his constellations Egyptian.

## Did Eratosthenes write the surviving *Catasterismi*?

**Disputed authorship.** A genuine Eratosthenic work underlies the tradition, but the surviving text is an epitome with later alteration. It is best cited as pseudo-Eratosthenes or the Eratosthenic *Catasterismi*. Hyginus’s “with Eratosthenes” testimony may preserve material absent from the epitome.

## Which myth is original?

No source tells us. Aratus knows the constellation but supplies no story. The Apollo–crow account is the earliest surviving explanatory narrative and elegantly accounts for all three adjacent figures; that makes it early and influential, not necessarily the constellation’s historical origin. The Icarius, Mastusius, Dionysiac, and Aloadae versions are competing ancient interpretations.

## Is it Apollo’s cup or Dionysus’s?

Both are ancient traditions, but of different strength. Apollo’s cup has a complete Hellenistic/Roman catasterism. Dionysus is supported by Manilius’s “dear to Iacchus” and by the Icarius wine story. Saying that it is definitively Dionysus’s personal chalice suppresses the evidence’s plurality.

## Is “Crater” a cup or a crater?

The constellation name means a mixing bowl. The volcanic and impact-depression meaning is secondary. Illustrating the constellation as a hole in the ground is a modern lexical mistake.

## Is Alkes an ancient name of α Crateris?

**Partly misleading.** *Al-kaʾs* is authentically Arabic for “the cup,” but historically it named the constellation or vessel. Its restriction to α and its spelling **Alkes** are products of European transmission. The IAU made Alkes the star’s official proper name only in 2016.

## Is Labrum official?

No. It is a traditional/modern Latin positional name for δ Crateris but is not on the IAU approved-name list.

## Is β Crateris one of Ptolemy’s bowl stars?

No. Ptolemy catalogued it with Hydra. It became a Crater star in the expanded Renaissance and modern tradition. This is the most important contested star-membership issue.

## Did Flamsteed give Crater thirty-one stars?

**Misleading formulation.** His sequence was *Hydra et Crater*. Later extraction of the Crater members makes it look like an independent numbered list running to 30 or 31.

## Did Bode invent Crater?

No. It predates Bode by more than two millennia. Bode inherited it from the Ptolemaic tradition. Statements grouping Crater with Bode’s invented figures such as *Globus Aerostaticus* or *Officina Typographica* are errors.

## Was Crater abolished?

No. It was retained by the IAU. The confusion may arise because many neighboring figures shown by Bode—Felis, Noctua, Robur Caroli, and others—were not retained.

## Is Schiller’s Ark an ancient Hebrew constellation?

No. It is a documented Christian invention of 1627. Schiller’s atlas is the evidence for the reinterpretation and simultaneously evidence that it was a reinterpretation.

## Is Crater the Holy Grail or cup of divine wrath?

These are later Christian/esoteric readings. They are not present in Aratus, Ptolemy, pseudo-Eratosthenes, Hyginus, or Ovid. The “cup of wrath” identification belongs especially to modern Gospel-in-the-stars literature.

## Is it a Behenian constellation?

No Crater star belongs to the canonical fifteen Behenian stars.

## Hoaxes and popular errors

No major historical Crater-specific hoax emerged from the searches. The recurring lower-grade errors are:

- calling α/Alkes the brightest star when δ is brighter;
- calling δ’s “Labrum” an IAU name;
- treating *Al Sharas* as a secure ancient Arabic name;
- presenting Ovid as the inventor or earliest source of the crow story;
- claiming that Ptolemy drew modern constellation borders;
- treating Chinese Wings as a Chinese translation of the Greek cup;
- assigning Crater to an Indian nakṣatra merely by longitude;
- calling Schiller’s Ark a Hebrew or biblical star tradition;
- confusing Crater/Laevens 1 with Crater 2;
- saying Bode invented or the IAU abolished Crater.

## Open questions

1. **Pre-Aratean history.** When did Greek observers first isolate the bowl from the stars on Hydra? No securely dated pre-Hellenistic text names it.

2. **Babylonian transmission.** Was the bowl an unattested component of a Mesopotamian serpent complex, a Greek reinterpretation of an unrelated asterism, or an independent Greek addition?

3. **Eratosthenic text.** How much of the surviving Apollo story and Icarius variant belongs to Eratosthenes’ original work rather than later epitomators?

4. **Mastusius’s local background.** Does Hyginus preserve a lost Hellenistic Thracian source, and can the “Mastusian Sea” and harbor Crater be matched securely to historical toponyms?

5. **Arabic membership of *al-Maʿlaf*.** Manuscripts and modern identifications do not always agree on the precise stars forming the manger. A full answer requires collation of Arabic manuscripts, not merely European summaries.

6. **Early Persian terminology.** Al-Ṣūfī worked in Persia but wrote in Arabic. No independent pre-Islamic Persian name for this particular asterism has been demonstrated here.

7. **Farnese identification.** Is the small vessel relief securely Crater, and how much positional information can legitimately be extracted from the damaged globe?

8. **Bode collection metadata.** The brief’s “830 stars in 109 groups” and “Bode collection on chain” could not be matched to Bode’s printed catalogue. Identifying the holding institution or database record may explain the wording.

9. **Meteor streams.** The Alpha, Theta, Eta, and Daytime Craterid labels remain vulnerable to revision as orbit surveys split, merge, confirm, or reject weak showers.
