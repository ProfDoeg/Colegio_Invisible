# Robur Carolinum: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

> **Status: abolished.** Robur Carolinum is not one of the 88 constellations adopted by the International Astronomical Union. It consequently has no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, area, or boundary. Its principal stars now lie in **Carina** and **Vela**; the changing outlines used by Halley, Hevelius, Bode, and nineteenth-century popularizers may also touch the modern domains of **Musca** and, depending on how the foliage is interpreted, the vicinity of **Centaurus, Crux, Chamaeleon,** and **Volans**.

The historical constellation was an oak tree commemorating the tree at Boscobel in which Charles Stuart, later Charles II, and the Royalist officer William Careless concealed themselves on 6 September 1651, three days after the Battle of Worcester. Edmond Halley transferred this Restoration symbol to the southern sky during or immediately after his 1676–78 observing expedition to St Helena. His planisphere was in print by 1678; the accompanying *Catalogus Stellarum Australium* appeared in 1679.

### Names

| Form | Language/date | Meaning and status |
|---|---|---|
| **Robur Carolinum** | Halley, Latin, 1678/1679 | “The Caroline/Charlesian Oak,” conventionally “Charles’s Oak.” *Robur* is a neuter Latin noun meaning oak, especially hard oakwood, and figuratively strength. *Carolinum* is the agreeing neuter adjective from *Carolus*, Charles. |
| **Robur Caroli** | Hevelius and several later atlases | “Charles’s Oak,” with *Caroli* as the possessive genitive. |
| **Robur Caroli II** | Bode, 1801 | “The Oak of Charles II,” removing any ambiguity about the honoree. |
| **Robus Carolinum** | Occasional early or modern transcription | A copying, printing, or cataloguing error for *Robur Carolinum*. It appears even in otherwise reputable collection descriptions and should not be treated as an alternative classical name. |
| **Robor Caroli / Robor Carolinum** | Modern reproductions, databases, and one scholarly illustration | Misspelling or OCR error, not good Latin. |
| **Robur Carolina** | Reported in connection with a nineteenth-century work by James Cook | Grammatically discordant if *Robur* is intended; no atlas carrying this form was located in the consulted evidence. |
| **Charles’s Oak / King Charles’s Oak / the Royal Oak** | English | Historical vernacular names. |
| **Le Chêne de Charles**, sometimes *de Charle* | French | “Charles’s Oak.” The singular *Charle* on eighteenth-century plates is an orthographic or engraving form, not a different person. |
| **El Roble de Carlos / Roble de Carlos II** | Spanish | “Charles’s Oak.” |
| **Quercia di Carlo** | Italian | “Charles’s Oak.” *Quercia* is the ordinary Italian word for oak. |
| **Karlseiche / Carls Eiche** | German | “Charles’s Oak”; Bode’s 1782 German label was **Carls Eiche**. |
| **Eik van Karel / Karelseik** | Dutch | “Charles’s Oak.” |
| **Дуб Карла** | Russian | “Charles’s Oak.” |

The sometimes-published genitives **Roboris Carolini** and **Roboris Caroli** are grammatically possible depending on whether the second word is treated as an adjective or a royal genitive. The commonly manufactured abbreviation **Rob** and genitive **Roboris Caroli** belong to modern reference or astrological tables, not to an IAU decision. The IAU never retained the figure.

### Position

The figure occupied the eastern or prowward part of ancient Argo Navis, in the dense southern Milky Way between the ship and Centaurus. Halley’s twelve catalogue entries cover what is now primarily southeastern Carina and adjacent Vela. The most recognizable modern skeleton runs through or near:

- β Carinae, Miaplacidus;
- θ Carinae;
- η Carinae and the Carina Nebula;
- ω Carinae;
- μ Velorum;
- several naked-eye Carina stars now bearing lower-case Latin Lacaille designations.

No single modern polygon can be given without choosing one historical depiction. Constellations before Delporte were figures and cataloguing conventions, not mutually exclusive parcels of sky.

### The IAU settlement

At its first General Assembly in Rome in May 1922, the IAU adopted a list described as the “88 principal constellations” and their three-letter abbreviations. Robur Carolinum was absent. Curiously, the printed list contained 89 names because it retained both old **Argo** and its three component names; this was an inconsistency, not a reprieve for Halley’s Oak. In 1925 Eugène Delporte presented a boundary system; the final B1875 right-ascension and declination boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in 1930. The Oak’s sky was thereby allocated chiefly to **Carina (Car)** and **Vela (Vel)**. The IAU did not convene a separate proceeding to “abolish” Robur Carolinum: its exclusion from the adopted list, followed by the exhaustive Delporte partition, made the rejection definitive. [IAU history and current list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [original 1922 list and its Argo anomaly](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html); [Delporte bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ).

## Stars and objects

### Halley’s original twelve stars

This is the most important corrective to later popular accounts. Halley did not create a 25-star constellation. His published catalogue contains **twelve** entries under Robur Carolinum. Lacaille explicitly confirms the number twelve and says nine were already in older catalogues and marked in Bayer’s Argo. Burritt’s later 25-star count belongs to Burritt’s expanded drawing.

Halley’s catalogue coordinates are ecliptic longitudes and latitudes for the completed year 1677, not modern J2000 equatorial coordinates. His descriptions are:

| Halley no. | Historical magnitude | Halley’s Latin description | Translation |
|---:|---:|---|---|
| RCa 1 | 2 | *Quæ ad radicem* | The star at the root |
| RCa 2 | 3 | *In summo trunco* | At the top of the trunk |
| RCa 3 | 5 | *In ramis præcedentibus de quatuor borea* | Northern of four in the preceding branches |
| RCa 4 | 4 | *Sequens* | The following one |
| RCa 5 | 4 | *Præcedens* | The preceding one |
| RCa 6 | 4 | *Media* | The middle one |
| RCa 7 | 4 | *In ramis sequentibus duarum borea* | Northern of two in the following branches |
| RCa 8 | 5 | *Austrina* | The southern one |
| RCa 9 | 4 | *In summâ arbore, duarum præcedens* | Preceding of two at the top of the tree |
| RCa 10 | 3 | *Sequens* | The following one |
| RCa 11 | 4 | *Informium ad truncum præcedens* | Preceding of the unformed stars near the trunk |
| RCa 12 | 4 | *Sequens* | The following one |

The entries and their original coordinates can be checked directly in the digitized catalogue transcription at [Halley catalogue lines 142–153](https://pbarbier.com/halley/halley.dat). Baily’s nineteenth-century reduction associates them with Lacaille catalogue numbers 848, 964, 935, 968, 922, 943, 1025, 1037, 949, 970, 894, and 920, respectively; see the [Baily cross-identification transcription](https://pbarbier.com/halley/cross_b.dat).

Several identifications in popular lists silently mix Halley’s twelve stars with Hevelius’s lines, Bode’s foliage, or Burritt’s 25-star version. The following identifications are the secure, historically consequential ones:

- **β Carinae — Miaplacidus**, visual magnitude about 1.7. It was the Oak’s brightest star and was called **α Roboris** or **α Roboris Caroli** in subsequent nomenclature. Lacaille reassigned it as β Argus; it became β Carinae after the component constellations acquired their modern suffixes. It lies at the southwestern end of the modern Diamond Cross.

- **θ Carinae**, magnitude about 2.7. It is the brightest member of IC 2602 and the northeastern point of the Diamond Cross. In Hevelius’s rendering it serves as a conspicuous branching junction.

- **η Carinae**, historically Halley’s RCa 4, *Sequens*, cross-identified by Baily with Lacaille 968. Halley’s 1677 observation is the earliest secure record presently known. Its later designations included η Roboris Caroli, η Argus, η Navis, and finally η Carinae. Halley could not have known that it was an unstable luminous blue variable.

- **μ Velorum**, magnitude about 2.7, lies on one of the strong tree-lines in Hevelius’s depiction and demonstrates that at least some later versions of the Oak extended into modern Vela.

- **ω Carinae**, magnitude about 3.3, and various lower-case-letter Carina stars occur in later, enlarged delineations.

The statement sometimes encountered that “all its stars are now in Carina” is therefore too simple for the full atlas tradition. It is approximately true of Halley’s principal catalogue group as commonly reconstructed, but Hevelius’s and Bode’s drawn tree reaches into modern Vela, and at least one modern reconstruction of the Hevelian line work reaches toward γ Muscae.

### Proper names and their derivations

#### Miaplacidus — β Carinae

**Documented modern fact:** Miaplacidus is the IAU-approved proper name of β Carinae.

**Disputed etymology:** The name is late and opaque. It first becomes conspicuous in Elijah Burritt’s mid-nineteenth-century *Geography of the Heavens* tradition, rather than in ancient Arabic astronomy. The conventional explanation treats it as a hybrid of Arabic *miyāh*, “waters,” and Latin *placidus*, “calm/gentle”: approximately “calm waters.” That bilingual construction is philologically suspicious and no secure Arabic manuscript phrase yielding the exact form has been demonstrated. It should therefore be called a traditional modern explanation, not a proven Arabic derivation. [β Carinae summary and first-map dating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_Carinae).

#### η Carinae

It has no ancient proper name securely attached to the star. **Foramen**, Latin “hole/opening,” is sometimes printed, but evidence traced in this research points only to twentieth-century astrological literature, notably Vivian Robson’s 1923 compilation. It is not Halley’s name and not an ancient designation.

The Chinese expression **Tseen She**, often assigned to η Carinae by Richard Hinckley Allen and derivative sources, is a misidentification of an asterism name, not a traditional individual proper name of η Carinae. In the seventeenth-century Chinese southern-sky system η Carinae belongs to **海山, Hǎi Shān**, “Sea and Mountain,” and is now termed **海山二**, “the second star of Sea and Mountain.” [η Carinae naming discussion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eta_Carinae).

#### Vathorz, Simiram, and similar forms

Names such as **Vathorz Prior**, **Vathorz Posterior**, and **Simiram** appear in modern astrological lists for υ, θ, or ω Carinae. They are not found in Halley’s catalogue, Bode’s labelling, or an identifiable Arabic primary tradition consulted here. Their transmission appears to run through modern fixed-star compilations. They should be labelled **unverified antiquarian or astrological names**, not Arabic names.

### Bayer letters, Lacaille letters, and Flamsteed numbers

Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria* treated the region as Argo Navis. Halley detached twelve stars but did not apply a complete Bayer-letter sequence to them. Later authors called the brightest α Roboris and sometimes gave other Oak-qualified letters, particularly η Roboris Caroli.

Lacaille restored the group to Argo, distributed Greek letters across the brightest stars of the entire ship, and then divided the ship for cataloguing purposes into **Pouppe**, **Corps**, and **Voilure**—Puppis, Carina, and Vela—reusing upper- and lower-case Latin letters within those parts. This is why the surviving Bayer sequence is incomplete in each modern component: α and β are in Carina; γ and δ in Vela; ζ in Puppis.

Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* of 1877/1879 made such forms as η Carinae standard by appending the daughter-constellation name to the inherited Argo letter.

Flamsteed numbers are largely irrelevant here. Flamsteed observed from England, where much of the Oak is below the horizon, and the modern southern constellations do not possess an ordinary indigenous series of Flamsteed numbers comparable to Orion or Taurus. Lists assigning “Flamsteed numbers” to all the Oak’s stars are usually confusing catalogue running numbers, Lacaille numbers, or later catalogue IDs with Flamsteed designations.

### Asterisms within the same stars

- **Diamond Cross:** β, θ, υ, and ω Carinae. This is a modern navigational/popular asterism, not Halley’s tree, though all or nearly all lie in the enlarged Oak tradition.

- **Southern Pleiades:** IC 2602, centred visually on θ Carinae. The name is a modern comparison with the Pleiades in Taurus.

- **南船, Nán Chuán, Southern Boat:** a Chinese asterism commonly reconstructed from β Carinae, θ Carinae, ω Carinae, PP Carinae, and V337 Carinae. Its overlap with Halley’s Oak is substantial. The individual name θ Carinae is **南船三, Nán Chuán sān**, the third star of Southern Boat. The southern asterisms were added to Chinese uranography in the early seventeenth century from European observations, so this is not evidence for an ancient Chinese oak. [θ Carinae and Nán Chuán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theta_Carinae).

- **海山, Hǎi Shān, Sea and Mountain:** η Carinae, s Carinae, λ Centauri, and λ Muscae in the reconstructed Chinese southern sky.

### Nebulae, clusters, and other deep-sky objects

Because Robur Carolinum never had fixed boundaries, “objects in the constellation” must mean objects visually enclosed by a particular atlas tree. The following lie in or immediately against the region claimed by its most influential depictions:

- **NGC 3372, the Carina Nebula / Eta Carinae Nebula:** the dominant emission-nebula complex of the area. It contains η Carinae and the Homunculus Nebula. Lacaille recorded the surrounding nebulosity; modern designations include Caldwell 92.

- **Homunculus Nebula:** ejecta from η Carinae’s nineteenth-century Great Eruption. It did not exist in its present form when Halley drew the tree.

- **Keyhole Nebula:** a dark and bright structure within NGC 3372, historically associated with John Herschel’s southern observations.

- **IC 2602:** open cluster, the Southern Pleiades or θ Carinae Cluster. It is among the clearest deep-sky objects enclosed by the later Oak.

- **NGC 3293:** compact young open cluster northwest of the Carina Nebula; included by some broad reconstructions of the foliage.

- **NGC 3324 / Gum 31:** emission nebula and cluster on the northwestern edge of the wider Carina complex. Whether it belongs to “the Oak” depends entirely on the chosen outline.

- **NGC 3532:** the Wishing Well Cluster. It lies farther from the secure Halley twelve and should not be assigned to the Oak without reference to a specific enlarged atlas outline.

- **IC 2448:** planetary nebula near β Carinae, an angular neighbor rather than an object historically associated with the Oak.

No Messier object carries the name Robur Carolinum, and none of the securely enclosed objects has an official “Charles’s Oak” NGC name. Claims that a nebula was historically named after the Oak could not be traced.

### Variables, eruptions, novae, and supernovae

#### η Carinae

This is the great historical transient of the figure.

- **1677, documented:** Halley recorded it as a fourth-magnitude star, RCa 4, *Sequens*. His catalogue appeared in 1679.
- **1751–52, documented:** Lacaille observed it and eventually catalogued it as Lacaille 968, assigning η in the Argo sequence.
- **1830s–1850s, documented:** η Carinae underwent the Great Eruption. Around 1843 it brightened to approximately magnitude −1, becoming for a time one of the brightest stars in the sky, then faded dramatically.
- **Modern classification:** a massive interacting binary dominated by a luminous blue variable. The Great Eruption was a “supernova impostor,” not a core-collapse supernova.
- **Cultural reconstruction:** Hamacher and Frew argued that a Boorong Aboriginal account of a conspicuous red star called **Collowgullouric War** may preserve observation of η Carinae during its eruption. The identification is scholarly reconstruction, not an explicit historical statement by the Boorong informants; the authors themselves discuss uncertainties in Stanbridge’s atlas and catalogue identifications. [Hamacher and Frew, 2010](https://old.narit.or.th/files/JAHH/2010JAHHvol13/2010JAHH...13..220H.pdf).

Other variables in the broad historical footprint include pulsating and eclipsing stars bearing lower-case Lacaille designations, but no complete “Robur Carolinum variable-star list” can be defined scientifically because there is no boundary.

No historical supernova is securely recorded as occurring “in Robur Carolinum.” Eta Carinae is frequently but incorrectly called a supernova in popular accounts.

### Meteor showers

No meteor shower is officially named **Robur Carolinids**, **Charles’s Oakids**, or the like. Modern radiants in this part of the sky are assigned to Carina, Vela, Centaurus, or nearby constellations. Lists that back-project modern Carinid radiants into the abolished tree are geometrically possible but historically anachronistic.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1603: Argo, not an oak

**Documented fact:** The stars that Halley appropriated were not an ancient tree. In Greco-Roman astronomy they lay within or near **Argo Navis**, the ship of Jason. Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogue described Argo as a single constellation, partly outside the main drawn figure.

**Negative finding:** No Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, or Indian source located in this research identifies these particular stars as Charles’s Oak or as an independent oak constellation. Any such claim would be chronologically impossible in its Caroling form.

The southern declination of much of the group also limited ancient Mediterranean observation. Precession changes visibility slowly, but the far-southern stars were never conveniently placed for systematic observation from Babylonia, Egypt, Greece, or medieval Europe.

### 1595–1603: southern voyages and Bayer

Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser’s observations from the first Dutch East Indies voyage and Frederick de Houtman’s later catalogue supplied European uranography with much improved southern stars and twelve new southern constellations. Petrus Plancius placed these on globes; Johann Bayer incorporated them in *Uranometria* (Augsburg, 1603).

Bayer retained the relevant bright stars in **Argo Navis**. Lacaille later emphasized this point: nine of Halley’s twelve Oak stars were already in old catalogues and marked by letters on Bayer’s Argo plate.

η Carinae presents a special identification problem. A Keyser entry of about fourth magnitude lies near its expected position and was copied into Plancius/Hondius/Bayer material, but de Houtman omitted it. Modern studies therefore distinguish a possible Keyser precursor from Halley’s first **secure** observation.

### 1627: Schiller’s Christian sky

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaced pagan Argo with **Noah’s Ark**. The atlas gives both Christian and conventional descriptions, making comparison possible. Since Robur Carolinum had not yet been invented, Schiller did not replace Charles’s Oak with a saint or Christian object; rather, the stars later taken for the Oak belonged to his expanded biblical Ark. [Linda Hall Library, Schiller’s Noah’s Ark plate](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/12-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum/).

### 1651: Worcester and Boscobel

On 3 September 1651 Parliamentarian forces defeated Charles’s army at Worcester. Charles fled northward. After failed efforts to cross the Severn, he reached Boscobel early on 6 September. He and William Careless spent that day concealed in the crown of a pollarded oak while troops searched nearby.

The political story is exceptionally well attested for a Restoration legend, but its sources have layers:

- **Contemporary Restoration publication:** Thomas Blount’s *Boscobel; or, The Compleat History of His Sacred Majesties Most Miraculous Preservation After the Battle of Worcester* appeared in 1660, alongside other loyalist escape narratives.
- **Royal retrospective:** Charles dictated his own account to Samuel Pepys in October 1680, twenty-nine years after the event; it was not published until 1766.
- **Material/place tradition:** Boscobel House, the priest hole, the Royal Oak site, and the Giffard/Penderel associations survive, but the present tree is a descendant, not securely the original trunk.
- **Duration dispute:** later authors repeatedly say Charles hid “twenty-four hours.” English Heritage’s reconstruction says approximately fourteen hours or “all day.” The twenty-four-hour formula probably compresses the entire dangerous Boscobel interval into the time in the tree.

The episode rapidly became Restoration political mythology. Charles was restored on 29 May 1660; 29 May became Oak Apple Day, and the Royal Oak proliferated in pub signs, prints, medals, ceramics, and loyalist decoration. [English Heritage narrative](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/boscobel-house-and-the-royal-oak/history/charles-ii-and-the-royal-oak/); [site history](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/boscobel-house-and-the-royal-oak/history/); [Pepys narrative edition record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jj.13167955).

### 1676–1678: Halley at St Helena

Halley sailed to St Helena aboard an East India Company vessel to improve knowledge of the southern sky. He observed there chiefly from early 1677 to early 1678, contending with cloud, wind, and heavy dew. He measured southern-star positions relative to reference stars, observed the 7 November 1677 transit of Mercury—28 October in the Julian calendar then used in England—and returned in 1678.

**Documented fact:** His printed planisphere was produced by July 1678, before the 1679 catalogue. It displays the oak and is dedicated to Charles II. [Royal Museums Greenwich description of Halley’s southern planisphere](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-546948); [modern account of publication sequence](https://c01.purpledshub.com/bbcskyatnight/2023/05/17/mapping-the-southern-constellations/).

**Modern-invention status:** Robur Carolinum first exists as a celestial figure here. It had no independent traditional prototype in those stars.

**Motive:** Halley’s text and dedication place the royal commemoration beyond reasonable dispute. Calling the act “patriotic,” “courtly,” or an effort “to please the king” is interpretation; Lacaille’s later phrase was explicitly hostile—Halley detached the stars *pour faire sa cour au roi d’Angleterre*, “to pay court to the King of England.”

### 1678–1679: the degree story

Halley received an Oxford M.A. by royal mandate in November 1678 and was elected to the Royal Society. Later authors, especially Richard Hinckley Allen, say that inventing the Oak “secured” the degree.

That causal formulation is **disputed and overstated**. The surviving wording of the royal mandate praised Halley’s learning in mathematics and astronomy and the observations made at St Helena. Charles’s approval and patronage are real; direct evidence that the constellation-name alone purchased the degree was not located. The Queen’s College exhibition repeats the connection but also makes clear that the scientific expedition and catalogue were the broader accomplishment. [Queen’s College, “Edmond Halley: In Print”](https://www.queens.ox.ac.uk/exhibitions/edmond-halley-in-print/).

### 1679: *Catalogus Stellarum Australium*

Halley published *Catalogus Stellarum Australium sive Supplementum Catalogi Tychonici* in London, printed by Thomas James. It contains 341 entries and twelve stars under Robur Carolinum. The planisphere embodies the commemorative claim more vividly than the terse star descriptions.

A French translation, *Catalogue des estoilles australes*, also appeared in Paris in 1679, helping dissemination beyond England. [1679 original scan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ACatalogus_stellarum_australium-_sive%2C_Supplementum_catalogi_Tychenici_..._%28IA_catalogusstella00hallgoog%29.pdf); [catalogue research transcription](https://pbarbier.com/halley/halley.html).

### 1690: Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius included **Robur Caroli** in the posthumously published *Prodromus Astronomiae* and its atlas, *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* (Gdańsk, 1690). His plate depicts a full oak adjoining the mutilated Argo.

Hevelius had met Halley in Gdańsk in 1679, but popular descriptions that call Halley simply “Hevelius’s pupil” are misleading. Their relationship involved mutual observation and Halley’s Royal Society mission to examine Hevelius’s naked-eye methods; it was not an ordinary master-pupil apprenticeship.

### 1700–1750: atlas reception

The Oak enjoyed conspicuous paper life:

- **1700:** Carel Allard’s celestial work belongs to the atlas culture in which the figure circulated, although individual editions require plate-by-plate verification.
- **1721/22:** John Senex’s planispheres show Robur Carolinum.
- **1729:** Flamsteed’s posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* includes it pictorially, although Flamsteed did not supply the region with a normal southern Flamsteed-number sequence.
- **1731:** Richard Cushee’s pocket globe labels **Robur**.
- **c. 1750:** John Bevis’s unpublished or posthumously circulated *Uranographia Britannica* shows the oak beside Argo.
- **1742:** Johann Gabriel Doppelmayr’s *Atlas Coelestis* uses **Robur Caroli**.
- **1750:** Tobias Conrad Lotter’s *Planisphaerium Coeleste* engraves a form sometimes read **Robur Carolim**, probably an engraving or transcription error.

These appearances demonstrate reception, not universal astronomical acceptance.

### 1751–1756: Lacaille’s rejection

At the Cape of Good Hope, Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille catalogued far more southern stars than Halley. His observations were made in 1751–52; the relevant memoir appeared in the *Mémoires de l’Académie royale des sciences* volume for 1752, published in 1756.

Lacaille omitted the Oak and explained why in an unusually sharp final paragraph:

> “One will not find here the new constellation that Mr Halley inserted in his planisphere in 1677 under the name *Robur Carolinum*, because I have restored to the Ship the beautiful stars which that astronomer, then aged twenty-one, detached from it to pay court to the King of England.”

He continued that Halley had shortened Argo to isolate the tree, left fairly bright stars unformed between ship and tree, and failed to compare the Oak stars with old catalogues, allegedly to suggest they were newly observed. Lacaille concluded that nine of the twelve were already in old catalogues and marked in Bayer’s Argo.

These are **documented accusations by Lacaille**, not neutral findings. The numerical part—twelve stars, nine previously catalogued—is independently checkable and broadly supports him. The accusation that Halley intended to deceive readers into thinking the stars were new is an inference about motive. Halley’s larger title claimed a supplement to Tycho, not that every star was newly visible.

Lacaille did not merely object to royal flattery. His operational concern was catalogue continuity: Argo was already established, and detaching its finest stars made an old figure and its nomenclature harder to use. [French text and English translation context](https://pbarbier.com/lacaille/remarks.html).

### 1763 and the later eighteenth century

Lacaille’s *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*, published posthumously in 1763, codified his southern survey and retained the restored Argo arrangement. Its shorter Latin remarks omit the full polemic printed in the French Academy memoir.

French and continental atlas makers increasingly followed Lacaille, although the Oak remained in reissues influenced by Flamsteed. Jean Fortin’s French *Atlas Céleste* editions of 1776 and 1778 still pictured **le Chêne de Charle**, illustrating the lag between professional cataloguing reform and popular atlas iconography.

### 1782: Bode’s first restoration

Johann Elert Bode included the figure in *Vorstellung der Gestirne* (Berlin, 1782) as **Carls Eiche**. This was a deliberate antiquarian retention or revival after Lacaille, not a new invention.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode’s large *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* was published in Berlin in 1801. It contains twenty engraved plates and an accompanying catalogue/description of 17,240 stars and other objects. The Oak appears on **plate XX** in the crowded southern celestial region, labelled **ROBUR CAROLI II**.

Bode’s Latin label means “The Oak of Charles II.” The illustration fills the gap beside the eastern end of Argo with a naturalistic trunk and spreading foliage. It preserves Halley’s political monument while Bode simultaneously adopts much of Lacaille’s expanded southern sky. This is characteristic of Bode: his atlas was encyclopedic and included many figures that had only weak or obsolete standing.

Bode’s descriptive text does not establish the Oak as a newly measured constellation with boundaries. Its significance lies in inclusion, naming, and depiction. The figure is one of the historical and proposed groups gathered into Bode’s expansive visual census. The supplied claim that Bode’s collection has “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be matched to a statement in the consulted *Uranographia* title matter. Bode’s accompanying catalogue is normally described as containing 17,240 stars; “830 in 109 groups” may refer to a separate institutional database or image collection and remains **unverified** here. [ETH Zürich digital *Uranographia*](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [Marseille digital copy](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035); [plate contents](https://raremaps.com/gallery/detail/69577/uranographia-sive-astrorum-descriptio).

### 1810–1845: popular afterlife

- **1810:** William Croswell’s *Mercator Map of the Starry Heavens* labels “Robur Carolinum the Royal Oak.”
- **1822:** Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas*, plate 28, depicts the tree.
- **1825:** *Urania’s Mirror* drew heavily on Jamieson, but its standard card set does not give Charles’s Oak an independent card. Claims that the Mirror independently established the figure should therefore be treated cautiously.
- **1835:** Elijah H. Burritt’s popular atlas tradition shows “Robur Carolinum, King Charles’ Oak.” Burritt’s mapped figure has **25 stars**, more than double Halley’s twelve.
- **1845:** celestial-globe gores attributed to Bale and preserved at Greenwich still show **Robur Caroli**.
- **1856 editions:** Burritt’s *Geography of the Heavens* helped transmit both the figure and the unexplained name Miaplacidus.

The Oak survived longest in historical, educational, and decorative atlases. Professional catalogues increasingly used Lacaille’s Argo subdivisions.

### Argelander and Heis

Friedrich Wilhelm Argelander’s nineteenth-century programme and Eduard Heis’s star atlases belong to the movement toward observation-based northern catalogues, magnitude estimates, and standardized nomenclature. No evidence was found that either restored Robur Carolinum as a scientifically operative constellation. Their omission is evidence of declining technical use, although not a formal abolition.

### 1877–1930: disappearance from scientific use

Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* regularized the southern sky and attached daughter names to inherited Argo letters. Eta Argus became η Carinae; β Argus became β Carinae. The Oak no longer performed useful catalogue work.

The IAU’s 1922 list omitted it, and Delporte’s boundaries assigned every point in its former vicinity to retained constellations. Robur Carolinum thus differs from an IAU constellation later renamed: it never entered the official system.

### Present

The name survives in:

- histories of obsolete constellations;
- reproductions of Halley, Hevelius, Bode, and Burritt;
- astrological fixed-star lists;
- exhibitions about Halley or Restoration political imagery;
- occasional commercial, literary, or organizational names.

It has no standing in positional astronomy.

## Invention, meaning, and political reception

### What the figure honored

Robur Carolinum honored neither a botanical discovery nor the English oak in general. It specifically memorialized the **Royal Oak of Boscobel** as an emblem of Charles II’s providential preservation and Restoration.

The image carried several simultaneous messages:

1. **Dynastic memory:** the threatened Stuart heir survived.
2. **Providence:** Restoration publications called the escape miraculous.
3. **Loyalty:** the Penderels, Careless, Jane Lane, and other helpers became models of faithful service.
4. **Durability:** Latin *robur* means both oakwood and strength, making the name an implicit emblem of restored monarchical firmness.
5. **Patronage:** the dedication associated Halley’s scientific success with Charles II’s support.

The double meaning of *robur* is a plausible learned resonance, but no statement by Halley explicitly unpacking it was found. It should be described as literary interpretation.

### Relation to Cor Caroli

**Cor Caroli**, the “Heart of Charles,” is α Canum Venaticorum in the northern sky. It belongs to the same broad Restoration habit of political celestial commemoration, but its dedication is contested: sources variously associate it with Charles I, Charles II, or a statement by Sir Charles Scarborough.

The heart and oak were never parts of one formal constellation. Later writers often conflate them because both contain *Caroli* and both were linked with Stuart monarchy. Cor Caroli survived as a proper star name; Robur Carolinum did not survive as a constellation.

### Political constellations and their fate

Robur Carolinum belongs beside:

- Cor Caroli;
- Hevelius’s Scutum Sobiescianum, honoring Jan III Sobieski;
- Kirch’s Sceptrum Brandenburgicum;
- Pomum Imperiale;
- Taurus Poniatovii;
- Psalterium Georgii;
- Honores Friderici.

Some survived because their stars filled genuinely under-defined regions and later cataloguers found them serviceable. Others failed because they displaced established figures, honored ephemeral rulers, or lacked broad international adoption. The Oak suffered all three disadvantages: it mutilated ancient Argo, advertised one monarchy, and became unnecessary after Lacaille’s survey.

## Myths and legends

Robur Carolinum has no classical catasterism. Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Aratus, Germanicus, Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, Pausanias, and the ancient mythographers cannot mention a constellation invented in the seventeenth century.

The relevant inherited myths belong to **Argo Navis**, whose stars Halley used.

### Argo and the Golden Fleece

**Greek myth, not historical astronomy:** Argo was the ship built for Jason’s expedition to Colchis to obtain the Golden Fleece. Athena assisted its construction, and a speaking timber from the oak of Dodona was placed in the prow. This creates a striking but accidental oak connection: Halley’s political oak occupied the area traditionally associated with a ship whose prow contained sacred oak.

Ancient sources vary over whether Athena, Argus, or both built the ship. Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1.18–20 and 1.109–114, makes Argus the builder under Athena’s direction; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9.16, likewise has Argus build it with Athena’s aid and install speaking Dodonaean timber.

No evidence shows that Halley chose the Boscobel Oak because Argo already possessed a Dodona oak timber. The parallel is symbolically attractive but **unverified**.

### Catasterism of Argo

Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* and the surviving Epitome say that the ship was placed among the stars because it was the first ship and because of the Argonauts’ celebrated voyage; only part appears, generally from the stern to the mast, so that the heavens preserve its memory without showing the dangerous prow.

Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* 2.37, repeats variants: some said Athena placed the first ship in the sky; others tied it to the Argonauts. He notes that only part is visible. Germanicus’s Aratea and scholia likewise discuss the half-ship.

This ancient iconographic incompleteness made the eastern/prow end unusually negotiable. Early modern artists hid or truncated it with cloud, rock, or mist. Halley inserted his oak in precisely this unstable pictorial zone.

### Jason and the Argonauts

The major episodes include:

- Jason’s claim against Pelias and the commission to recover the Fleece: Pindar, *Pythian* 4; Euripides, *Medea*; Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9.16.
- The gathering of heroes, with variable crew lists: Apollonius 1.23–227; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 14.
- Lemnos and Hypsipyle: Apollonius 1.609–909; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.17; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 15.
- Cyzicus’s accidental death: Apollonius 1.936–1077.
- Hylas and Heracles: Apollonius 1.1207–1357; Theocritus, *Idyll* 13; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.19.
- Amycus and Polydeuces: Apollonius 2.1–163; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.20.
- Phineus and the Harpies: Apollonius 2.178–499; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.21.
- The Symplegades or Clashing Rocks: Apollonius 2.549–606; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.22. Atlas artists sometimes placed rocks or cloud around Argo’s missing prow; later readers can mistakenly interpret this as an astronomical precursor of Halley’s Oak.
- Medea’s aid, the dragon, and seizure of the Fleece: Apollonius books 3–4; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.23.
- The murder or dismemberment of Apsyrtus, in sharply divergent versions: Apollonius 4.410–481; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.24; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 23.
- Talos in Crete: Apollonius 4.1638–1693; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.26.
- The return and the ship’s later dedication: traditions place Argo at Corinth or dedicate it to Poseidon; celestial versions make Athena the catasterizing power.

These are myths of the displaced parent figure, not myths of Robur Carolinum.

### The Royal Oak story

The constellation’s own “myth” is Restoration political legend grounded in a historical escape. The basic concealment is documented by near-contemporary accounts. Providential embellishment began immediately after the Restoration:

- the king’s preservation is repeatedly termed miraculous;
- loyal helpers are idealized;
- the oak becomes a quasi-sacred royal relic;
- fragments, acorns, descendants, pub signs, and Oak Apple Day materialize the story;
- durations and troop proximity grow more dramatic in retelling.

The best evidentiary label is therefore **documented event surrounded by political tradition and legend**, not pure myth and not a hoax.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

No secure Babylonian asterism covering β, θ, η Carinae and μ Velorum was located. These stars are too far south for regular visibility from Babylonia. Claims that Robur Carolinum descends from a Mesopotamian sacred tree confuse the globally widespread tree symbol with demonstrable star-to-star transmission.

### Egyptian

No pharaonic Egyptian constellation can be securely mapped onto Halley’s twelve stars. Argo itself was sometimes allegorically identified by early modern and nineteenth-century authors with the boat of Isis or Osiris, but those are comparative reconstructions, not evidence that Egyptians grouped this exact southern field as the same ship.

### Greek and Roman

The stars belonged to Argo or its unformed surroundings. Greek names include **Ἀργώ, Argō**; Latin names include **Argo**, **Navis**, **Navis Argo**, **Argo Navis**, **Iasonia Carina**, and poetic descriptions of the Jasonian ship.

### Arabic and al-Sufi

Al-Sufi’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābitah* (964) transmitted Ptolemy’s **Ship** as **al-Safīnah**, السفينة, “the ship,” with mirrored internal and external views in manuscript illustration. Because much of Halley’s later Oak is extremely southern, identifications are sparse or uncertain.

No independent Arabic oak is recorded in these stars. The relevant Arabic tradition is the ship, supplemented by indigenous Bedouin star lore farther north. Modern proper names in Carina should not automatically be treated as ancient Arabic: Miaplacidus is especially doubtful as a genuine inherited Arabic form. [Al-Sufi Argo manuscript image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABook_of_the_Fixed_Stars_Auv0373_Argo_navis_%28part%29.jpg).

### Persian

Persian astronomical texts translated and adapted the Ptolemaic-Arabic ship tradition. No distinct Persian royal oak in the Halley stars was found. Modern Persian names for the abolished constellation are translations of the European name, not evidence of pre-Halley attestation.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

Biblicalizing writers associated Argo with **Noah’s Ark**. Schiller’s 1627 visualization is the clearest atlas expression, but it is Christian, not evidence of an ancient Jewish constellation system.

No Hebrew astronomical manuscript, rabbinic text, or early Kabbalistic source was found that maps Halley’s exact twelve stars to an oak, the Tree of Life, Noah’s Ark, or a sefirah. Modern esoteric diagrams that associate southern constellations with the Kabbalistic Tree of Life are **modern inventions unless independently sourced**.

### Indian

Canopus, α Carinae, is the famous Indian star **Agastya**, but it lies outside the Oak’s central group. No nakshatra crosses the far-southern Oak region: the classical lunar mansions follow the Moon’s ecliptic path, while Robur Carolinum is far south of it.

Later Indian nautical or regional traditions may group southern stars into boats, but no continuous Indian “Charles’s Oak” analogue was found. The suggestion that Argo was an Indian *Argha* steered by Agastya occurs in nineteenth-century comparative star lore and is not accepted here as established historical derivation.

### Chinese

The traditional mansion system did not contain most of these far-southern stars. They entered formal Chinese mapping in the early seventeenth century through the work of Xu Guangqi and Jesuit collaborators drawing on European southern charts.

Relevant asterisms include:

- **南船, Nán Chuán**, Southern Boat: β and θ Carinae, ω Carinae, PP Carinae, and V337 Carinae.
- **海山, Hǎi Shān**, Sea and Mountain: η Carinae with stars in Carina, Centaurus, and Musca.

Allen’s statement that **Nan Chuen** was “doubtfully” or incorrectly placed in Robur Carolinum reflects imperfect nineteenth-century cross-mapping, not a Chinese tradition of the Royal Oak.

### Alchemical and Hermetic interpretation

No early modern alchemical or Hermetic text was found that assigns a stable operation, metal, planet, or emblem to Robur Carolinum. Oak symbolism—strength, Jupiter, kingship, endurance—is common in emblem books, but applying it to this constellation without a cited historical text is modern analogical interpretation.

### Astrology

Robur Carolinum does not occur in Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* because it did not exist. It has no Ptolemaic planetary “nature” and is not part of the ancient zodiac.

It is not one of the fifteen Behenian fixed stars. No Arabic or European lunar mansion crosses the figure as a constellation, although ecliptic longitude projections used by modern astrologers can assign its stars to tropical Libra or Scorpio. That projection does not mean the Moon passes through the actual far-southern figure.

Vivian E. Robson’s *The Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology* (1923) assigned the Oak the influence: frank, honorable, generous, hospitable, and steady. This is **modern astrological invention**, apparently deduced from the moralized oak/royal symbolism, not transmitted from classical astrology. Modern astrological websites that give **Rob** as abbreviation or exact zodiacal spans are extending Robson’s system, not reporting IAU astronomy.

## Iconography

### Ancient and medieval precursors: Argo only

- **Farnese Atlas, Roman, probably second century CE, now Naples:** shows the ancient celestial globe and a ship figure, not Robur Carolinum. Any attempt to locate the later Oak on it is retrospective.
- **Al-Sufi manuscripts, from the medieval Islamic tradition after 964:** depict *al-Safīnah*, the Ship, usually in paired mirror views.
- **Dürer–Stabius–Heinfogel southern celestial map, Nuremberg, 1515:** renders the Ptolemaic southern sky with Argo. No oak.
- **Bayer, Augsburg, 1603:** Argo’s prow is pictorially incomplete or obscured; the stars later taken by Halley remain associated with the ship.
- **Schiller, Augsburg, 1627:** Noah’s Ark occupies Argo’s stars.

### Halley’s planisphere, London, 1678/1679

Halley’s southern hemisphere planisphere is the first image of the constellation. The oak is placed beside the shortened ship. The chart is dedicated to Charles II and surrounded by diagrams connected with the Mercury transit and Halley’s spherical-trigonometric method.

Its visual rhetoric is courtly: a living, spreading dynastic emblem grows in a sky newly measured under royal patronage.

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Hevelius’s *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* gives one of the clearest large engravings of **Robur Caroli**. Like the other plates, it follows the external or globe-oriented convention, so modern comparisons must reverse orientation carefully. The tree’s lines have been reconstructed as running from a central θ Carinae region toward β Carinae, μ Velorum, and in some readings γ Muscae.

### Senex, London, 1721/22

Senex’s planispheres show the oak as part of the increasingly crowded early-eighteenth-century sky. His work belongs to the controversial publication history surrounding Flamsteed’s observations, but that catalogue dispute does not itself determine ownership of the Oak.

### Flamsteed, London, 1729

The posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* admits the figure pictorially. It is misleading to say simply that “Flamsteed accepted it” without distinguishing the atlas prepared and published after his death from his observing catalogue.

### Cushee, London, 1731

A pocket celestial globe labels the figure **Robur**, showing that the image circulated beyond monumental folio atlases into portable educational instruments.

### Doppelmayr, Nuremberg, 1742

Doppelmayr’s *Atlas Coelestis* includes **Robur Caroli** among the southern figures.

### Lotter, Augsburg, c. 1750

Lotter’s *Planisphaerium Coeleste* pictures the tree between Argo and Centaurus. Readings such as *Robur Carolim* appear to result from engraving or transcription difficulty.

### Bevis, London, c. 1750

The *Uranographia Britannica* depicts the oak, although Bevis’s atlas had a complicated publication history and did not achieve the circulation originally intended.

### Lacaille, Paris, 1756

Absence is the iconographic statement: the planisphere omits the tree and restores its stars to Argo. Lacaille explains the deletion explicitly rather than silently redesigning the sky.

### Fortin, Paris, 1776 and 1778

French reworkings of Flamsteed retain **le Chêne de Charle**, evidence that attractive inherited pictures outlived catalogue consensus.

### Bode, Berlin, 1782

*Vorstellung der Gestirne* labels the figure **Carls Eiche**.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Plate XX of *Uranographia* gives the grand late form **ROBUR CAROLI II**. The spreading crown overlaps the star-rich Milky Way field and presses against Argo, visually revealing the very territorial dispute Lacaille had raised.

### Croswell, United States, 1810

Croswell’s Mercator map uses the bilingual explanatory label “Robur Carolinum the Royal Oak,” translating the political emblem for American readers.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s plate 28 retains the tree in a restrained neoclassical style. Its inclusion helped transmit the figure into nineteenth-century popular star lore.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1825

The cards borrowed heavily from Jamieson’s designs, but Robur Carolinum lacks a separate standard card. Composite southern cards and derivative reproductions can cause it to be attributed too freely to the set.

### Burritt, United States, 1835 onward

Burritt calls it “King Charles’ Oak” and expands it to approximately 25 stars. This is a popular-atlas recension, not Halley’s original star count.

### Bale globe gores, Britain, 1845

Surviving gores in the Greenwich collection still show **Robur Caroli**, one of the late material witnesses to the constellation.

### Royal Oak secular imagery

The terrestrial emblem appears far more widely than the celestial one:

- Restoration engravings of Boscobel and White Ladies;
- paintings by Isaac Fuller and Robert Streater in the 1660s–70s;
- medals and loyalist tokens;
- pub signs;
- Oak Apple Day calendars and popular prints;
- architectural and heraldic oak imagery.

No ancient coin, cathedral zodiac portal, or medieval calendar can depict Robur Carolinum. Assertions that an oak in such works is “the constellation” are anachronistic unless a post-1678 programme explicitly names it.

No securely identified major painted ceiling portraying the Oak was located in the consulted sources.

## People, events, and works

### Edmond Halley

Halley’s connection is primary and documented: observer, cartographer, catalogue author, and inventor. The user’s lead that “Halley’s own atlas journey” should be treated as an **Edmond Halley subject**, not as something contemporary with Johann Bode, is correct. Bode was born in 1747, five years after Halley died; he was a later restorer, not a collaborator or eyewitness.

### Charles II and the Boscobel helpers

The figure memorializes Charles, but its terrestrial story also depends on:

- William Careless, who shared the tree;
- Richard, William, Humphrey, John, and George Penderel;
- the Giffard household network;
- Jane Lane and later helpers in the escape.

Halley’s Latin name suppresses these helpers in favor of royal possession: it is Charles’s Oak, not Careless’s or the Penderels’ refuge.

### Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille

Lacaille is the constellation’s most important critic. His rejection was practical, historical, and political. He restored the stars to Argo, supplied enduring letter designations, and provided later astronomers with a superior southern catalogue.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode preserved the figure at the end of the great illustrated-atlas tradition. His 1801 plate is not evidence that the constellation had regained professional consensus; it is evidence of his encyclopedic desire to show received and proposed figures together.

### Navigators

No explorer is documented as steering by “Robur Carolinum” as such. Navigators used the bright stars of Argo, Canopus, the Southern Cross, and later nautical tables. β Carinae, μ Velorum, and nearby stars could of course contribute to orientation, but naming the tree was unnecessary.

Modern spacecraft use Canopus as an attitude-reference star, but Canopus was in Argo/Carina, not one of Halley’s twelve Oak stars. Statements that “spacecraft navigate by Robur Carolinum” are false.

### Observers and discoveries

- Halley: first secure recorded observation of η Carinae.
- Lacaille: extensive southern catalogue and observation of the Carina nebulosity.
- John Herschel: detailed nineteenth-century southern observations of the Carina Nebula and Keyhole region.
- Observers at the Cape and elsewhere: records of η Carinae’s Great Eruption.
- Modern observatories and Hubble/JWST teams: imaging of η Carinae, the Homunculus, and the Carina star-forming complex.

### Literature and music

The Royal Oak incident generated poems, broadsides, histories, tavern names, and patriotic songs. The constellation itself has a much smaller literary footprint.

Nabokov’s *Pale Fire* mentions the “pine groves of Boscobel” in its invented royal-escape narrative, a literary echo of Charles II rather than an explicit reference to the constellation.

No canonical poem, opera, symphony, or major building demonstrably titled *Robur Carolinum* was located. Modern uses of the Latin phrase by companies or private properties are namesakes, not continuations of astronomical practice.

### Ships and missions

Many vessels have been named **Royal Oak**, most famously HMS *Royal Oak*, but their names refer to the Boscobel emblem, not necessarily to Halley’s constellation. No space mission officially named Robur Carolinum was located.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was the constellation invented in 1677, 1678, or 1679?

All three dates appear because they refer to different stages:

- **1677:** observing epoch at St Helena and the date Lacaille assigns to Halley’s planisphere insertion;
- **1678:** the planisphere was printed and circulated, and Halley returned to England;
- **1679:** formal publication of the catalogue.

The safest wording is: **conceived from Halley’s 1677 observations, first printed on his 1678 planisphere, formally catalogued in 1679**.

### 2. Twelve stars, nine stars, or twenty-five?

- Halley catalogued **twelve**.
- Lacaille says **nine of those twelve** were already in old catalogues/Bayer’s Argo.
- Burritt later drew or assigned **twenty-five**.

These figures answer different questions and should not be merged.

### 3. Was β Carinae or η Carinae the brightest/star “alpha”?

β Carinae was the Oak’s brightest and became α Roboris in subsequent usage. η Carinae was historically variable and later underwent a colossal eruption, but in Halley’s catalogue it was only fourth magnitude. Statements making η the original “alpha” confuse later fame with seventeenth-century brightness.

### 4. Did the Oak contain only Carina stars?

Not in every form. The core catalogue is usually reconstructed mainly in modern Carina, but atlas branches reach μ Velorum and, in some readings, γ Muscae. “All in Carina” is a simplification caused by treating a boundaryless picture as a fixed polygon.

### 5. Did Lacaille “abolish” it?

Lacaille rejected and omitted it; he had no international legal power to abolish constellations. His catalogue’s authority made his decision influential. Formal exhaustive standardization came only with the IAU. Thus “Lacaille abolished it” is shorthand, not institutional fact.

### 6. Did the IAU vote specifically against it in 1930?

No separate anti-Oak resolution was found. It was absent from the 1922 official list; Delporte’s 1930 publication completed the boundary partition. It was excluded, not tried and condemned by name.

### 7. Did Halley deceive readers?

Lacaille alleged that Halley withheld comparisons with old catalogues to make the Oak stars seem new. The omission is observable; deceptive intent is not. Halley plainly presented the catalogue as a southern supplement and the figure as a royal commemoration. The fairest label is **documented omission, disputed motive**.

### 8. Did flattering Charles secure Halley’s M.A.?

Charles ordered the Oxford degree, and the Oak flattered Charles. The surviving mandate praises Halley’s mathematical and astronomical achievement at St Helena. The stronger claim that the Oak itself bought the degree first becomes prominent in later biography and remains unproved.

### 9. Was Charles in the tree for twenty-four hours?

The early narratives describe concealment during the day. Modern site interpretation gives approximately fourteen hours. “Twenty-four hours” is a durable later formula but not a precise reconstruction.

### 10. Was Charles hiding from “Cromwell’s soldiers”?

Broadly, they were Parliamentarian forces under the Commonwealth regime and victorious command associated with Oliver Cromwell. Cromwell did not personally inspect Boscobel. The popular phrase is political shorthand.

### 11. Miaplacidus: Arabic or hybrid?

Its exact origin remains unresolved. A late appearance and mixed Arabic-Latin explanation make a pure ancient-Arabic derivation doubtful. It should not be confidently translated without qualification.

### 12. *Robur*, *Robor*, or *Robus*?

**Robur** is correct. *Robor* commonly results from visual misreading, OCR, or careless normalization; *robus* is likewise an error. A 2010 scholarly paper caption’s “Robor Caroli” and a museum exhibition’s “Robus Carolinum” show how easily the errors propagate.

### 13. *Robur Carolinum* or *Robur Caroli II*?

Both are historical. Halley’s form is *Robur Carolinum*; Bode’s 1801 plate is *Robur Caroli II*. Hevelius commonly used *Robur Caroli*. They are revisions of one figure, not three constellations.

### 14. Is Robur Carolinum classical mythology?

No. It is a modern political constellation superimposed on stars of a classical constellation. Retelling the Argonaut myths is necessary background, but attributing them directly to “Charles’s Oak” falsifies the chronology.

### 15. Chinese “Tseen She” and “Nan Chuen”

Allen’s placement of **Nan Chuen** was already marked doubtful. **Tseen She** is an asterism name incorrectly individualized as η Carinae. Modern Chinese reconstruction instead places η in Hǎi Shān and β/θ/ω in Nán Chuán.

### 16. Astrological ancientness

Robson’s character description and modern zodiacal degree tables are twentieth- and twenty-first-century inventions. They have cultural existence but no Ptolemaic pedigree.

### 17. Hoaxes

The dedicated searches found no substantial hoax specifically concerning Robur Carolinum. The recurring problems are ordinary transmission errors: false dates, Latin misspellings, exaggerated causal claims about Halley’s degree, overconfident star boundaries, and the misclassification of η Carinae’s eruption as a supernova.

## Open questions

1. A definitive modern identification of every one of Halley’s twelve entries should be produced from the Verbunt–van Gent HIP cross-matches, with proper-motion correction and comparison against Baily’s Lacaille-number identifications. Existing popular star lists often describe Bode’s larger tree instead.

2. The precise star membership of Bode’s plate XX needs a plate-specific catalogue collation. A drawn branch passing near a star is not automatically evidence that Bode counted it as part of the constellation.

3. The exact wording of Halley’s dedicatory explanation should be transcribed directly from multiple 1679 copies; digitization quality and Latin typography have encouraged variant quotations.

4. “Miaplacidus” still lacks a satisfactory documented etymological chain from first print to source phrase.

5. The alleged form **Robur Carolina** in James Cook’s work remains unverified from an atlas image.

6. The statement “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be reconciled with Bode’s published total of 17,240 stars. It may refer to a modern collection database rather than the *Uranographia* itself.

7. A full census of surviving globes bearing the Oak would likely add Dutch, German, French, and British examples beyond Cushee, Senex-derived globes, and the Bale gores.

8. No historical Hebrew, Kabbalistic, Indian, Hermetic, or alchemical identification of the exact Oak stars was located. Future claims require manuscript or printed-text citations, not symbolic resemblance.
