# Andromeda: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — directly attested by a primary text, catalogue, atlas, observation, institutional record, or physical object.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern inference from fragmentary literary, astronomical, linguistic, or archaeological evidence.
- **Tradition** — a transmitted identification or interpretation whose historical existence is documented, although its subject need not be factual.
- **Disputed claim** — an attribution or interpretation on which sources or scholars disagree.
- **Unverified claim** — a statement repeated in reference works but not traced here to adequate primary evidence.
- **Myth** — a narrative reported as mythology, with no assertion that its events occurred historically.
- **Legend** — a story localized or presented as historical by later communities without sufficient confirming evidence.
- **Modern invention** — a recent reinterpretation, new name, equivalence, or adaptation not inherited from the ancient tradition.

Dates for ancient authors are approximate. “Eratosthenes” below normally means the surviving pseudo-Eratosthenic *Catasterismi*: the original Hellenistic work is lost, and its contents survive principally in an epitome and the *Fragmenta Vaticana*. That textual distinction matters whenever “first attestation” is discussed.

## Basic identification

### Status

**Documented fact.** Andromeda is a living constellation, not an abolished one. It belongs to Ptolemy’s traditional 48 and to the International Astronomical Union’s 88 constellations. The IAU adopted the modern constellation list in 1922; Eugène Delporte subsequently drew exact boundaries, approved in 1928 and published in 1930.

- Official nominative: **Andromeda**
- Genitive: **Andromedae**
- IAU abbreviation: **And**
- Conventional English description: **the Chained Maiden**, **Chained Woman**, or **Chained Princess**
- Modern Spanish: **Andrómeda**
- French: **Andromède**
- Italian: **Andromeda**
- German: **Andromeda**, historically also **die angekettete Frau/Jungfrau**
- Present neighbors: Cassiopeia, Lacerta, Pegasus, Pisces, Triangulum, and Perseus
- Approximate modern extent: right ascension 22h57m–2h39m; declination +21.7°–+53.2°
- Area: approximately 722 square degrees, making it the nineteenth-largest IAU constellation

The IAU now treats a constellation as a bounded area, not merely a stick figure. Every celestial position within Delporte’s polygon is “in Andromeda” regardless of whether it belongs to the traditional woman’s body. [IAU constellation history and Andromeda data](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### The name

**Documented linguistic fact with interpretive uncertainty.** Greek forms include Ἀνδρομέδη (*Andromédē*, notably in Aratus) and Ἀνδρομέδα (*Androméda*, in Eratosthenes, Hipparchus, and Ptolemy). Latin adopted *Andromeda*.

The first element is securely related to ἀνήρ, genitive ἀνδρός, “man” or “husband.” The second is connected with μήδομαι/μέδομαι, “consider, plan, be mindful of,” or μέδω, “rule, protect.” Plausible glosses therefore include “ruler/protector of men” and “mindful of her husband.”

**Disputed etymology.** “Mindful of her husband” is grammatically possible and suits the catasterism in which her fidelity to Perseus earns celestial placement, but it need not be the name’s original semantic intention. “Ruler of men” is equally widespread. Assertions that the name means “half-man,” from Greek *anēr* plus *meta* or Latin *media*, are medieval or early-modern wordplay, not sound Greek etymology. [Greek-name analysis](https://www.behindthename.com/name/andromeda), [modern etymological summary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Andromeda), [Leshem on medieval reinterpretations](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/rest.70063)

**Disputed transmission.** Medieval forms such as *Asnade*, *Ansnade*, and descriptions of a husbandless woman have variously been derived from corrupted *Andromeda*, from Arabic *al-armala*, “widow,” or from a mistranslation of a Greek descriptive phrase. No single explanation accounts securely for every manuscript form. Nineteenth-century compilations often printed these variants as though they were independent ancient names; they are better understood as evidence for a difficult multilingual manuscript transmission. ### The traditional figure

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy catalogued 23 stars under Andromeda, all “formed” within the figure. The principal anatomical sequence is:

- α Andromedae: head; simultaneously Pegasus’s navel
- δ Andromedae: shoulder or base of the neck
- π, ε, ζ, η, and related stars: an arm
- β, μ, ν: girdle/waist
- γ Andromedae: foot
- ι, κ, λ, ψ and neighboring stars: the extended chained hand or arm
- fainter southern stars: robe, legs, feet, and hem

Exact modern identifications of several faint Ptolemaic entries depend on coordinate correction and are sometimes disputed. A modern machine-readable edition gives Andromeda as 23 formed stars and no “unformed” stars. [Ptolemy catalogue dataset](https://cdsarc.cds.unistra.fr/viz-bin/ReadMe/V/61?format=html&tex=true), [Almagest reconstruction](https://tjohncan.github.io/star-schema/)

### Bode’s Andromeda, 1801

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode’s *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* was published in Berlin in 1801. Its twenty enormous double-page copperplates mapped approximately 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. The companion text, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, appeared in parallel German and French.

Bode depicts Andromeda as a classically draped young woman, her body extending from Alpheratz through Mirach toward Almach, with conspicuous restraints. His Latin label is **ANDROMEDA**. The map combines ancient anatomy, Bayer letters, later catalogue stars, and telescopic objects.

**Important correction to the supplied lead.** I could not verify “830 stars in 109 groups” as a description of Andromeda or of Bode’s *Uranographia*. Bode’s documented headline count is 17,240 catalogue entries across the atlas. The wording may belong to a modern collection interface or a different counting scheme rather than Bode’s publication. [Bode companion catalogue](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036), [1801 atlas scan](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/download/pdf/3341734)

Bode also inserted **Honores Friderici**, honoring Frederick II of Prussia, in the region of Andromeda’s extended hand. To accommodate it, the traditional arm was distorted or displaced. That short-lived political constellation drew on stars historically assigned to Andromeda’s hand and on nearby Lacerta/Cassiopeia territory. It was never part of Ptolemy’s Andromeda and did not survive IAU standardization. [History of Honores Friderici](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Honores_Friderici)

### Alpheratz and the Great Square

**Documented fact.** α And is the northeastern corner of the Great Square of Pegasus. Ptolemy explicitly made it common to Andromeda and Pegasus: Andromeda’s head and the horse’s navel. Hyginus likewise says the same star joins the head of Andromeda to Pegasus’s belly.

Bayer consequently labelled it both α Andromedae and δ Pegasi in different contexts. Modern usage suppressed δ Pegasi when Delporte’s boundary placed the star wholly inside Andromeda.

**Contested point.** It is often said that the IAU “transferred” Alpheratz from Pegasus to Andromeda in 1930. That is oversimplified. The star had belonged to both figures for nearly two millennia and possessed an established α And designation. Delporte’s exclusive areas forced a choice; the boundary retained it in Andromeda while the visual Great Square continued to use it. No located IAU minute supplies a mythological or aesthetic reason beyond the necessity of unique territorial assignment.

### Almach

**Documented fact.** γ Andromedae is a celebrated visual double: the bright orange primary and blue-appearing companion provide a strong color contrast. The companion is itself a closer multiple system, making Almach a hierarchical multiple star.

**Name-history caution.** “Almach means Andromeda’s foot” is wrong. The star marks a foot in the Greek figure, but its proper name derives from the Arabic caracal asterism. This is a common example of Arabic indigenous sky lore surviving inside a European constellation.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

**Documented fact.** Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria* assigned Greek letters through ψ, with superscripts where needed. The letters do not form a perfectly strict brightness ranking. Flamsteed numbers run historically from 1 through 66 Andromedae and order stars largely by increasing right ascension as understood in his catalogue.

Boundary standardization produced anomalies:

- 54 Andromedae is now **φ Persei**, inside Perseus.
- 60 Andromedae is now **V520 Persei**.
- 39 Andromedae became associated with **69 Piscium/σ Piscium** in Pisces.
- Conversely, some modern Andromeda stars had descriptions or designations tied to neighboring figures.
- Alpheratz lost practical use of Bayer’s alternate **δ Pegasi**.

These are not moving stars; they are fossils of overlapping figures and later boundary choices. [Flamsteed designation table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_stars_with_Flamsteed_designations), [historical star list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stars_in_Andromeda)

### Internal asterisms

- **Andromeda’s Chain/backbone:** Alpheratz–δ–Mirach–Almach, the long bent line commonly used to identify the constellation.
- **Great Square of Pegasus:** Alpheratz plus Markab, Scheat, and Algenib. It crosses the modern Pegasus–Andromeda boundary.
- **The girdle:** β, μ, ν And, important because ν is used to locate M31.
- **The classical arms/chains:** lines toward π/ε/ζ and ι/κ/λ/ψ; artists disagree about which arm is chained and how the body faces.
- **“Andromeda Cross”:** a later pattern sometimes described using β, γ, δ as an upright and α/κ as a transverse. **Tradition, weakly documented:** it never achieved standard constellation status.

### M31: the Great Nebula and Andromeda Galaxy

**Documented fact, c. 964.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī recorded a *laṭkha saḥābiyya*, a “nebulous smear/patch,” usually rendered “little cloud,” near the fourteenth Ptolemaic star and at the mouth of an indigenous Arabic Fish. He also plotted the patch in at least part of the manuscript tradition. This is the earliest secure written description of M31.

**Disputed claim.** Proposals that Babylonian texts describe M31 as the head of Anunitu or as the “Rainbow” are speculative. No cuneiform passage securely describes a nonstellar fuzzy object at M31’s position. Identifying an asterism’s “head” with the galaxy rather than a star is a modern reconstruction.

**Documented fact, 1612/1614.** Simon Marius observed M31 telescopically on 15 December 1612 and published the observation in *Mundus Iovialis* (1614), comparing its pale glow to a candle seen through translucent horn. He did not discover an object unknown to all earlier observers; he supplied the first securely published telescopic description.

**Documented fact, 1764.** Charles Messier entered it as M31. Messier credited Marius because al-Ṣūfī’s notice was not then part of the commonly used European discovery record.

**Documented nineteenth-century observations.**

- William Herschel treated it as a nebula and attempted to infer its depth and structure.
- William Parsons, third Earl of Rosse, and later observers saw indications of structure.
- Isaac Roberts’s long-exposure photograph of 1888 clearly showed spiral form.
- Nova searches and spectroscopy fed the dispute over whether spirals were nearby clouds or remote “island universes.”

**Documented fact, 1885.** A transient in the nucleus, S Andromedae/SN 1885A, reached naked-eye brightness. Ernst Hartwig’s verified observation of 20 August produced the effective discovery announcement, although Ludovic Gully and Isaac Ward reported earlier sightings after the announcement. At the time it was called a nova; it is now classified as a peculiar Type I supernova. Its extraordinary apparent relation to the nebula was used on both sides of the scale debate.

**Documented fact, 1917.** Heber Curtis found novae in photographic plates of M31 and argued that their faintness implied an enormous distance, supporting the “island universe” interpretation.

**Documented fact, 1920.** Harlow Shapley and Curtis publicly debated the scale of the universe. The debate did not itself settle M31’s nature.

**Documented fact, 1923–1925.** On a Mount Wilson plate taken 5–6 October 1923, Edwin Hubble first marked an object “N,” then recognized it as a Cepheid and changed the notation to “VAR!” Continued measurements of V1 and other Cepheids established a distance far beyond contemporary estimates of the Milky Way’s dimensions. Hubble announced the result to the American Astronomical Society through a paper read in January 1925.

**Qualification.** Hubble’s original distance, roughly 900,000 light-years, was much too small because the Cepheid calibration was incomplete, but it was decisively extragalactic. The modern distance is about 2.5 million light-years.

**Documented modern fact.** M31 is a large spiral galaxy and the most massive or near-most-massive major member of the Local Group, accompanied by M32, M110, and many dwarf satellites. [Al-Ṣūfī study](https://www.eso.org/sci/publications/messenger/archive/no.49-sep87/messenger-no49.pdf), [NASA on Hubble’s V1](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/hubble-views-the-star-that-changed-the-universe/), [SN 1885 review](https://adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1985ApJ...295..287D)

### Variable stars and transients

- **R Andromedae:** Mira variable; the “R” illustrates Argelander’s convention of starting variable-star lettering at R.
- **Z Andromedae:** prototype of the Z And class of symbiotic variables, a cool giant interacting with a hot compact companion.
- **λ Andromedae:** prototype of active, spotted Lambda Andromedae binaries.
- **α² Canum Venaticorum-type variability in Alpheratz:** chemical peculiarities and rotational modulation.
- **ο Andromedae:** variable Be multiple system.
- **ζ Andromedae:** magnetically active RS CVn binary.
- **SN 1885A/S Andromedae:** first observed extragalactic supernova, although it was not understood as such in 1885.
- **M31N 2008-12a:** remarkable recurrent nova in M31, erupting at intervals close to one year.
- Numerous novae, eclipsing binaries, Cepheids, RR Lyrae variables, and microlensing candidates have been catalogued in M31. These are physically in the galaxy but appear within the terrestrial constellation.

### Meteor showers

**Documented fact.** The **Andromedids**, formerly called the Bielids, derive from the debris of the disintegrated comet 3D/Biela. Their radiant historically lay near γ Andromedae, hence the name. Spectacular storms occurred in 1872 and 1885. The radiant has migrated because of planetary perturbations; modern weak returns are often associated with a radiant in or near Cassiopeia.

**Contested popular error.** The Perseid meteor shower is not an Andromeda shower merely because Perseus borders Andromeda. Its radiant lies in Perseus and its parent is 109P/Swift–Tuttle. [Andromedid dynamical study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.5980)

## Chronology of attestation

### Mesopotamian background, second to first millennia BCE

**Documented fact.** Babylonian lists place the goddess **Anunitu/Anunītu**, an aspect or title of Ishtar associated with Akkad, in a region covering portions of modern Pisces and Andromeda. Other nearby Babylonian figures include the Great Swallow and the Field.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Because Greek Andromeda, Pegasus, Pisces, and related constellations occupy overlapping territory, scholars have long looked for Mesopotamian prototypes. The correspondence is positional, not an attested translation: no Babylonian text says that Anunitu was chained, sacrificed to a sea monster, or rescued by a Perseus-equivalent.

**Disputed diffusion theory.** A once-popular theory derived the entire Perseus–Andromeda story from Marduk’s defeat of Tiamat. Similarities—hero, threatened order, sea monster—are broad, while the personal and narrative correspondences are weak. It should not be presented as established descent.

### Egypt

**Documented fact.** Egyptian astronomical ceilings and decanal lists possess their own figures. In the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, Egyptian monuments such as the Dendera zodiac incorporated Greco-Babylonian zodiacal imagery.

**Negative finding.** No securely identified pharaonic Egyptian constellation corresponding specifically to Andromeda, the chained princess, was located. Claims that a Dendera figure “is Andromeda” generally impose later Greek figures on an Egyptian-Hellenistic composite monument.

### Late sixth or fifth century BCE: visual and dramatic evidence

**Documented fact.** The Perseus–Andromeda episode appears in Greek vase painting by the later sixth or early fifth century BCE. Early pictures may show Andromeda tied between posts rather than chained to a seaside rock; Perseus confronts the sea creature with stones or weapons.

**Documented but lost literature.** Sophocles wrote an *Andromeda*, probably in the mid-fifth century BCE; its date and plot details are uncertain. A supposed *Andromeda* by Phrynichus is disputed.

**Documented fact, 412 BCE.** Euripides’ *Andromeda* was produced with *Helen*. The play is lost but unusually well represented by fragments, ancient quotations, vase paintings, and parody in Aristophanes’ *Thesmophoriazusae* (411 BCE). Its famous opening presented Andromeda alone and lamenting while Echo answered her. Perseus arrived through the air, saw what seemed a statue, fell in love, and offered rescue.

**First-attestation caution.** The constellation family may be older than the surviving literary story, but neither Homer nor securely surviving Hesiodic poetry tells Andromeda’s myth. Assertions that Homer knew “Andromeda” confuse later constellations with Homeric ones.

### Eudoxus and Aratus, fourth–third centuries BCE

**Documented through later transmission.** Eudoxus of Cnidus, c. 350 BCE, is the earliest securely reported astronomical witness to the constellation group under the mythological names Andromeda, Cepheus, Cassiopeia, Perseus, and Cetus.

**Documented fact, c. 275 BCE.** Aratus’ *Phaenomena* describes Andromeda beneath her mother, with her outstretched arms and the shared star connecting her head to the horse. Aratus assumes the audience knows the figure rather than retelling the entire rescue.

### Pseudo-Eratosthenes, Hellenistic source preserved later

**Myth/catasterism.** *Catasterismi* 17 says Andromeda was exposed to the monster because of Cassiopeia’s boast; Perseus rescued her; Andromeda elected to follow him rather than remain with her parents. Athena placed her among the stars as a memorial to her devotion. The neighboring family constellations turn one narrative into a celestial tableau.

The source invokes Euripides or Sophocles in parts of this cycle, but the surviving epitome’s dependence and exact citations vary by recension.

### First century BCE–first century CE: localization at Joppa

**Documented geographic tradition.** Pseudo-Scylax, Strabo, Pomponius Mela, Josephus, and later Pliny and Pausanias associate Joppa/Iope—modern Jaffa—with Andromeda.

**Legend.** Josephus (*Jewish War* 3.420) says marks of Andromeda’s chains remained on the coastal rocks. Pliny (*Natural History* 5.69; 9.11) reports Joppa as the location and says the bones of the monster were brought to Rome and exhibited by Marcus Scaurus. Pausanias (4.35.9) describes red water at Joppa where Perseus supposedly washed off the monster’s blood.

These reports document Roman-period tourism, local identity, and relic display. They do not establish that a historical rescue occurred there.

**Alternative rationalization.** Conon’s *Narrations* 40, preserved by Photius, converts the monster into a ship named *Ketos*. Cepheus arranged for Phoinix to abduct Andromeda, promised to Phineus; Perseus intercepted the vessel. The crew were “petrified” figuratively by terror. This is an ancient euhemerizing variant, not the earliest form.

### Hyginus

**Myth, *Astronomica* 2.11 and 3.10.** Hyginus repeats the exposure, rescue, marriage, and celestial reward. Minerva/Athena sets Andromeda among the stars because she followed Perseus and refused another husband. Book 3 anatomizes the stellar figure and states that her head star is also Pegasus’s navel.

**Myth, *Fabulae* 64.** Cassiopeia ranked her daughter’s beauty above the Nereids. Neptune demanded Andromeda be thrown to the *cetus*. Perseus, flying with Mercury’s sandals, saved her. When Cepheus and Andromeda’s former fiancé **Agenor** plotted to kill him, Perseus petrified them with Medusa’s head and returned home with Andromeda.

This differs from Apollodorus and Ovid, where the rival is normally Phineus. “Agenor versus Phineus” is a genuine ancient variant, not merely a modern error. [Hyginus, *Fabulae* 64, Latin](https://www.lateinlex.de/?call=Puc&permalink=Hyg_fab_64), [Hyginus, *Astronomica* 3.10](https://www.lateinlex.de/?call=Puc&permalink=Hyg_astr_3_10)

### Ovid, c. 8 CE

**Myth, *Metamorphoses* 4.663–752.** Perseus sees Andromeda chained by the arms to rock in Ethiopia, at first mistaking her for marble except for wind-blown hair and tears. Ammon’s oracle has condemned the innocent daughter for her mother’s speech. Perseus negotiates marriage with Cepheus and Cassiopeia, then kills the sea monster by aerial sword-fighting rather than by showing it Medusa’s head. He lays Medusa’s head on sea plants, producing coral.

**Myth, *Metamorphoses* 5.1–235.** At the wedding, Phineus attacks, claiming Andromeda as his prior bride. Perseus eventually displays Medusa’s head and petrifies Phineus and his supporters. Andromeda herself has little agency in Ovid’s battle narrative.

**Tradition, *Heroides* 15.35–36.** Ovid’s Sappho refers to “dark/dusky Andromeda” (*fusca Andromede*) as attractive to Perseus. This is central to the modern color controversy but does not supply a precise modern racial classification. [Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4, Latin and English](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0028%3Abook%3D4), [book 4 episode outline](https://ovid.lib.virginia.edu/trans/Metamorph4.htm)

### Manilius, early first century CE

**Documented astrological poetry.** *Astronomica* 5.538–618 makes the rising Andromeda produce people who punish, imprison, torture, execute, or rescue prisoners. Manilius turns the chained maiden into an astrological symbol of bondage and deliverance. He gives an elaborate poetic account of Perseus’s combat.

### Pseudo-Apollodorus, first–second century CE

**Myth, *Bibliotheca* 2.4.3.** Cassiopeia competes with the Nereids in beauty. Poseidon sends flood and monster; Ammon says relief requires Andromeda’s exposure. The Ethiopians compel Cepheus to bind her. Perseus falls in love and promises to kill the monster for her hand. After he succeeds, Phineus—Cepheus’s brother and Andromeda’s former betrothed—plots against him; Perseus petrifies the conspirators.

**Myth, 2.4.4 and 2.4.5.** Perseus takes Andromeda to Argos. Their children include Perses, left with Cepheus and treated as ancestor of the Persian kings, and Alcaeus, Sthenelus, Heleus, Mestor, Electryon, and Gorgophone. Through Electryon and Alcmene, Andromeda enters Heracles’ genealogy. [Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.4.3–4](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Apollodorus2.html)

### Pausanias, second century CE

Apart from the Joppa blood-spring legend, Pausanias connects the Perseid dynasty with Argos, Mycenae, Midea, Tiryns, and later descendants. Andromeda functions mainly as Perseus’s wife and ancestral mother rather than an independent cult figure.

### Nonnus, fifth century CE

**Literary myth.** Nonnus repeatedly uses Andromeda and Perseus as celestial and erotic exempla in the *Dionysiaca*. The rescue is familiar enough to serve as comparison rather than needing full narration. Nonnus belongs to the late-antique reception of an already canonical constellation myth, not its formation.

### Al-Ṣūfī, 964

**Documented astronomical synthesis.** Al-Ṣūfī preserved Ptolemy’s Andromeda as *al-Marʾa al-Musalsala*, corrected magnitudes, drew it twice—sky view and globe view—and described indigenous Arabic asterisms occupying the same stars. His record of M31 is a landmark independent observation. [Al-Ṣūfī constellation overview](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_constellations.htm)

### Medieval Latin and Hebrew transmission

Arabic versions of Ptolemy and al-Ṣūfī entered Latin Europe, especially through twelfth-century Spain. Gerard of Cremona’s Latin *Almagest* and the *Liber de locis stellarum fixarum* tradition transmitted Arabic names, sometimes corruptly.

**Documented iconographic anomaly.** Some medieval Andromedas are masculine, bearded, partly male, or anatomically ambiguous. Recent scholarship connects this with difficult textual instructions, copying from reversed globe images, and phrases interpreting the name as a woman lacking or not seeing a man. It is unsafe to reduce every example to one “translation error”; iconographic and grammatical causes interacted.

### 1515: Dürer, Stabius, and Heinfogel

**Documented fact.** Albrecht Dürer engraved northern and southern celestial hemispheres designed with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel. Andromeda appears in the northern map within the Ptolemaic/Aratean tradition. The maps helped stabilize Renaissance constellation imagery and influenced later printed astronomy.

### 1603: Bayer

**Documented fact.** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* offered a full-page Andromeda and its stellar table. It systematized Greek-letter designations and included α as common with Pegasus. Bayer’s maiden is richly draped, with arms extended and restrained. [Bayer’s stellar-table explanation](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/10-bayer-johann-explicatio-uranometrias/)

### 1627: Schiller

**Documented fact.** Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, produced with assistance from Bayer and engraved by Lucas Kilian, replaced pagan northern figures with New Testament subjects. Andromeda became **Sepulchrum Christi**, the Sepulchre of Christ—the Holy Tomb—not a saint. The replacement failed to displace the classical name. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/), [Linda Hall identification](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/13-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum-concavum/)

### 1690: Hevelius

**Documented fact.** Johannes Hevelius’s *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*, published posthumously at Gdańsk/Danzig by Elisabeth Hevelius, shows Andromeda in mirror-reversed globe orientation. It incorporates more stars than the ancient catalogue while preserving the stretched classical body. [Hevelius Andromeda plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AJohannes_Hevelius_-_Prodromus_Astronomia_-_Volume_III_%22Firmamentum_Sobiescianum%2C_sive_uranographia%22_-_Tavola_V_-_Andromeda.jpg)

### Flamsteed, 1725–1729

Flamsteed’s *Historia Coelestis Britannica* and posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* greatly increased the number of catalogued Andromeda stars. An 1823 reference counted 84 under Flamsteed against Ptolemy’s 23, Tycho’s 22, and Bayer’s 27; counts vary with edition and inclusion rules.

Flamsteed sought to correct Bayer’s figures and coordinates, but the engraved human figures were criticized artistically. Andromeda was among those retouched in later atlas states. [Historical count](https://jimclifford.ca/britannica-articles-2/a/EB.11/192015835/343.html)

### Bode, 1782–1801

Bode’s smaller *Vorstellung der Gestirne* appeared in 1782 and was intended for knowledgeable amateurs. In 1787 he proposed **Friedrichs Ehre/Honores Friderici**, honoring the recently deceased Frederick the Great. The 1801 *Uranographia* was his monumental culmination; the political trophy occupies part of the old arm region. [Heidelberg Bode collection](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/), [Bode atlas context](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/einl.html)

### Nineteenth-century popular reception

- **Alexander Jamieson, 1822:** *A Celestial Atlas* adapted Bode’s compositions to a smaller format.
- **Urania’s Mirror, first issued 1824:** translucent-punched constellation cards copied Jamieson closely; Andromeda appears with neighboring figures and the obsolete *Gloria Frederici*.
- **Elijah Burritt, 1833:** *Geography of the Heavens* and atlas further popularized a simplified Bode/Jamieson sky.
- **Argelander, 1843:** *Uranometria Nova* emphasized stars visible to the naked eye and practical constellation outlines.
- **Heis, 1872:** *Atlas Coelestis Novus* continued the scientific simplification, reducing reliance on elaborate human anatomy.

By the later nineteenth century, scientific atlases increasingly omitted pictorial bodies while popular atlases retained the chained princess.

### IAU, 1922–1930

**Documented fact.**

1. Rome, 1922: the IAU regularized 88 constellation names and three-letter abbreviations.
2. Cambridge, 1925: Delporte presented a boundary method.
3. Leiden, 1928: the IAU approved the definitive northern boundaries.
4. 1930: Delporte published *Délimitation scientifique des constellations*.

The lines followed constant right ascension and declination for epoch B1875.0 and were later precessed for modern coordinate use. Andromeda was kept; Honores Friderici was not. Alpheratz fell wholly within Andromeda. Former Andromeda-numbered stars south or east of the polygon became parts of Pisces or Perseus. [Delporte bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

## Classical myths and legends

## The sacrifice and rescue

**Myth; earliest secure narrative evidence: fifth-century BCE visual art and tragedy.** Cassiopeia claimed that she or Andromeda surpassed the Nereids. The offended sea nymphs appealed to Poseidon, who sent flood and a *kētos*, a great sea creature. An oracle—usually Ammon—declared that the country could be saved only by exposing Andromeda. Perseus encountered her after killing Medusa, fell in love, bargained for marriage, slew the monster, and released her.

Source differences matter:

- Euripidean reconstruction: aerial arrival, love at first sight, Echo scene.
- Pseudo-Eratosthenes: emphasis on fidelity and celestial reward.
- Ovid: sword combat and the origin of coral.
- Apollodorus: Ethiopians compel Cepheus; formal sworn marriage bargain.
- Hyginus: Mercury’s sandals; rival Agenor rather than Phineus.
- Later art: frequently substitutes Pegasus for the winged sandals, although the principal ancient narratives do not make Perseus ride Pegasus during the rescue.

## The catasterism

**Myth; surviving source pseudo-Eratosthenes 17, followed by Hyginus *Astronomica* 2.11.** Athena/Minerva places Andromeda among the stars because she left her parents willingly for Perseus and remained faithful to him. In the sky she remains with Perseus, Cepheus, Cassiopeia, Cetus, and Pegasus.

**Variant/interpretive point.** The chains commemorate danger, while the constellation’s placement commemorates fidelity. The humiliating pose is therefore simultaneously punishment, memorial, and honor.

## The rival bridegroom

**Myth.**

- **Phineus version:** Apollodorus 2.4.3 and Ovid *Metamorphoses* 5.1–235. Phineus was Andromeda’s uncle or former fiancé. He attacks the wedding party and is petrified by Medusa’s head.
- **Agenor version:** Hyginus *Fabulae* 64. Agenor conspires with Cepheus to murder Perseus and is petrified.
- **Phoinix/Phineus version:** Conon 40 rationalizes the episode as a planned abduction by ship.

The identities are genuinely competing. Treating Agenor as a mere typo for Phineus is possible but unproved.

## Marriage, migration, and dynasty

**Myth; Apollodorus 2.4.4–5 and later genealogists.** Perseus brings Andromeda to Seriphos and Argos. They become associated with Tiryns and Mycenae. Their children form the Perseid dynasty. Perses remains with Cepheus and becomes legendary ancestor of the Persians; Electryon becomes grandfather of Heracles; Gorgophone is remembered as a notable daughter and, in Pausanias, the first widow said to have remarried.

Andromeda’s role after marriage is primarily genealogical. No independent ancient adventure-cycle centered on her survives.

## Joppa and the relics

**Legend; securely present by the Hellenistic/Roman period.** Coastal Joppa displayed chain marks, a blood-colored spring, and an alleged monster skeleton. Roman authors could reconcile Ethiopia and Joppa by calling Cepheus an Ethiopian ruler at Joppa or by treating “Ethiopia” broadly.

The legend’s persistence is evidence for local civic appropriation and ancient tourism, not proof of the myth’s historicity. The similarity to Jonah’s Joppa and great fish may have helped later Jewish and Christian association, but direct derivation in either direction is not demonstrated.

## The whole royal family in the sky

**Mythic astronomical tradition.** Cepheus sits crowned; Cassiopeia sits on her chair; Andromeda is extended in chains; Perseus approaches with Medusa’s head; Cetus waits below; Pegasus adjoins her head. The proximity is older than the surviving pseudo-Eratosthenic epitome and is already implicit in Eudoxus and Aratus.

**Qualification.** Pegasus’s presence does not prove the later popular image of Perseus rescuing Andromeda while mounted on the horse. Ancient sources normally give Perseus winged sandals; Pegasus belongs principally to Bellerophon’s cycle.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Indigenous Arabian asterisms

Al-Ṣūfī carefully distinguished translated Greek figures from older Arabic star lore.

**Documented tradition.**

- A large **Fish** crossed the region of Andromeda and extended toward Pisces. M31 lay at or near its mouth.
- A **Horse** or horse-related group occupied stars around Andromeda/Pegasus.
- A **Camel** was recognized in another grouping across this northern region.
- The **caracal/desert lynx**, *al-ʿanāq*, supplied the modern name Almach.
- *Kaff al-Musalsala*, “hand of the chained woman,” belongs to the translated Greek anatomical tradition.

The occasional label “chained sea calf,” Latinized by Bayer as *Vitulus marinus catenatus*, reflects hybrid Arabic-European transmission. It should not be treated as the canonical Arabic name of the entire constellation.

### Chinese astronomy

The modern Andromeda polygon cuts across the Black Tortoise of the North and White Tiger of the West. Its stars belonged to several asterisms rather than a single princess:

- **奎宿 Kuí Xiù**, the Legs/Stride mansion: stars across Andromeda and Pisces.
- **壁宿 Bì Xiù**, Wall mansion: α Andromedae and γ Pegasi form the eastern wall of the celestial enclosure.
- **天大將軍 Tiāndàjiāngjūn**, Great General of Heaven: a large military asterism incorporating γ And and other stars.
- **螣蛇 Téngshé**, Flying Serpent: numerous northern Andromeda stars.
- **天廄 Tiānjiù**, Celestial Stable, and associated minor groups.

Modern Chinese **仙女座**, “constellation of the immortal/fairy maiden,” is a translation-equivalent for the Western IAU constellation, not the ancient name of a unified Chinese figure. [Traditional Chinese mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_in_Chinese_astronomy), [Dunhuang sky study](https://dds.sciengine.com/cfs/files/pdfs/1440-2807/A0F21360559C4EB4BEECF67C477064E5.pdf)

### Indian astronomy

**Negative finding with modern overlay.** Andromeda lies well north of the ecliptic; no classical nakshatra corresponds to the whole constellation. Portions near Pisces may be discussed relative to the lunar mansions, but identifying Andromeda wholesale as **Devayani** is a modern popular mapping, not an attested early Sanskrit constellation equivalence. No primary Sanskrit source establishing that identity was found.

### Hebrew astronomy and Kabbalah

**Documented reception, limited evidence for independence.** Medieval Jewish astronomers working in Arabic and Hebrew transmitted Ptolemaic constellations and Arabic star terminology. Abraham bar Ḥiyya and related traditions contain paraphrases interpreted as “the woman without a husband” or “who has not seen a man.”

**Negative finding.** No ancient biblical Hebrew constellation securely corresponding to Andromeda has been identified. Attempts to read the chained woman into Job, Isaiah, or the *Mazzaroth* are modern Christian-Hebraist constructions.

**Kabbalistic claims.** Modern esoteric works sometimes attach Andromeda to sefirot, Hebrew tribes, zodiacal decans, or redemptive feminine symbolism. I found no stable premodern Kabbalistic consensus or primary *Zoharic* mapping. These should be labelled **modern invention or unverified esoteric tradition** unless accompanied by a specific manuscript citation.

### Christianized sky

**Documented fact.** Schiller’s 1627 replacement is **Sepulchrum Christi**, Christ’s Sepulchre or Holy Tomb. Schiller suppressed pagan figures throughout the sky, replacing the zodiac with apostles and northern constellations with New Testament persons or objects. The system was admired as an ingenious devotional atlas but not adopted in technical astronomy.

### Astrology

**Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* 1.9.** The stars in Andromeda were assigned planetary natures according to anatomical location. The constellation’s bright stars were commonly treated as resembling Venus, with some stars showing mixtures involving Mars or Mercury depending on recension and commentator.

**Later fixed-star astrology.**

- Alpheratz: commonly Venus/Jupiter or Venus/Mercury qualities; freedom, honor, motion, or—through the chained figure—release.
- Mirach: Venus-like; beauty, marriage, artistic or receptive qualities.
- Almach: Venus-like, sometimes Venus/Mars; artistic or martial interpretations.
- Andromeda as a whole: bondage, threatened innocence, rescue, fidelity, and liberation.

These are **documented astrological traditions**, not empirically established stellar effects.

**Behenian stars.** None of the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars is an Andromeda star. Lists that make Alpheratz or Mirach “Behenian” are expanded modern lists.

**Lunar mansions.** Alpheratz belongs in later Arabic mansion schemes to the region of **al-Fargh al-Muʾakhkhar**, the Hind Spout of the Water Jar, normally using α And and γ Peg. This is a positional lunar-mansion association, not an Andromeda myth.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

Renaissance and modern occult authors use the chained maiden allegorically for imprisoned soul, prima materia, matter awaiting liberation, or the feminine soul rescued by heroic intellect.

**Negative finding.** No canonical ancient Hermetic text was found that identifies Andromeda’s stars with a specific alchemical operation. Most explicit “alchemical Andromeda” schemes appear post-medieval and should be labelled interpretive invention, not ancient Hermetic astronomy.

## Boundary history

### Ptolemy

The constellation was a list of 23 stars attached to a body, not a polygon. α And belonged explicitly to both Andromeda and Pegasus. Empty sky and stars outside the body had no exclusive territorial owner.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer preserved overlap, supplied letters, and expanded the charted field. His artistic boundary was the maiden’s outline, not a legal border. α remained both α And and δ Peg.

### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius added stars from his observations and redrew the woman in reversed globe view. His catalogues still permitted figurative overlap. The hand occupied space later used by Honores Friderici.

### Flamsteed

Flamsteed catalogued many more stars under Andromeda; later Flamsteed numbers became quasi-territorial in practice. Yet no internationally binding border prevented a star from being described under a neighboring figure.

### Lacaille

Lacaille’s work chiefly reorganized the far southern sky. He did not invent or abolish Andromeda, and no major northern boundary intervention by him is documented. Invoking him as an Andromeda boundary-maker would overstate his role.

### Bode

Bode’s imagery admitted Honores Friderici between Andromeda, Lacerta, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, Cygnus, and Pegasus. This effectively took traditional hand stars from Andromeda in one of Bode’s own visual arrangements, although other plates or editions could remain inconsistent.

### Delporte

The 1930 polygon made every position exclusive. Consequences included:

- α And became solely Andromeda, ending formal use of δ Peg.
- The Great Square remained a cross-boundary asterism.
- Flamsteed stars now in Perseus or Pisces retained fossil “And” numbers in historical catalogues.
- Honores Friderici disappeared, its stars being divided principally among Andromeda, Cassiopeia, Lacerta, Cepheus, and Cygnus depending on position.
- No physical star changed; nomenclatural jurisdiction changed.

## Iconography

### Classical antiquity

**Kugel globe, probably second–first century BCE.** A small ancient celestial globe shows classical constellation figures, including a female Andromeda-like figure. Identifications are reconstructed from worn relief and should not be described as perfectly certain.

**Farnese Atlas, Roman copy traditionally dated second century CE.** Atlas carries a celestial globe bearing the classical constellations. Andromeda appears as a clothed woman with raised or extended arms; a fish-like attribute has been argued for in the same region. The sculpture is in the Museo Archeologico Nazionale, Naples. Dating and dependence on Hipparchus have been extensively debated.

**Vase painting, sixth–fourth centuries BCE.** Scenes include Andromeda tied to stakes, servants preparing her exposure, Perseus fighting the monster, and the royal family. These precede many surviving literary accounts and are therefore crucial evidence for the myth’s early form.

**Roman wall painting and mosaics, first century BCE onward.** Pompeii and later sites repeatedly show the rescue, usually privileging the moment Perseus approaches or frees Andromeda. The scene became a standard image of heroic erotic rescue.

### Manuscripts

**Leiden Aratea, c. 816, probably made in the Carolingian court region and now Leiden University Library.** Andromeda is shown in the illustrated Germanicus tradition. Her stars are marked upon the body; the design transmits a late-antique pictorial archetype.

**Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, tenth century onward.** Each constellation normally appears twice, as seen in the sky and on a globe. Andromeda is clothed in Islamic manuscript convention, and some copies add indigenous Fish, Horse, or Camel patterns. The “little cloud” of M31 appears in the textual/cartographic tradition. [Al-Ṣūfī manuscript example](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABook_of_the_Fixed_Stars_Auv0175_andromeda.jpg)

**Ratdolt Hyginus, Venice, 1482.** Erhard Ratdolt’s illustrated *Poeticon Astronomicon* contains an influential woodcut Andromeda. Its body/star relationships do not always agree with Hyginus’s anatomical catalogue, showing that mythic recognizability could outweigh precision.

### Printed maps and atlases

- **Dürer/Stabius/Heinfogel, Nuremberg, 1515:** northern hemisphere woodcut.
- **Bayer, Augsburg, 1603:** full-page copperplate in *Uranometria*.
- **Schiller, Augsburg, 1627:** Andromeda replaced by Christ’s Sepulchre.
- **Hevelius, Gdańsk, published 1690:** reversed, richly engraved maiden.
- **Flamsteed, London, 1729:** expanded stellar field and artistic figure.
- **Bode, Berlin, 1782:** smaller practical atlas.
- **Bode, Berlin, 1801:** monumental draped and chained figure, crowded with catalogue stars and neighboring Honores Friderici.
- **Jamieson, London, 1822:** compressed Bode-derived composition.
- **Urania’s Mirror, London, 1824/25:** pierced card intended to be held to light; derived substantially from Jamieson.
- **Burritt, United States, from 1833:** popular educational simplification.
- **Argelander, Bonn, 1843:** scientifically cleaner naked-eye atlas.
- **Heis, Cologne, 1872:** practical observational atlas.

### Coins, architecture, and monumental art

**Ancient coins.** Perseus and Medusa are common; securely identifying small female or marine scenes as Andromeda requires caution. Joppa’s civic coinage used maritime and Perseid associations, but not every chained or marine female is Andromeda.

**Cathedral portals and calendars.** The rescue archetype influenced medieval monster-slayer imagery, particularly later comparisons with Saint George. Direct claims that a particular unlabeled portal “is Andromeda” are often speculative unless inscriptions or iconographic sequences identify her.

**Ceilings.** Renaissance and Baroque palace ceilings frequently incorporated the Perseus–Andromeda rescue; examples belong to broader Ovidian decorative cycles rather than scientific star mapping.

**Negative finding.** No cathedral program was located that securely labels a constellation Andromeda while preserving the ancient chained maiden as part of a standard Christian calendar.

## People, events, and works

### Navigation and observing

Andromeda is circumpolar only at sufficiently northern latitudes and is less useful for direct latitude determination than Polaris. Alpheratz and the Great Square nevertheless served as seasonal and directional landmarks in northern navigation.

**Qualification.** Claims that a named ancient explorer “steered by Andromeda” are rarely source-specific. General use of bright northern stars is plausible; individual attribution requires a logbook or navigational manual.

### Discoverers and observers

- **Al-Ṣūfī:** earliest secure written observer of M31.
- **Simon Marius:** first published telescopic description.
- **Charles Messier:** catalogued M31 and M32; observed the companion now called M110.
- **William Herschel:** surveyed Andromeda nebulae and discovered objects including NGC 891.
- **Caroline Herschel:** contributed to the nebular survey tradition, although not every later attribution in Andromeda is hers.
- **Ernst Hartwig:** effective announcing discoverer of S Andromedae.
- **Isaac Roberts:** produced the transformative 1888 photograph of M31.
- **Vesto Slipher:** measured M31’s large negative radial velocity in 1912, important evidence for spiral-nebula dynamics.
- **Heber Curtis:** used M31 novae to argue for an island universe.
- **Ernst Öpik:** produced an independent large-distance estimate in 1922.
- **Edwin Hubble:** identified Cepheids and established M31’s extragalactic distance.
- **Walter Baade:** resolved stellar populations and revised the Cepheid distance scale during the 1940s–1950s.

### Literature

- Sophocles, lost *Andromeda*
- Euripides, lost *Andromeda* (412 BCE)
- Ennius and Accius, fragmentary Latin tragic adaptations
- Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4–5
- Manilius, *Astronomica* 5
- Pierre Corneille, *Andromède* (1650), a machine-play with music and spectacular stage effects
- Calderón de la Barca, *Fortunas de Andrómeda y Perseo* (1653)

### Music

- Francesco Manelli, *Andromeda* (1637), associated with the opening of Venice’s first public opera house.

### Ships, missions, buildings, and technology

- **HMS Andromeda** was used repeatedly by the Royal Navy, most notably for Leander-class frigates.
- Numerous commercial, naval, and fictional vessels bear the name.
- **Andromeda Theatre**, observatories, apartment buildings, and hotels commonly invoke either the princess or galaxy; most lack direct constellation-specific histories.
- **Andromeda Software Development Kit** and many computing projects use the name metaphorically.
- The European Space Agency studied an **ANDROMEDA** mission concept in contexts unrelated to the official constellation.
- Andromeda is prominent in science-fiction franchises, including *Mass Effect: Andromeda* (2017).
- No major launched astronomical spacecraft has borne simply the official mission name “Andromeda” on the scale of *Gaia* or *Hipparcos* as of the date of this dossier.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is Andromeda Babylonian?

**Disputed claim.** Positional overlap with Anunitu and the Great Swallow is real. A direct narrative line from Anunitu or Tiamat to the chained Ethiopian princess is not documented. Similarities became especially popular in nineteenth-century pan-Babylonian scholarship. The responsible conclusion is: possible Near Eastern influence on the constellation field, but no demonstrated Babylonian Andromeda myth.

### 2. Is there an Egyptian Andromeda?

**Unverified claim.** No specific pharaonic chained princess has been securely identified. Dendera is late and syncretic. Online diagrams that label an Egyptian ceiling figure “Andromeda” commonly back-project Greek names.

### 3. Was Andromeda Black, white, or “Ethiopian” in a modern sense?

**Documented ancient complexity; modern controversy.**

- Ancient literary geography places her in *Aithiopia*, most often imagined south of Egypt but sometimes extending to remote southern/eastern peoples.
- The Greek term *Aithiops* was associated etymologically with a “burnt face” and commonly denoted dark-skinned peoples.
- Ovid calls her *fusca*, dark/dusky, in *Heroides* 15.
- Greek vase paintings can distinguish dark-skinned Ethiopian attendants from a light-skinned Andromeda; artistic conventions for gender and ethnicity complicate literal reading.
- Philostratus explicitly describes a fair-skinned Andromeda among Ethiopians.
- Heliodorus builds the plot of the *Aethiopica* around a white Ethiopian princess conceived while her mother viewed an image of Andromeda.

Thus “the ancients unanimously portrayed her as Black” and “the ancients unanimously portrayed her as white” are both false. Ancient sources themselves produced a tension between Ethiopian origin, color terminology, artistic convention, and exceptional whiteness. Modern European art overwhelmingly whitened her, but this was a reception choice rather than the only possible reading. [Study of ancient visual descriptions](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/greece-and-rome/article/figure-ekphrasis/E07396151965CDFAB1B10870EB8B11A5), [current debate overview](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/european-institute/events/2025/mar/black-europe-andromeda-and-representations-myth)

### 4. Ethiopia or Joppa?

**Documented competing traditions.** The sacrifice occurs in Ethiopia in the principal mythographers but at Joppa in geographic and antiquarian sources. Pliny effectively combines them. Moving the setting to Joppa may reflect Hellenistic civic self-promotion and tourist culture; that motive is scholarly reconstruction, not an ancient confession of fabrication.

### 5. Did Perseus ride Pegasus?

**Popular error/late iconographic tradition.** Ancient sources normally give Perseus winged sandals. Pegasus springs from Medusa and belongs to Bellerophon’s story. Renaissance and modern artists increasingly put Perseus on Pegasus because the combined image is visually legible and both constellations lie nearby. It is now traditional art, but not the standard early text.

### 6. Did Perseus petrify Cetus?

**Variant and modern conflation.** Ovid has Perseus kill the monster with his sword. Other summaries allow Medusa’s head. Hyginus explicitly uses the head against human conspirators. No universal classical version exists.

### 7. Who discovered M31?

**Terminological dispute.**

- Al-Ṣūfī: earliest secure written description.
- Marius: first published telescopic observation.
- Messier: catalogue entry, not discovery.
- Unknown prehistoric observers: almost certainly saw it under dark skies, but left no securely identified record.

Calling Marius “the discoverer” follows early-modern European citation practice but ignores al-Ṣūfī. Calling al-Ṣūfī the first human to see it goes beyond the evidence.

### 8. Did ancient Babylonians record M31?

**Unverified/disputed.** Suggested readings of the “Rainbow” or Anunitu’s head as M31 depend on modern positional inference. No secure description equivalent to al-Ṣūfī’s nebulous patch has been demonstrated.

### 9. Did Hubble “discover the Andromeda Galaxy”?

**Popular error.** Hubble did not discover M31, its spiral form, its spectrum, or even the idea that it might be a galaxy. He supplied decisive Cepheid-distance evidence in 1923–25. Curtis, Öpik, Slipher, Roberts, and many others were essential predecessors.

### 10. Was the 1920 Great Debate settled that night?

**Popular simplification.** Shapley and Curtis presented divergent models; neither had decisive data. Hubble’s later Cepheids, followed by calibration corrections, resolved the central issue.

### 11. Was S Andromedae a nova?

**Historical classification, now superseded.** It was called a nova in 1885 because the supernova class had not been formulated. It is now SN 1885A, probably a peculiar thermonuclear supernova. Its exact subtype remains debated.

### 12. Who first saw SN 1885A?

**Contested priority.** Hartwig made the verified observation that generated the international alert. Gully and Ward claimed earlier sightings but did not recognize or promptly announce the object. “Discoverer” can therefore mean earliest sighting or effective scientific announcement.

### 13. What does Alpheratz mean?

**Documented etymology with corrupt forms.** It derives from “the horse’s navel,” not “Andromeda’s head” or “shoulder.” *Sirrah* is another reduction of the same Arabic phrase. The horse meaning is evidence of the shared Pegasus star.

### 14. What does Mirach mean?

The best-supported derivation is Arabic *al-marāqq*, the loins. “Girdle” is a reasonable anatomical gloss but not the most literal translation. Confusion with Merak in Ursa Major arises because both passed through related Arabic anatomical terminology.

### 15. What does Almach mean?

It means the caracal/desert lynx, not a foot. Spellings Almaak, Almaach, Almak, and Alamak record differing transliterations. The IAU selected **Almach** as the standardized modern form.

### 16. Was Alpheratz “stolen” from Pegasus in 1930?

**Misleading formulation.** The star was anciently shared. Delporte’s exclusive polygons could not preserve dual territorial membership. Its ancient Andromeda designation and place as her head were retained; the Great Square remains intact as an asterism.

### 17. Is M110 an original Messier number?

**Modern retroactive designation.** Messier observed and depicted the object, but Kenneth Glyn Jones added the label M110 in 1967. It is now conventional but was not printed as entry 110 in Messier’s catalogue.

### 18. Were Bode’s figures scientific boundaries?

**No.** His mythological outlines and constellation names organized a chart but did not create exclusive official areas. Reading Delporte’s boundaries back into Bode distorts the atlas.

### 19. Did Bode invent Andromeda?

**No.** Andromeda was ancient. Bode’s local invention was Honores Friderici and his particular visual arrangement, not the chained maiden.

### 20. “Andromeda is the nearest galaxy”

**Popular error.** It is the nearest major spiral galaxy, not the nearest galaxy of any kind. Several dwarf galaxies, including Milky Way satellites, are much closer.

### 21. “M31 is inside the constellation”

**Perspective warning.** Correct only in the angular IAU sense. M31 is not physically among the foreground Milky Way stars that form the visual chain.

### 22. Hoaxes and pseudo-history

Searches combining Andromeda with “hoax,” “abolished,” “obsolete,” and equivalent terms found:

- recurring “Andromedan alien” and channeling claims, without verifiable astronomical evidence;
- commercial star-naming claims implying official ownership, contrary to IAU practice;
- assertions that Andromeda was abolished by the IAU, apparently confusing it with Honores Friderici;
- pseudo-historical claims that biblical passages name the Andromeda Galaxy;
- claims that ancient Egyptians or Babylonians precisely mapped M31 as a galaxy.

These are **modern inventions or unverified claims** unless independent primary evidence is supplied.

## Open questions

1. How much of the Perseus–Andromeda constellation complex was inherited from Mesopotamian positional astronomy, and how much was created after Greek mythological reinterpretation?
2. Was the constellation family already mythologized by Eudoxus, or did the complete catasterism develop through fifth-century tragedy and later Hellenistic scholarship?
3. Can the surviving fragments distinguish Sophocles’ plot reliably from Euripides’?
4. Which recension of the *Catasterismi* best preserves the original reason for Athena’s catasterism?
5. What exact chain of Arabic-to-Latin corruption produced *Asnade/Ansnade* and the “woman without a husband” descriptions?
6. Why do some medieval manuscripts masculinize or make Andromeda anatomically ambiguous? Translation, reversed globe exemplars, symbolic interpretation, and copying error all remain plausible contributors.
7. Did any pre-al-Ṣūfī culture explicitly recognize M31 as a nonstellar cloud? Present Babylonian proposals remain inconclusive.
8. Which observer deserves priority for SN 1885A under a rigorously defined discovery standard: first sighting, first recognition, or first announcement?
9. What is SN 1885A’s exact thermonuclear subtype?
10. Is the newly imaged oxygen-emission structure near M31 physically connected to the galaxy, a Galactic foreground remnant, or something else?
11. What documentary reasoning, if any beyond inherited catalogue usage and geometric convenience, governed Delporte’s exact choice to put Alpheratz in Andromeda?
12. The supplied phrase “on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be reconciled with Bode’s own catalogue description. Its source or intended database remains unidentified.
