# Antlia: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Status — documented fact.** Antlia is one of the 88 constellations retained by the International Astronomical Union. The IAU accepted the constellation name and three-letter abbreviation at its first General Assembly in Rome in 1922; Eugène Delporte’s boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930. Antlia was therefore **kept, not abolished**. Its official stars did not subsequently “go” anywhere, although two stars formerly lettered β and γ Antliae fell on the Hydra side of Delporte’s boundary and are now catalogued there. The IAU defines Antlia as an area rather than as Lacaille’s or Bode’s pictured pump. [IAU constellation history and table](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [Delporte, *Délimitation scientifique des constellations*](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

**Name and etymology — documented philology.** New Latin *antlia* continues Latin *antlia*, a water-raising device or pump, borrowed from Greek ἀντλία, *antlía*. In Greek the word could mean a ship’s hold, bilge water, a reservoir, or an apparatus for drawing water; it is related to ἄντλος, “bilge” or “hold.” The deeper Indo-European derivation is uncertain; one proposal associates it with a root meaning “draw water.” *Pneumatica* is the feminine adjective from Greek πνεῦμα, “breath, wind, air,” hence “pneumatic” or “worked by air.” Thus *Antlia Pneumatica* is literally a pneumatic pump, not simply any ancient water pump. [Merriam-Webster etymology](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Antlia); [Smith, *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities*, “Antlia”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/secondary/SMIGRA%2A/Antlia.html)

**Important distinction — documented fact.** The word is ancient; the constellation is not. The existence of Greek and Roman pumps supplies the vocabulary but no ancient catasterism of Antlia.

**Position and extent — documented fact.** Antlia is a small southern constellation between Hydra to the north, Centaurus to the east, Vela to the south, and Pyxis to the west. It covers about **238.9 square degrees**, 0.579 percent of the celestial sphere, ranking 62nd among the 88 constellations. Its modern extent is approximately right ascension 9h 26.5m–11h 05.6m and declination −24.5° to −40.4°. The entire figure is visible only south of roughly 49° N, although northern observers can see its upper parts.

**Official boundary — documented fact.** Delporte made Antlia a twelve-sided polygon along right-ascension and declination lines referred to equinox B1875.0. Precession makes those edges appear slightly tilted in J2000 charts. Delporte was instructed to preserve already established variable-star designations where practicable, which accounts for some ungainly modern outlines. [IAU boundary explanation and downloadable coordinates](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

**The visual figure.** Modern line drawings vary. A common minimal stick figure joins α and η Antliae; other charts use α, ε, ι, θ, and η. None has official IAU standing. The historical object was a complete vacuum pump with receiver, stand, cylinder or cylinders, handles, pipes, and sometimes a table.

**Brightness and “emptiness” — documented fact with a necessary qualification.** Antlia has no star brighter than fourth magnitude: α Antliae is about magnitude 4.25. It consequently appears empty to the unaided eye under light-polluted skies. It is not astrophysically empty: its official area contains nearby stars, variable stars, planetary systems, a major nearby galaxy group, the Antlia Cluster, several dwarf galaxies, and a vast foreground supernova remnant.

## Stars and objects

### Historical lettering and the figure’s principal stars

**Documented fact.** Lacaille applied Greek letters α through θ, treating the close ζ pair under one letter. Benjamin Apthorp Gould later added ι Antliae. Bode additionally printed letters including λ, ν, and π beside fainter stars; these did not all remain standard Bayer designations. Because Antlia did not exist when Bayer published the *Uranometria* in 1603, “Bayer letters” in Antlia are really later Greek-letter designations in the Bayer style.

The principal traditional designations are:

| Designation | Approx. visual magnitude | Character | Historical note |
|---|---:|---|---|
| α Antliae | 4.2–4.3 | K-type orange giant, suspected small-amplitude variable | Brightest star; no inherited proper name |
| ε Antliae | 4.5 | evolved orange K giant | Usually second-brightest |
| ι Antliae | 4.6 | K-type orange giant | Added by Gould, not Lacaille |
| θ Antliae | 4.7–4.8 | multiple/double system; bright component A-type | A conspicuous telescopic pair |
| η Antliae | 5.2 | F-type yellow-white dwarf/subgiant with faint companion | Part of some modern stick figures |
| δ Antliae | 5.6 | blue-white primary with fainter companion | Also variable designation BZ Ant |
| ζ¹ Antliae | about 6.2, with companion | double/multiple | Lacaille combined nearby ζ stars |
| ζ² Antliae | about 5.9 | main-sequence star | Physically distinct from ζ¹ |

Catalogue cross-identifications include α Ant = HD 90610, HR 4104; ε Ant = HD 82150, HR 3765; θ Ant = HD 84367, HR 3871; η Ant = HD 86629, HR 3947; δ Ant = HD 90972, HR 4118; and ι Ant = HD 94890, HR 4273. Magnitudes and classifications vary slightly by catalogue and component treatment.

**Flamsteed numbers — documented absence.** Antlia has no ordinary Flamsteed-number sequence. Flamsteed’s catalogue and atlas predated Lacaille’s constellation and did not establish numbered stars for this far-southern field. Modern tables that imply otherwise are conflating HD, HR, HIP, or other catalogue numbers with Flamsteed designations.

### Proper names

**Documented fact.** None of Antlia’s conspicuous naked-eye stars possesses a traditional Greek, Latin, Arabic, or medieval European proper name accepted by the IAU. “Alpha Antliae” is a designation, not a proper name.

**Modern invention, officially adopted.** **Macondo** is the IAU proper name of the eighth-magnitude exoplanet host HD 93083. Colombia selected it in the 2019 IAU NameExoWorlds campaign for the fictional town in Gabriel García Márquez’s *Cien años de soledad*. Its planet HD 93083 b was named **Melquíades**, after the novel’s wandering sage. These are literary twentieth-century derivations, not recovered indigenous or ancient star names. [IAU catalogue evidence](https://exopla.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/2022_oldIAU-CSN.pdf); [HD 93083/Macondo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HD_93083)

**Contested or unsupported popular usage.** Commercial star registries assign purchasable “names” to stars in Antlia, but the IAU explicitly disclaims such names. They should not be treated as astronomical nomenclature. [IAU naming FAQ](https://www.iau.org/IAU/IAU/Astronomy-FAQs/FAQs.aspx)

### Notable stellar systems

- **α Antliae — documented fact.** An evolved orange giant approximately a few hundred light-years away. Small reported changes around magnitude 4.22–4.29 have led to its description as a suspected variable, but it has no secure ancient name or folklore.

- **θ Antliae — documented fact.** A visually accessible double/multiple system. Historical charts often used it as part of the pump’s structure.

- **S Antliae — documented fact.** A W Ursae Majoris-type contact eclipsing binary. H. M. Paul recorded its variability in 1888, when it had the shortest known variable-star period. E. C. Pickering questioned an Algol classification, and Alfred H. Joy in 1926 recognized its resemblance to W UMa systems. The stars share a common envelope and orbit in roughly 0.65 day. [History of S Antliae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S_Antliae)

- **T Antliae — documented fact.** A classical Cepheid, visually around ninth to tenth magnitude, valuable as a pulsating distance indicator. It lies several thousand light-years away. [T Antliae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T_Antliae)

- **U Antliae — documented fact.** A carbon-rich asymptotic-giant-branch variable surrounded by detached shells of gas and dust, evidence of episodic mass loss. It is among Antlia’s most astrophysically studied stars even though it is not among its brightest.

- **R Antliae and related red variables — documented fact.** Antlia includes long-period and semiregular red variables, but no classical naked-eye nova is securely recorded under the constellation’s name.

- **TWA 7 / CE Antliae — documented fact.** A young M-type T Tauri star associated with the TW Hydrae association; it has a circumstellar debris disk.

- **DENIS J1048−3956 — documented fact.** A very nearby, faint late-M dwarf roughly 13 light-years away, frequently identified as the nearest stellar system within Antlia’s official borders.

- **2MASS J0939−2448 — documented fact.** A very cool T-type brown-dwarf system, illustrating how misleading the constellation’s visual “emptiness” is.

### Asterisms

**Documented absence.** No widely established Western naked-eye asterism comparable to the Big Dipper or Teapot is recognized within Antlia. Modern two-, three-, or five-star “pump” outlines are chart conventions.

**Historical figure as asterism.** Lacaille’s working pattern consisted of the stars positioned on his air-pump drawing. Because most are faint, the object is recognized from a chart rather than perceived spontaneously as a coherent star-chain.

### Deep-sky objects

**Messier objects — documented absence.** Antlia contains **no Messier object**. Messier worked primarily in a northern European observing context and his catalogue was not intended as a complete sky survey.

#### NGC 2997

**Documented fact.** NGC 2997 is Antlia’s most celebrated individual galaxy, a nearly face-on, grand-design spiral around magnitude 10. It was discovered by William Herschel on **4 March 1793**. Its two prominent spiral arms contain dust lanes, H II regions, and luminous star-forming complexes. Published distances vary by method, roughly 30–45 million light-years. It is the principal member of the NGC 2997 group.

**Recorded supernovae.**

- **SN 2003jg**, discovered on CCD images in October 2003, was classified as a stripped-envelope Type Ib/c event.
- **SN 2008eh**, reported in July 2008, reached about magnitude 15.

These are supernovae in an external galaxy projected within Antlia, not explosions of the constellation’s foreground pattern stars. [NGC 2997 and supernova record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_2997)

#### The Antlia Cluster

**Documented fact.** The **Antlia Cluster**, Abell S0636, is a nearby galaxy cluster in the Hydra–Centaurus region, conventionally described as the third-nearest rich cluster after Virgo and Fornax. It lies roughly 40 Mpc, about 130 million light-years, away; exact membership and distance depend on the adopted survey.

The cluster has two main concentrations centered on the giant ellipticals **NGC 3258** and **NGC 3268**. Photometric, spectroscopic, X-ray, and globular-cluster studies suggest that the two subgroups may be merging. Each principal elliptical has thousands of globular clusters. Other members or line-of-sight objects include NGC 3267, NGC 3269, NGC 3271, NGC 3273, NGC 3281, and IC 2587. [Dirsch, Richtler and Bassino, globular-cluster study](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0307200); [Smith Castelli et al., cluster populations](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.6004)

**Terminological caution.** “The Antlia Cluster” is not an open star cluster in the Milky Way; it is a cluster of galaxies. ESO 435-SC09 is a small Milky Way open-cluster candidate in the same constellation and should not be confused with Abell S0636.

#### The Antlia Dwarf

**Documented history with classification dispute.** The Local Group or near-Local-Group system now called the **Antlia Dwarf Galaxy** was noted as a possible dwarf by Corwin and the de Vaucouleurs in 1985 and confirmed through resolved-star observations by Alan Whiting, Mike Irwin, and George Hau in 1997. Their discovery paper described it as a typical dwarf spheroidal or dwarf elliptical at about 1.15 Mpc. A nearly simultaneous analysis by Aparicio, Dalcanton, Gallart, and Martínez-Delgado found younger stars and central star formation more characteristic of a dwarf irregular, at about 1.32 Mpc.

Accordingly, the simple label “dwarf spheroidal” is historically understandable but incomplete. Modern descriptions often use **transition-type dwarf**, reflecting its mixed gas and stellar-population properties. Its apparent proximity to NGC 3109 prompted proposals of association or interaction, but whether the pair is gravitationally bound has remained model-dependent. [Whiting, Irwin and Hau discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9706173); [Aparicio et al. analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9706197); [later HST assessment](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/419/2/1153/988835)

#### Antlia B

**Documented fact.** **Antlia B**, reported in 2015, is another faint dwarf galaxy in the NGC 3109 association, separated in projection from NGC 3109 by about 72 kpc. Its name is geographical: it lies in Antlia and is related observationally to the Antlia/NGC 3109 dwarf environment. [Discovery of Antlia B](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.01800)

#### Antlia 2

**Documented fact.** **Antlia 2**, announced from Gaia DR2 data in 2018 and published in 2019, is an enormous but extraordinarily diffuse satellite of the Milky Way. It spans about 1.26° on the sky—roughly 2.9 kpc at the original estimated distance of 130 kpc—yet has a surface brightness near 31.9 mag arcsec⁻². Its discovery made literal the contrast between visual emptiness and hidden structure. It was detected through Gaia astrometry, photometry, and variable stars and confirmed with DECam imaging and spectroscopy. [Torrealba et al., discovery paper](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/488/2/2743/5514354)

#### Antlia Supernova Remnant

**Documented discovery; physical associations disputed.** McCullough, Fields, and Pavlidou identified the enormous **Antlia supernova remnant** in 2002 through diffuse X-ray and Hα emission. It is approximately 24° across and crosses Antlia into Vela, Centaurus, and Hydra.

Initial work favored a distance of a few hundred parsecs and tentatively linked it with PSR B0950+08. A 2013 kinematic study proposed PSR J0630−2834 instead. Later optical and ultraviolet work suggested an age inconsistent with either old pulsar association. Current multiwavelength analyses place it roughly a few hundred parsecs away and interpret its bowl-like shape as interaction with surrounding interstellar material. The progenitor and compact remnant therefore remain open questions. [2013 pulsar study](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/435/1/879/1131044); [2026 institutional summary](https://www.sternwarte.uni-erlangen.de/blog/2026/04/22/stellar-explosion-in-our-neighborhood/)

#### Other notable galaxies

Antlia also contains:

- **NGC 3175**, an edge-on spiral;
- **NGC 3244**, a spiral galaxy;
- **IC 2560**, a Seyfert 2 spiral with a water-megamaser nucleus;
- **IC 2545**, an interacting galaxy system;
- **NGC 3281**, another Seyfert galaxy;
- numerous faint Antlia Cluster members and compact dwarf systems.

### Novae and transients

**Documented negative finding.** No famous historical naked-eye nova is securely recorded as “Nova Antliae.” Searches of catalogues and historical literature produce variable and cataclysmic stars but not an ancient or early-modern transient tied to Antlia, unsurprisingly because the constellation name did not exist before 1756.

**Open classification.** A faint nebula discovered around ASASSN-19ds has been proposed as either a nova shell associated with a Z Camelopardalis-type cataclysmic variable or an ancient planetary nebula. The title of the discovery paper itself preserves the uncertainty; it is not yet evidence for a documented historical nova. [Discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11306)

### Meteor showers

**Contested nomenclature.** Popular constellation summaries commonly say that Antlia has no meteor shower. A weak radar-derived stream called the **alpha Antliids** has nevertheless appeared in meteor literature. Galligan and Baggaley detected it with New Zealand radar data. The problem is that its quoted radiant lies in Sextans or near Hydra, roughly 20° north of α Antliae. The American Meteor Society has explicitly questioned the reason for the name.

The best conclusion is:

- there is no major, securely observed annual shower radiating from central Antlia;
- “alpha Antliids” is a weak historical/radar stream name with a geographically anomalous radiant;
- it should not be confused with the **Antihelion Source**, abbreviated ANT, which is a broad moving ecliptic radiant and not the constellation Antlia. [American Meteor Society discussion](https://amsmeteors.org/2026/01/meteor-activity-outlook-for-january-24-30-2026/); [IMO explanation of the Antihelion Source](https://imo.net/files/meteor-shower/cal2022.pdf)

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 150 CE: no identifiable Antlia

**Documented absence.** No Mesopotamian cuneiform star list, Egyptian astronomical ceiling, Greek astronomical poem, or Roman catalogue yet identified contains a pump constellation corresponding to modern Antlia.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Portions of this sky were visible from Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Mediterranean, but the principal Antlia stars are only fourth to sixth magnitude and lie south of the central Greco-Roman constellation network. Visibility does not establish recognition.

### Second century CE: Ptolemy

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogue contains the classical constellation Hydra and the enormous Argo Navis, together with “unformed” stars outside figures. It has no Antlia, no air pump, and no group that can securely be identified as its direct ancestor.

**Boundary caution.** Ancient constellations had descriptive figures and catalogued star lists, not polygonal territorial boundaries. It is therefore anachronistic to say that Ptolemy “assigned the area” to Argo. Some modern Antlia positions can be drawn near later expansive images of Argo or south of Hydra, but Lacaille created the new pattern in a sparsely catalogued interval rather than cutting a clearly defined pump-shaped piece from a Ptolemaic boundary.

### Tenth century: al-Ṣūfī

**Documented fact.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita* (964) revised Ptolemy’s catalogue and illustrated the 48 Ptolemaic figures. Hydra was **Ṣūrat al-Shujāʿ**, “the figure of the great/strong snake.” Argo was transmitted in its Ptolemaic form. There is no Antlia and no Arabic air-pump name attached to these stars. [Al-Ṣūfī constellation concordance](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_constellations.htm)

**Bedouin star lore — negative finding.** Al-Ṣūfī carefully distinguished inherited Greek figures from indigenous Arab star names and asterisms. Searches did not locate a securely identified Bedouin asterism composed specifically of α, ε, η, θ, and ι Antliae. The old Arab **al-Ḥail**, “the Horses,” used stars spread over modern Leo, Sextans, and Hydra, but cannot be equated with Antlia without stronger positional evidence.

### 1515: Dürer

**Documented absence.** Albrecht Dürer’s northern and southern celestial maps of 1515 reproduce the Ptolemaic tradition. They do not show Antlia, which would not be invented for another 241 years. Any claim that a shape near Argo or Hydra on Dürer’s southern sheet is “Antlia” is retrospective.

### 1590s–1603: Plancius, Keyser, de Houtman, and Bayer

**Documented absence.** Petrus Plancius, Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser, Frederick de Houtman, and Johann Bayer established and publicized several new southern constellations. Antlia was not among them. Bayer’s *Uranometria* of 1603 consequently contains no Antlia and could not have assigned its Greek letters. Claims calling α Antliae a “Bayer 1603 designation” are terminological shorthand, not historical fact.

### 1627: Julius Schiller

**Documented absence.** Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaced the constellations then recognized in Bayer’s atlas with Christian subjects. Because Antlia had not been created, Schiller assigned it no saint, apostle, biblical object, or Christian counterpart. The nearby inherited southern figures were absorbed into Schiller’s Old Testament program, but there is no legitimate “Christian Antlia.” [Library of Congress edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/); [ETH digital edition](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720)

### 1690: Hevelius

**Documented absence.** Johannes Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* predates Antlia by more than six decades. He did not invent or portray it. Since Hevelius charts constellation figures rather than Delporte-style territories, no exact “Hevelius boundary” for Antlia exists.

### 1650–1680s: the instrument before the constellation

**Documented technological history.**

- Otto von Guericke constructed an effective vacuum pump around 1650 and demonstrated the force of atmospheric pressure with the Magdeburg hemispheres.
- Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke developed a rack-and-pinion air pump and published experiments in *New Experiments Physico-Mechanical, Touching the Spring of the Air* (1660).
- Denis Papin published *Nouvelles expériences du vuide* in Paris in 1674 and subsequently worked with Boyle in England.
- Papin introduced important refinements, including paired-cylinder arrangements and automatic valves; Francis Hauksbee and instrument makers later produced further practical designs.

Thus “invented by Boyle,” “invented by Papin,” and “invented by Guericke” can each appear in popular Antlia histories, but they refer to different stages. Guericke has priority for the seventeenth-century vacuum pump; Boyle and Hooke created the celebrated experimental English machine; Papin published and developed new forms, including double-cylinder technology. [National Gallery history of the air pump](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/egerton-2000/an-experiment-on-a-bird-in-the-air-pump); [Sapienza Museum on the double-cylinder pump](https://museofisica.web.uniroma1.it/it/pompa-hauksbee-due-cilindri); [MacTutor biography of Papin](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Papin/)

### 1751–1753: Lacaille at the Cape

**Documented fact.** The Académie Royale des Sciences sent Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille to the Cape of Good Hope. From an observing station in Cape Town he measured southern stars principally between August 1751 and July 1752 and continued related work until his departure in 1753. His expedition combined stellar cataloguing with lunar parallax, geodesy, refraction, and longitude work.

The often repeated “10,000 stars” is a rounded description. Lacaille’s final catalogue contained roughly **9,766 stars**; his first published table contained **1,935 brighter stars and nebulous objects**. The survey was unprecedented in southern coverage but should not be quoted as exactly 10,000 observations without qualification. [Lacaille catalogue reconstruction](https://pbarbier.com/lacaille/lacaille.html); [Linda Hall Library, southern planisphere](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/27-the-flamsteed-industry-1729-1805/29-lacaille-nicolas-planisphere-contenant/)

### 1754–1756: first presentation and publication

**Documented fact.** Lacaille presented a large southern planisphere to the French Academy on 15 November 1754. The work was printed in the Academy’s volume for 1752, which appeared in 1756, under the extensive title *Table des Ascensions Droites et des Déclinaisons Apparentes des Étoiles australes renfermées dans le tropique du Capricorne*. The accompanying planisphere names the figure **la Machine Pneumatique**.

This is the earliest secure attestation of the constellation. “1752” refers to the nominal Academy volume and observing epoch; “1756” to publication. Both dates occur correctly in different bibliographical senses.

### Lacaille’s program

**Scholarly interpretation grounded in documented choices.** Lacaille created fourteen constellations. Thirteen represented scientific, artistic, or navigational instruments; Mensa commemorated Table Mountain. The ensemble—air pump, chemical furnace, clock, microscope, telescope, octant, compass, drawing instruments, engraver’s burin, sculptor’s apparatus, painter’s easel, and reticle—has long been interpreted as an Enlightenment workshop in the sky.

Lacaille did not state a political dedication to a monarch or patron for Antlia. It honored experimental physics and its apparatus rather than Boyle or Papin as a named individual. Modern statements that it was “dedicated to Denis Papin” are plausible iconographic interpretations but stronger than Lacaille’s surviving wording.

### 1763: *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*

**Documented fact.** Lacaille died in 1762. His *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* appeared posthumously in Paris in 1763. Here the constellation bears the Latin name **Antlia Pneumatica**. The engraving shows a single-cylinder apparatus with glass receiver and support. [Digital edition information](https://www.meteo-maarssen.nl/lib_lacaille_1763.html)

### 1782: Bode’s first reception

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode included the constellation, in German context as the **Luftpumpe**, in his *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln* (Berlin and Stralsund, 1782). This work adapted and greatly enlarged the Fortin reduction of Flamsteed’s atlas, adding southern constellations and updated objects.

Bode’s 1782 rendering follows a relatively simple, essentially single-cylinder pump. [Heidelberg’s digital Bode project](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/einl.html)

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact.** Bode’s monumental *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* was published at Berlin in 1801. Its twenty immense copperplates presented over 17,000 stars and about one hundred constellation figures, including both accepted and experimental ones. Antlia appears on **Chart XIX**, the sheet with Hydra’s tail, Centaurus, Crater, Corvus, Argo, Pyxis, Felis, and neighboring southern figures.

Bode labels it **ANTLIA PNEUMATICA** and depicts an elaborate double-cylinder vacuum pump. The glass receiver is above a system of cylinders, handles, pipes, and framework. The transformation from Lacaille’s simpler machine makes the pictured technology look more modern and mechanically impressive.

Bode’s accompanying catalogue, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, lists coordinates rather than supplying a myth. Its published title describes **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters**. The atlas was dedicated financially to Friedrich von Hahn, but Antlia itself was not a Hahn honorific. [ETH complete digital *Uranographia*](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [Marseille digital copy](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035); [Bode Antlia image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AAntlia_bode.JPG)

**The “830 stars in 109 groups” lead — unverified and probably conflated.** This wording could not be traced to Bode’s *Uranographia*, its catalogue, or a recognized “Bode collection on chain.” It conflicts with the work’s documented 17,240-object catalogue and approximately 102 constellation figures. It may describe a modern digital collection, linked-data visualization, educational chain, or another catalogue. Without an identifiable repository record, it should not be presented as a property of Antlia or of Bode’s atlas.

### 1822–1824: Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

**Documented fact.** Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* (London, 1822) retained **Antlia Pneumatica** and the figurative pump.

In 1824 *Urania’s Mirror* placed Antlia at the lower part of Card 32 with Hydra, Corvus, Crater, Sextans, Felis, Lupus, Centaurus, Argo Navis, and Pyxis. The cards were pierced at star positions so that light shone through. They were advertised as designed “by a lady,” but Peter Hingley identified the designer in 1994 as the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam. The pictures substantially copy Jamieson; concealment of authorship may have helped mask that borrowing. [Royal Astronomical Society history](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror)

### 1833–1849: Burritt

**Documented fact.** Elijah H. Burritt’s *Atlas, Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens*, first published in 1833 and issued in a widely circulated 1835 edition, included Antlia among the southern figures. Burritt’s inexpensive hand-colored atlas helped transmit the instrument constellations to American schools and households. [American Museum of Natural History copy](https://digitalcollections.amnh.org/archive/Celestial-map-from-Burritt-s-Atlas-designed-to-illustrate-the-geography-of-the-heavens-2URM1T14S658.html)

### 1843–1844: Argelander and Herschel

**Documented fact.** Friedrich Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) listed the abbreviated **Antlia Pneumat.** and indicated its extent without an elaborate figurative engraving.

In 1844 John Herschel, in “Farther Remarks on the Division of Southern Constellations,” recommended shortening several cumbersome Lacaille names. He observed that Lacaille himself had used shortened forms in tables. **Antlia** gradually displaced **Antlia Pneumatica**. The change was editorial economy, not a change from air pump to water pump. [Herschel bibliographical details](https://everything.explained.today/Antlia/)

### Later nineteenth-century atlases

**Documented reception.** Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* (1872), influenced by Argelander, belongs to the transition from luxuriant constellation pictures to precise naked-eye stellar cartography. Antlia remained, but the pictorial pump became less important than the labels and stars.

Flamsteed cannot be counted as receiving Antlia: his atlas was published before Lacaille’s invention. Fortin’s later French adaptation of Flamsteed’s atlas and Bode’s reworking could add it, but that does not make it a Flamsteed constellation.

### 1922–1930: standardization

**Documented fact.** At Rome in 1922, IAU Commission 3 accepted 88 Latin constellation names and Russell’s three-letter abbreviations. Antlia and **Ant** were retained. This was not yet a complete boundary decision.

Delporte later drew the official areas in B1875 coordinates. His boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in 1930. β and γ Antliae landed in **Hydra**; the remaining familiar Lacaille/Gould stars stayed in Antlia. Antlia did not take a major named star from another surviving constellation, but its northern edge replaced the informal illustrated space where Bode had placed **Felis**, an abolished constellation now wholly in Hydra. Its western and southern limits formalized divisions beside Pyxis and Vela, themselves derived from Lacaille’s reorganization of the Argo region.

### 1997–present

- **1997:** Confirmation and publication of the Antlia Dwarf.
- **2002:** Recognition of the Antlia supernova remnant.
- **2003 and 2008:** Supernovae detected in NGC 2997.
- **2015:** Antlia B reported.
- **2018–19:** Antlia 2 discovered through Gaia DR2.
- **2019:** HD 93083 officially named Macondo.
- **Present:** Antlia remains an official IAU constellation whose scientific identity is territorial; Lacaille’s and Bode’s pump survives as its historical icon.

## Invention, meaning, and reception

### What did Antlia honor?

**Documented core.** It honored the air pump and experimental pneumatics.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** In the context of Lacaille’s thirteen instrument figures, it embodies the mathematical and experimental sciences through which eighteenth-century European savants represented Enlightenment knowledge.

**Disputed attribution.**

- “Papin’s air pump” appears frequently in modern constellation histories.
- “Boyle’s air pump” is equally frequent in English-language literature.
- Guericke has the best claim to invention of the seventeenth-century vacuum pump.
- Boyle and Hooke produced the iconic experimental pump familiar through Boyle’s publications.
- Papin made major improvements and is associated particularly with two-cylinder forms.
- Lacaille’s published label names no inventor.

Consequently, the safest formulation is: **Lacaille commemorated the pneumatic or vacuum pump developed through the work of Guericke, Boyle, Hooke, Huygens, Papin, Hauksbee, and instrument makers; later writers variously personalized it as Boyle’s or Papin’s machine.**

### Bode’s meaning

**Documented iconography, interpretive significance.** Bode did not invent Antlia. His contribution was to monumentalize it. The double-cylinder pump on Chart XIX makes a faint stellar field into a detailed emblem of experimental physics. In Bode’s atlas it stands amid ancient Hydra, Centaurus, Corvus, Crater, and Argo and modern Pyxis and Felis—a visual confrontation between inherited myth, exploration, scientific equipment, and whimsical modern invention.

## Myths and legends

### Greek and Roman

**Documented absence.** Antlia has no Greek or Roman myth, catasterism, divine identity, heroic actor, or beast. It does not occur in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus’ *Phaenomena* or the ancient scholia;
- Germanicus’ translation and scholia;
- Ovid’s *Fasti* or *Metamorphoses*;
- Manilius’ *Astronomica*;
- Nonnus’ *Dionysiaca*;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus’ *Bibliotheca*;
- Pausanias’ *Description of Greece*.

No god placed it in the sky, no transformation created it, and no competing classical identity survives. Supplying the myths of Argo merely because Antlia lies near Argo would manufacture a false catasterism.

### The Argo association

**Later cartographic tradition, not an Antlia myth.** Some modern descriptions say Antlia’s stars “belonged to Argo Navis.” This is defensible only in the loose sense that early-modern artists expanded the immense ship into neighboring southern space. Ptolemy’s catalogue does not identify the principal Antlia stars as the pump-like part of Argo, and Lacaille’s figure occupies a comparatively blank region north of the ship.

The Argonaut saga—Jason, the Golden Fleece, Athena’s construction of the Argo, its speaking timber from Dodona, and the ship’s catasterism—belongs to **Argo Navis**, not Antlia. It is therefore contextual tradition rather than Antlia mythology.

### Modern invented “myths”

Commercial astrology and children’s constellation sites sometimes place the pump in a generalized story of scientific progress or describe Papin or Boyle as being “honored in heaven.” These are modern narrative inventions, not myths transmitted from antiquity. No evidence was found for a traditional tale in which Papin, Boyle, or Guericke was catasterized.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

**Negative finding.** No securely identified Babylonian constellation or ziqpu-star group corresponds to the principal Antlia pattern. General claims that every modern constellation has a Babylonian predecessor are false. Because Antlia’s important stars are faint and southerly, positional identifications would require explicit cuneiform coordinates or culmination data; none was found.

### Egyptian

**Negative finding.** No decan, coffin-lid figure, astronomical ceiling image, or named Egyptian constellation has been securely mapped to Antlia. The ancient Egyptian shaduf and water-lifting technologies are relevant to the remote history of the word and object “pump,” not evidence for an Egyptian pump in these stars.

The Farnese Atlas, a Roman copy of a Hellenistic celestial globe, contains no Antlia. Apparent blank space beside Hydra or Argo cannot be treated as a lost pump.

### Arabic and Persian

**Documented absence for Antlia proper.** Al-Ṣūfī preserved Hydra as **al-Shujāʿ** and the Ptolemaic ship, but no Antlia. No established Arabic proper name survives for α Antliae or the other main Antlia stars.

Modern Arabic calls the constellation **مضخة الهواء**, “air pump,” or uses a transliteration of *Antlia*. Modern Persian similarly uses **تلمبهٔ هوا / پمپ هوا**, “air pump.” These are translations of the Western modern constellation, not medieval Iranian or Arabic asterisms.

**Bedouin material — open question.** The absence of a traced name does not prove that southern Arabian observers ignored every star here. It means that no securely mapped Bedouin grouping in the consulted al-Ṣūfī and modern concordance material can be assigned to Antlia without conjecture.

### Chinese

**Documented tradition, but mappings are reconstruction.** Chinese observers incorporated the region into different asterisms:

- **天廟, Tiān Miào**, “Celestial Temple,” included stars now identified with ε, η, and θ Antliae and stars in Pyxis. It belonged to the southern sky associated with the Vermilion Bird.
- **東甌, Dōng’ōu**, “Eastern Ou,” represented a historical region or people of southeastern China and included faint stars in southern Antlia.

The exact membership of faint Chinese asterisms varies among Qing catalogues and modern identifications; assignments should be credited to comparative star-map scholarship rather than treated as self-evident ancient coordinates. [Hong Kong Space Museum glossary and history](https://hk.space.museum/en/web/spm/resources/teachers-corner/constellations-and-myths/glossary-of-chinese-star-regions-asterisms-and-star-name.html); [Chinese constellation overview](https://sprite.phys.ncku.edu.tw/astrolab/e_book/astron_chinese/captions/chinese_stars.html)

The modern Chinese **唧筒座** is a translation of Western Antlia and must not be confused with Tiān Miào or Dōng’ōu.

### Indian

**Negative finding.** Antlia lies well south of the ecliptic and is crossed by no classical nakshatra. No standard Sanskrit constellation corresponding to Lacaille’s stars was located. Modern Indian-language names are translations or transliterations of the IAU figure.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

**Negative finding.** Antlia is absent from biblical star names, medieval Jewish constellation lists based on the Greco-Arabic canon, and the usual twelvefold tribal or zodiacal mappings. It was invented too late for the classical Kabbalistic textual corpus.

Modern attempts to map all 88 constellations to Hebrew letters, tribes, angels, or sefirot are private esoteric systems. No historically continuous Antlia identification was located.

### Christianized sky

**Documented chronological impossibility.** Schiller’s 1627 atlas cannot provide a Christian substitute for a constellation invented in 1756. Claims that Antlia was one of Schiller’s biblical objects are anachronistic. Schiller’s atlas remains relevant only as the last major attempt to replace the pre-Lacaille sky wholesale with Christian figures.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

**Negative finding.** No early alchemical emblem, Hermetic text, or Renaissance astrological manual discusses Antlia as a celestial figure. The pump could readily be given modern symbolic meanings—air, spirit, vacuum, purification, experiment—but no historical lineage for such readings was found.

### Astrology and fixed stars

**Documented absence.** Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* gives planetary natures for stars in ancient constellations; it cannot mention Antlia. None of Antlia’s principal stars is among the traditional fifteen Behenian fixed stars. No Babylonian, Arabic, Indian, or European lunar mansion is centered on Antlia, because lunar mansions follow the Moon’s path near the ecliptic, whereas Antlia lies far south.

Modern “constellation astrology” pages that assign Antlia qualities such as pressure, compression, exhaustion, or intellectual invention are modern inventions. They do not descend from Ptolemy.

## Iconography

### Antiquity and the Middle Ages

- **Farnese Atlas, Roman, probably second century CE:** no Antlia.
- **Aratea manuscripts, ninth–fifteenth centuries:** no Antlia.
- **Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts from the eleventh century onward:** no Antlia; the relevant inherited pictures are Hydra/al-Shujāʿ and Argo.
- **Cathedral portals, zodiac calendars, manuscript calendars:** no identifiable pump constellation. Their cycles predate its invention.

Absence is decisive here: apparent mechanical objects elsewhere in medieval celestial art cannot be relabeled Antlia.

### Early printed maps

- **Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515:** no Antlia.
- **Bayer, Augsburg, 1603:** no Antlia.
- **Schiller, Augsburg, 1627:** no Antlia.
- **Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690:** no Antlia.

### Lacaille, Paris, 1756

**Documented first image.** On the southern planisphere printed with the Academy memoir, *la Machine Pneumatique* appears as laboratory apparatus occupying faint stars between Hydra and Argo. The engraving is schematic and belongs visually to Lacaille’s workshop of scientific instruments.

### Lacaille, Paris, 1763

**Documented.** The posthumous *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* presents **Antlia Pneumatica**, normally interpreted as a single-cylinder pump with receiver.

### Bode, Berlin and Stralsund, 1782

**Documented.** *Vorstellung der Gestirne* shows the **Luftpumpe** in a simpler form. This was Bode’s popular, comparatively compact atlas.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

**Documented.** Chart XIX of the *Uranographia* gives the most influential elaborate image: a large double-cylinder pump amid a dense engraved star field. The constellations are viewed in the atlas’s chosen celestial orientation, and surviving impressions may be plain, hand-colored, or highlighted. Variations in coloring belong to individual copies, not necessarily to Bode’s original specification.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

**Documented.** Jamieson retained the full pump under the Latin name. His figures were simplified for a popular British readership.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824

**Documented.** Card 32 puts Antlia near the lower center among numerous neighboring constellations. Pinholes mark stars. The card derives closely from Jamieson and was engraved by Sidney Hall.

### Burritt, Hartford/New York, 1833–1835

**Documented.** Burritt carried the figure into American educational cartography with hand-colored seasonal and southern charts.

### Argelander and Heis, 1843–1872

**Documented change of style.** These atlases subordinated the ornate machine to star positions, magnitudes, and borders. This anticipates the twentieth-century disappearance of pictorial figures from professional maps.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

**Negative finding.** No securely documented ancient coin, medieval cathedral portal, Renaissance ceiling, or traditional calendar was found that depicts Antlia as a constellation. Modern planetarium ceilings and constellation merchandise may show it, but these reproduce the post-1756 convention.

Joseph Wright of Derby’s *An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump* (1768) is sometimes associated visually with Antlia. It is an outstanding contemporary image of pneumatic experiment, but there is no evidence that Wright meant the constellation or that Lacaille influenced the painting. The relationship is contextual, not genealogical. [National Gallery catalogue](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/egerton-2000/an-experiment-on-a-bird-in-the-air-pump)

## People, events, and works

### Lacaille

Antlia’s principal human association is Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille. His Cape survey reorganized the southern sky and established a practical star catalogue on which later southern astronomy depended. The constellation commemorates his instrumental conception of science more directly than any single explorer or patron.

### Boyle, Hooke, Papin, Guericke, and Hauksbee

These experimenters and instrument makers form the technological prehistory of the image. Popular accounts often select one inventor, but the apparatus pictured by Lacaille and Bode belongs to an evolving seventeenth- and early-eighteenth-century instrument tradition.

### Bode

Bode gave Antlia its grandest canonical picture and integrated it into an atlas containing both traditional and experimental constellations. He did not rename it or invent it.

### William Herschel

Herschel discovered NGC 2997 in 1793. His son John Herschel later helped normalize the shortened constellation name **Antlia** and conducted foundational southern observations from the Cape.

### Alan Whiting, Mike Irwin, and George Hau

They confirmed the Antlia Dwarf as a nearby resolved stellar system in 1997. Aparicio, Dalcanton, Gallart, and Martínez-Delgado immediately complicated its initial dwarf-spheroidal classification by demonstrating younger populations and star formation.

### Gaia and Antlia 2

The discovery team led by Gabriel Torrealba used Gaia DR2’s astrometry, photometry, and variables to reveal Antlia 2. It is a particularly apt modern counterpart to the constellation’s theme: an object effectively invisible in ordinary light isolated by precision instrumentation.

### Navigation

**Negative finding.** No major explorer is securely recorded as steering by α Antliae or by an Antlia asterism. Its stars are too faint and the constellation too recent to rival Canopus, the Southern Cross, α/β Centauri, or the Magellanic Clouds as navigational markers. Lacaille observed it from the Cape, but that does not make it a traditional mariner’s constellation.

### Literature, art, and music

- Gabriel García Márquez’s **Macondo** and **Melquíades** entered Antlia through the 2019 exoplanet naming campaign.
- Joseph Wright’s air-pump painting supplies relevant Enlightenment iconography but does not name the constellation.
- *Urania’s Mirror* and Bode’s engravings are Antlia’s most significant works of visual culture.
- No canonical ancient poem, opera, or major classical musical composition devoted to Antlia was located.

### Ships and missions

**Negative finding with caution.** “Antlia” has been used in modern fiction, amateur astronomy, software, businesses, and proposed vehicle names, but no historically prominent exploration ship or space mission was found whose official name demonstrably commemorates this constellation. Claims require case-by-case documentation because *antlia* may instead refer to a pump or to unrelated scientific equipment.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Who invented the air pump?

**Disputed popular claim.** Lacaille is variously said to have honored Boyle’s pump or Papin’s pump.

**Evidence.** Guericke built an early vacuum pump around 1650; Boyle and Hooke’s machine and experiments appeared in 1660; Papin published in 1674 and later improved pump design; Hauksbee and others refined double-cylinder forms.

**Finding.** No consulted text by Lacaille explicitly dedicates Antlia to a named inventor. “Papin” is widespread secondary tradition, while “Boyle” reflects Anglophone scientific memory. The constellation most securely honors the instrument collectively.

### 2. Single-cylinder versus double-cylinder

**Documented iconographic difference.** Lacaille’s image is normally described as a one-cylinder apparatus; Bode’s 1801 plate is unmistakably more elaborate and double-cylinder.

**Caution.** Some captions incorrectly call Bode’s 1782 figure double-cylinder or label the 1801 machine simply “Papin’s pump.” Surviving pump designs combined features from several inventors, so precise attribution from visual resemblance alone is uncertain.

### 3. Was Antlia part of Argo Navis?

**Disputed wording.** The modern area touches the traditional Argo region, and later drawings could fill empty space expansively. But the principal Antlia stars do not form a documented Ptolemaic section of the ship.

**Finding.** “Created in blank sky north of Argo” is safer than “cut out of Argo.” Pyxis has a stronger visual relationship to the ship, although even Pyxis was a Lacaille addition rather than an ancient part named “the compass.”

### 4. Did the Greeks know Antlia?

**Popular error.** The Greek word ἀντλία is ancient, and pumps were known in antiquity; therefore some sources imply an ancient Greek constellation.

**Finding.** The inference is invalid. No Greek astronomical text or image has the figure. Antlia is eighteenth-century.

### 5. Bayer designations

**Terminological trap.** α Antliae is routinely called a Bayer designation.

**Finding.** It is a Bayer-style Greek-letter designation assigned by Lacaille, not an entry in Bayer’s 1603 atlas.

### 6. The missing β and γ

**Documented boundary consequence.** Lacaille’s β and γ stars were placed in Hydra by Delporte’s official boundaries. They survive historically as “former β/γ Antliae” but should not be used as current Antlia designations.

This is not evidence that the IAU abolished part of Antlia in 1922. It reflects the later conversion of overlapping pictorial domains into exclusive territorial areas.

### 7. The shortening to Antlia

**Documented editorial reform.** John Herschel advocated concise southern names in 1844. “Antlia” became conventional and was retained by the IAU.

**Potential misunderstanding.** The short form does not officially change the meaning to an ordinary water pump. Historical symbolism remains the air or vacuum pump.

### 8. Antlia Dwarf classification

**Scientific dispute.** The 1997 discovery paper called it dE/dSph; concurrent work found dwarf-irregular-like star formation. Later literature recognizes transitional properties. Calling it unqualifiedly a dwarf spheroidal suppresses this history.

### 9. Antlia supernova-remnant progenitor

**Open scientific dispute.** Associations with PSR B0950+08 and PSR J0630−2834 have both been proposed and challenged. Age and distance revisions weaken simple identifications.

### 10. Alpha Antliids

**Contested meteor name.** The proposed radiant is well north of Antlia. It may reflect historical clustering nomenclature rather than a radiant genuinely close to α Antliae. It is also easily confused with the Antihelion Source’s abbreviation ANT.

### 11. “No objects” or “empty constellation”

**Popular exaggeration.** Antlia lacks bright stars and Messier objects but contains significant galaxies, dwarfs, variables, exoplanets, and the huge Antlia SNR. “Empty” accurately describes casual naked-eye appearance, not physical content.

### 12. Schiller and Christian Antlia

**Anachronistic error.** Schiller died and published his atlas more than a century before Antlia’s invention. No saint or biblical implement replaced it.

### 13. Ancient astrology

**Modern invention.** Antlia has no Ptolemaic fixed-star nature, Behenian star, classical zodiacal rulership, or lunar mansion. Modern astrological interpretations begin after the constellation’s creation and often much later.

### 14. Misprints

**Documented minor errors.** “Antila” occurs in modern captions and secondary literature; “Antlia Pnumatica” occurs online as a dropped vowel. Neither is a historical variant with nomenclatural standing. The obsolete four-letter abbreviation **Antl** appears in twentieth-century systems and should not be confused with a typo or with the official **Ant**.

### 15. “109 groups and 830 stars”

**Unverified claim.** No matching statement was found in Bode’s primary atlas, catalogue descriptions, or standard bibliographies. Bode’s documented catalogue contains 17,240 objects and his atlas roughly 102 constellation figures. The unexplained figures should be treated as metadata from an unidentified collection until a repository link is supplied.

### 16. Hoaxes

No Antlia-specific historical astronomical hoax was located. Commercially sold star names and internet “ancient Antlia myths” are the principal misleading modern material, but they are better described as unofficial commerce and fabricated backstory than as coordinated hoaxes.

## Open questions

1. Which exact historical pump model did Lacaille’s engraver use? A close instrument-catalogue comparison might distinguish Papin, Hauksbee, or a contemporary French maker more securely.

2. Did Lacaille ever privately state that the constellation honored Papin? The published label does not establish a personal dedication; correspondence and Academy papers may contain more.

3. Can Bode’s double-cylinder engraving be tied to a specific Berlin instrument or published plate?

4. Which faint stars did every Qing-era source assign to Dōng’ōu and Tiān Miào? Modern Western identifications differ, and a catalogue-by-catalogue study is needed.

5. Did any indigenous southern African community recognize a pattern in precisely these stars? The present search found no secure equivalence, but absence from European catalogues is not evidence of cultural absence.

6. Can the alpha Antliids be reproduced as a coherent stream in current Global Meteor Network data, and why was the name chosen for a radiant outside Antlia?

7. What object, database, or exhibition is meant by “the Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)”? The phrase remains untraced.

8. Which compact object, if any, was born in the explosion that produced the Antlia SNR?

9. Is the Antlia Dwarf gravitationally bound to NGC 3109, and how should its transition morphology be classified under current dwarf-galaxy schemes?
