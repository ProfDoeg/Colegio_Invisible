# Apus: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **Kept by the International Astronomical Union.** Apus is one of the 88 official constellations. Its abbreviation is **Aps**, its Latin genitive **Apodis**, and its permanent boundaries are those approved by the IAU in 1928 and published by Eugène Delporte in 1930. No abolition or wholesale transfer of its stars occurred, although Lacaille had already removed part of the early bird’s long tail to make room for Octans.

## Method and evidentiary labels

The following labels distinguish kinds of statement:

- **Documented fact:** directly supported by an original atlas, catalogue, text, institutional record, or modern scientific catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern historian’s inference from surviving objects or texts.
- **Tradition:** a historically transmitted interpretation that need not be literally true.
- **Disputed:** competing interpretations or attributions survive.
- **Legend/myth:** a narrative not offered here as historical or zoological fact.
- **Modern invention:** a retrospective name, association, or story without ancient continuity.
- **Unverified:** repeatedly asserted, but not traceable here to satisfactory primary evidence.

Apus is a modern, post-Columbian constellation. Consequently, the elaborate Greek and Roman catasterism dossier appropriate to Orion or Andromeda does **not** exist for it. Absence is important here: Apus is not in Ptolemy, Aratus, Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Manilius, Ovid, Germanicus, the Aratean scholia, al-Sufi, or any ancient Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, or Indian canonical constellation system.

## Basic identification

### Official identity

| Field | Identification |
|---|---|
| Official name | **Apus** |
| Meaning | Bird-of-paradise |
| IAU abbreviation | **Aps** |
| Genitive | **Apodis** |
| IAU status | Retained |
| IAU adoption | Name and abbreviation accepted at Rome, 1922 |
| Boundaries | Approved at Leiden, 1928; published by Delporte, 1930 |
| Area | About **206 square degrees**, 67th of 88 |
| Brightest star | **α Apodis**, magnitude about 3.83; officially named **Paradys** in 2025 |
| Location | Far-southern sky, roughly declinations −67.5° to −83.1° |
| Visibility | Entirely visible from latitudes south of about 7° N; circumpolar over much of the Southern Hemisphere |
| Modern neighbors | Ara, Triangulum Australe, Circinus, Musca, Chamaeleon, Octans, Pavo |
| Messier objects | None |
| Established meteor showers named from Apus | None securely established in the IAU shower list |

The IAU gives the English gloss “the Bird of Paradise,” the pronunciations *APE-us* or *APP-us*, and the genitive pronunciation *APP-oh-diss*. The official boundary is a six-segment polygon. Because Delporte drew it in B1875.0 right ascension and declination, its edges appear slightly tilted when precessed onto J2000 maps. [IAU constellation history and chart](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### Names and etymologies

#### Dutch

- **Paradysvogel / Paradijsvoghel / De Paradijs Voghel** — “bird of paradise.”  
  **Documented fact:** a form *Paradysvogel* appears on the Plancius–Hondius globe of 1598; Frederick de Houtman’s 1603 catalogue has *De Paradijs Voghel*. Historical spelling varies because Dutch *ij* could be represented graphically in ways later transcribers read as *y* or *ÿ*.
- Modern Dutch: **Paradijsvogel**.

The Dutch expression is the primary historical vernacular name. It predates the stable Latin name *Apus*.

#### Latin

- **Apis Indica** — literally “Indian Bee.”  
  **Documented fact:** the form occurs in the Bayer tradition and is associated with the Plancius globe label. The illustration is unmistakably a bird, not a bee.
- **Avis Indica** — “Indian Bird.”  
  **Documented fact:** used as an explicit correction or alternative in seventeenth-century astronomical literature, including the Rudolphine Tables tradition.
- **Avis Paradisi** — “Bird of Paradise.”  
  **Documented fact:** used by Paulus Merula in *Cosmographiae generalis libri tres* (1605).
- **Apus / Apus Indica** — “Footless [Bird]” or “Footless Indian Bird.”  
  From Greek ἄπους, *ápous*, “without feet,” formed from privative ἀ-/a- plus πούς/*pous*, “foot,” genitive ποδός/*podos*. The Latin genitive **Apodis** preserves the Greek stem *pod-*.

#### The bee/bird problem

**Scholarly reconstruction, highly probable:** *Apis* was a copying, typesetting, or engraving error for *Avis*. Evidence:

1. the figure is a bird;
2. *avis* means bird, whereas *apis* means bee;
3. a separate southern constellation was already correctly called *Apis*, the Bee—the constellation now called Musca;
4. later authors explicitly use *Avis Indica*;
5. the Dutch label says bird-of-paradise.

The exact physical stage at which the error entered—Plancius’s wording, the globe engraver, Bayer’s exemplar, Bayer’s text, or transmission among them—has not been conclusively established. Richard Hinckley Allen popularized the “misprint” explanation in *Star Names and Their Meanings* (1899), while acknowledging that he had not found an earlier discussion of it.

A secondary complication is that Latin and zoological **apus** could designate a swift or swallow-like bird, also conventionally explained as “footless” because its small feet were seldom seen. Thus *Apus* was semantically plausible independently of the paradise-bird legend.

#### European vernaculars

These are primarily translations, not independent inventions:

- English: **Bird of Paradise**
- German: **Paradiesvogel**, historically also **Indianischer Vogel**
- French: **Oiseau de Paradis**
- Italian: **Uccello del Paradiso**
- Spanish: **Ave del Paraíso**
- Portuguese: **Ave-do-Paraíso**
- Modern Greek: often Πτηνόν or an equivalent of “bird,” though the international Latin name remains *Apus*

#### Arabic, Persian, and Hebrew

No historical name for Apus occurs in the classical Arabic, Persian, or Hebrew astronomical traditions because the constellation was invented in Europe at the end of the sixteenth century and lies too far south for ordinary observation from the principal centers of those traditions.

Modern translations include:

- Arabic: **طائر الفردوس**, *ṭāʾir al-firdaws*, “bird of paradise”
- Persian: **مرغ بهشتی**, *morġ-e beheštī*, “paradisal bird”
- Hebrew: **ציפור גן־העדן**, *tsipor gan-ha-ʿeden*, “bird of the Garden of Eden/paradise”

These are **modern linguistic equivalents**, not names from al-Sufi, Ulugh Beg, medieval Hebrew astronomy, or Bedouin uranography.

### What stars make the figure?

There is no official IAU “stick figure”; only the boundary is official. Modern outline drawings normally emphasize:

- α Apodis
- γ Apodis
- β Apodis
- δ¹ and δ² Apodis

A narrow triangle made by β, γ, and δ, with α displaced eastward, provides the principal naked-eye framework. Historical artists used more stars to mark the wings, body, beak, and formerly longer tail.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

Lacaille, not Bayer himself, assigned the surviving Greek-letter scheme in the eighteenth century:

- α, β, γ
- δ¹, δ²
- ε, ζ, η, θ, ι
- κ¹, κ² Apodis

There are **no genuine Flamsteed numbers in Apus**. Flamsteed’s numbered catalogue and atlas did not cover this far-southern region adequately. Numbers sometimes printed beside Apus stars in modern tables are commonly Gould, catalogue, HR, HD, or HIP numbers and should not be called Flamsteed designations.

### Boundary history in one view

- **Ptolemy, second century CE:** no Apus; the region was below his effective observational horizon and outside his 48 figures.
- **Medieval Arabic and European Ptolemaic atlases:** no Apus.
- **Plancius–Hondius, 1598:** bird-of-paradise created from Dutch southern-star observations.
- **Bayer, 1603:** twelve-star bird shown on the south-polar planisphere.
- **Kepler, 1627:** catalogue form has eleven stars and the name *Apus, Avis Indica*.
- **Hevelius, 1690:** receives the new southern bird into the atlas tradition; the bird is portrayed as footless.
- **Lacaille, 1750s–1763:** remeasures the southern sky, gives Greek letters, and shortens the old tail while introducing Octans around the pole.
- **Bode, 1801:** retains the artistic bird within a dense south-polar chart.
- **Argelander, 1843:** retains Apus but abandons pictorial ornament in favor of scientific mapping.
- **IAU/Delporte, 1922–1930:** fixes the name, abbreviation, and polygonal territory.

Modern magnitudes and classifications vary slightly between catalogues and data releases.

| **β Aps** | 4.23 | Orange K giant; double-star status reported in catalogues | No traditional proper name |
| **δ¹ Aps** | about 4.66–4.87 | M giant; semiregular variable | No traditional proper name |

| **δ² Aps** | 5.27 | K giant; wide optical pair with δ¹ | No traditional proper name |
| **ι Aps** | about 5.4 | Multiple/double system | No traditional proper name |

| **θ Aps** | variable, roughly 5–8 | Cool semiregular variable | No traditional proper name |

The assertion sometimes repeated that “the five brightest Apus stars are red” is broadly descriptive rather than taxonomically exact: several are evolved K or M stars, giving this faint constellation an unusually warm visual palette.

### Paradys

**Documented modern fact:** in 2025 the IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Paradys** for α Apodis. It is derived from the oldest Dutch constellation label *Paradys-vogel*. It is not an ancient proper star name and should not be projected backward onto Bayer or Lacaille. The approval date was 18 May 2025. [IAU announcement and table](https://iau.org/Iau/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx)

### Karaka and Kererū

**Documented modern invention:** the exoplanet host HD 137388 was named **Karaka**, after the New Zealand tree, and its planet **Kererū**, the Māori name for the New Zealand pigeon, through the IAU NameExoWorlds program. These names belong to a stellar system geometrically inside Apus but have no connection with the historical paradise-bird figure.

### Variable stars

- **δ¹ Apodis:** semiregular red giant, with several reported pulsation periods near 68, 95, and 102 days.
- **ε Apodis:** Gamma Cassiopeiae-type emission-line variable, changing only slightly near naked-eye magnitude.
- **θ Apodis:** cool semiregular variable with a substantially larger visual range.
- **S Apodis:** a luminous evolved, chemically peculiar variable often discussed among post-AGB or R Coronae Borealis-related objects; its exact classification has been revised in the literature.
- **R Apodis:** an instructive naming fossil. It received a variable-star designation but was subsequently found not to show convincing variability. The designation remains historically attached even though the premise proved false.

There is no securely recorded ancient or early-modern “guest star” attributable to Apus. No celebrated historical naked-eye nova comparable to Nova Persei 1901 or Nova Aquilae 1918 is associated with it. Supernovae have been detected in faint galaxies lying inside the modern boundary, but none has created a culturally significant “supernova of Apus.”

### Double and multiple stars

- δ¹ and δ² are separated by about 103 arcseconds and can be distinguished in good conditions without optical aid or with binoculars.
- β and ι Apodis have catalogued companions or multiplicity.
- κ¹ Apodis is a more demanding telescopic multiple system.

### Exoplanet systems

Among systems frequently listed within Apus are HD 131664, HD 134606, and HD 137388/Karaka. Planet and brown-dwarf classifications have changed as orbital inclinations and companion masses have been refined. In particular, a nominal “planet” around HD 131664 was later recognized as possibly entering the brown-dwarf or stellar-companion regime. This is a modern astrophysical classification dispute, not a constellation-history controversy.

### Deep-sky objects

#### NGC 6101

- Globular cluster, apparent magnitude about 9.2.
- Discovered by **James Dunlop at Parramatta, New South Wales, on 1 June 1826**; Dunlop catalogue number 68.
- Reobserved by John Herschel on 18 June 1835.
- Also catalogued as GCL 40, ESO 069-SC004, and Caldwell 107 in Patrick Moore’s Caldwell list.
- Distance estimates are around 50,000 light-years.
- Dynamical studies have examined its unusually weak apparent mass segregation and possible retained population of stellar-mass black holes.

The cluster’s identification is secure: Dreyer’s coordinates and description lead unambiguously to it. [Historical identification](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/ngc61.htm) [Dunlop catalogue project](https://assa.saao.ac.za/sections/deep-sky/james-dunlops-catalogue/)

#### IC 4499

A loose outer-halo globular cluster, apparent magnitude about 10–11, discovered photographically in the southern survey work associated with **DeLisle Stewart** around 1900. It is notable for its RR Lyrae population and for debates about whether it was anomalously young; later photometry generally places it among the old Galactic globular clusters, with age estimates near 12 billion years.

### Messier and Caldwell objects

- **Messier:** none.
- **Caldwell:** NGC 6101 is **Caldwell 107**.

A popular guide sometimes claims that Apus has no Caldwell object. That is incorrect under the published Caldwell list.

### Internal asterisms

No widely standardized modern folk asterism inside Apus approaches the status of the Big Dipper or the Coathanger. The β–γ–δ triangle is an observational convenience, not a separately inherited constellation.

The Chinese Jesuit-era **Yìquè**, discussed below, is a genuine named asterism but belongs to a different, reconstructed Chinese system and crosses the present Apus–Octans boundary.

### Meteor showers

No established major meteor shower bears the name Apodid or has its accepted radiant in Apus. Lists claiming a prominent “Apodid” shower should be checked against the current IAU Meteor Data Center; no such major established shower emerged from this investigation. The extreme southern declination also makes weak candidate radiants difficult to confirm from globally distributed observations.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the sixteenth century: no Apus

**Documented absence:** the stars were too far south for Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, northern Arabic, Persian, Jewish, and Indian canonical observers to incorporate into their standard constellation systems.

At Alexandria, latitude about 31° N, α Apodis at approximately −79° never rises. The same geometrical barrier applies even more strongly to Babylon, Baghdad, Isfahan, Toledo, and most of India. Consequently:

- no Babylonian MUL.APIN antecedent is known;
- no Egyptian decan corresponds securely to Apus;
- Ptolemy’s *Almagest* has no stars of Apus;
- al-Sufi’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* has neither an Apus figure nor a Bedouin-Arab asterism among these stars;
- no Greek or Roman catasterism exists;
- no Hebrew biblical or rabbinic constellation corresponds to it;
- no classical nakshatra crosses it.

Claims that Apus was “always a bird” are therefore false if meant as independent ancient continuity.

### 1521–1524: the birds of paradise enter European report

On 17 December 1521 at Tidore, the ruler presented the Magellan expedition with extraordinarily beautiful dead birds. Antonio Pigafetta’s account, composed in the 1520s, calls them *bolon dinata/divata*, glossed as “birds of God” or birds from the earthly paradise.

Crucially, Pigafetta says their legs were long and slender—approximately a palm in length. He does **not** originate the claim that they had no legs. His Italian description includes:

> *le sue gambe sono lunghe un palmo e sottile come un calamo*  
> “their legs are a palm long and slender like a reed.”

He also describes their plume-like feathers and reports what islanders told the voyagers about their paradisal origin. [Pigafetta text](https://www.intratext.com/IXT/ITA1688/_P8.HTM)

### 5 October 1522: Maximilianus Transylvanus and the footless marvel

A report by Maximilianus Transylvanus, based on interviews with survivors soon after the *Victoria* returned, disseminated the more marvelous version: the *manucodiata* lived perpetually aloft, feeding on dew or air, the female incubating upon the male’s back, and the birds falling to earth only at death.

**Tradition/legend:** this is a major early carrier of the footless paradise-bird mythology. It was easier to believe because commercial skins imported from New Guinea and the Moluccan trade network had their feet—and often wings—removed during preparation.

### Mid-sixteenth century natural history

Conrad Gessner and other naturalists reproduced or debated footless specimens. Ulisse Aldrovandi illustrated a bird without legs. European natural history thus converted an artifact of specimen preparation into an alleged anatomical fact.

**Documented correction resisted:** Pigafetta’s legged description existed, and sailors sometimes testified that living birds possessed feet, yet the marvelous version remained commercially and symbolically attractive.

### 1595–1597: Keyser and de Houtman’s observations

The first Dutch expedition to the East Indies, the **Eerste Schipvaart**, left Texel in April 1595 under Cornelis de Houtman. Petrus Plancius had instructed chief pilot **Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser**—Latinized *Petrus Theodorus*—in making stellar observations.

Keyser observed the southern sky during the voyage but died near Bantam in September 1596. Frederick de Houtman participated in the observational and editorial transmission. The original observing notebooks do not survive.

**Documented fact:** a catalogue based upon this work reached Plancius and supplied positions for twelve new southern constellations.

**Disputed attribution:** early sources variously credit Keyser, Frederick de Houtman, Plancius, or generic Portuguese/Dutch navigators. Current reconstruction gives:

1. Keyser a principal role in the first observations;
2. de Houtman a role in observing, preserving, revising, and publishing them;
3. Plancius a major role in forming and naming the pictorial constellations;
4. Hondius and Blaeu a role in their cartographic dissemination.

The evidence does not justify treating Bayer as their inventor.

### 1597/1598: Plancius and Hondius

A 35-centimeter celestial globe designed by **Petrus Plancius** and produced with **Jodocus Hondius** at Amsterdam displays the bird as *Paradysvogel*, accompanied in the transmitted labeling tradition by *Apis Indica*.

This is the **earliest surviving secure depiction of Apus as a constellation**.

Its purpose was not merely decorative. The twelve new southern figures organized formerly sparse or unfigured regions on European globes, based on observations needed for long-distance navigation after Polaris vanished below the horizon.

### 1602–1603: Blaeu and de Houtman

Willem Janszoon Blaeu’s globes reproduced the new southern figures and credited the observational work to Frederick de Houtman.

In 1603 de Houtman printed his southern-star catalogue as an appendix to his Malay–Dutch dictionary, *Spraeck ende woord-boeck inde Maleysche ende Madagaskarsche talen*. The constellation is **De Paradijs Voghel**.

The catalogue assigns eleven stars to the bird in the form analyzed in modern machine-readable comparisons. This differs from the twelve shown by Bayer.

### 1603: Bayer’s *Uranometria*

Johann Bayer included the twelve new southern constellations on the final south-polar plate of *Uranometria*, Augsburg, 1603. The bird is visually unmistakable, while the printed name appears as **Apis Indica**.

Bayer did not devise the modern Greek-letter sequence now used in Apus. His plate’s stars were marked by magnitude and position, but Lacaille later supplied the standard α–κ lettering.

Bayer’s text credits a mixed group of earlier navigators—Amerigo Vespucci, Andrea Corsali, Pedro de Medina, and Petrus Theodorus/Keyser. This helped generate later confusion over invention. Only Keyser is closely connected with the stellar dataset actually underlying the Plancian twelve.

A reconstruction comparing the plate with modern stars identifies twelve Bayer stars. The three most northerly—now ζ, ι, and β Apodis—also appear at the bottom of Bayer’s Ara plate. Bayer’s accompanying text mistakenly associates them with Grus, a localized editorial error.

### 1605: Merula

Paulus Merula’s *Cosmographiae generalis libri tres* lists **Avis Paradisi** and the Dutch **Paradijs-Voghel**. This is early evidence that readers understood the creature as a paradise bird, not Bayer’s literal “Indian bee.”

### 1624–1627: Bartsch and Kepler

Jakob Bartsch used *Apus* or *Avis Indica* in seventeenth-century celestial writing. Johannes Kepler’s 1627 *Tabulae Rudolphinae* catalogue tradition gives **Apus, Avis Indica** and eleven stars.

This is the decisive early astronomical normalization of *Apus*. It corrects the semantic nonsense of *Apis Indica* while linking the bird to the Greek “footless” name.

### 1627: Julius Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, made with Bayer’s assistance and engraved by Lucas Kilian and collaborators, replaced pagan and secular figures with biblical ones.

The lead “Schiller had no southern saints for it” is **incorrectly framed**. Schiller generally assigned:

- apostles to the zodiac,
- New Testament persons and objects to the northern sky,
- Old Testament persons and objects to the southern sky.

Apus did receive a Christianized interpretation. Modern inventories of Schiller’s correspondence identify **Apus/Paradijsvogel with Eve and the Apple**, spread over Schiller’s plates LI–LII.

Thus Schiller did not leave the Bayer twelve untouched or blank; he absorbed them into his Old Testament program. His system, however, did not win astronomical acceptance. [1627 facsimile record](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/) [Schiller concordance](https://www.meteo-maarssen.nl/lib_schiller_1627.html)

### 1660–1661: Cellarius and Bayer reissues

Andreas Cellarius reproduced Schiller’s Christian heaven in *Harmonia Macrocosmica*, but the classical and post-voyage names remained dominant.

Later editions of Bayer continued to spread the southern figures. The plates frequently descended from earlier engravings rather than representing new southern observations.

### 1679: Halley

Edmond Halley’s catalogue of southern stars, based on observations from St Helena, contributed improved positions in the far-southern sky. His work forms part of the chain through which Apus became observationally secure rather than merely copied from Keyser/de Houtman.

### 1690: Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius’s *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*, published posthumously at Danzig by Elisabeth Hevelius, incorporated the post-Bayer southern sky. Apus appears as a richly engraved bird and, consistent with the prevailing legend, is represented without functional legs.

Hevelius’s atlas reversed the usual viewpoint: figures are generally shown as though viewed from outside the celestial sphere. This accounts for apparent left-right differences when compared with geocentric maps.

### 1720: abbreviated German Bayer

The shortened German Bayer tradition has **Apus Indica** and **Indianischer Vogel**. This demonstrates that *Apis* did not monopolize reception and that both the Greek and ordinary bird readings coexisted.

### 1751–1756: Lacaille at the Cape

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751–1752. His southern planisphere and 1756 catalogue substantially reorganized southern stellar cartography.

For Apus he:

- retained the constellation;
- assigned the Greek-letter sequence α through κ, including doubled δ and κ;
- removed part of the old, extravagant tail to create space for **Octans**, his navigational-instrument constellation surrounding the south celestial pole.

This is the most important pre-IAU boundary change. Stars once pictorially part of the paradise bird’s tail became Octans. No ancient territorial claim was displaced because neither figure was ancient.

### 1763: *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*

Lacaille’s posthumous full catalogue fixed the revised stellar content and disseminated the name **Apus**. His scientific mapping displaced much of the earlier decorative ambiguity.

### 1776–1782: Fortin and Bode

Jean-Baptiste Fortin’s French reduction of Flamsteed included Lacaille’s southern planisphere, extending the “Flamsteed atlas” tradition into regions Flamsteed himself had not catalogued.

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* (Berlin and Stralsund, 1782) was an enlarged German reworking of the Fortin/Flamsteed atlas. It received **Apus/Paradiesvogel** as an established southern figure.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

Johann Elert Bode’s monumental *Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio* was published at Berlin in twenty large copperplate maps. The plates were issued in installments between 1797 and 1801; the completed atlas bears the date 1801.

Apus appears on the south-polar chart among Pavo, Octans, Chamaeleon, Musca, Triangulum Australe, Ara, and the other far-southern figures. Bode’s treatment combines:

- Latin nomenclature;
- a natural-history-style bird-of-paradise;
- Lacaille’s reduced territorial extent;
- a dense background of telescopic stars;
- constellation figures drawn through, rather than instead of, the scientific star field.

Bode’s accompanying volume, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, rather than the plate volume alone, supplies the systematic description and catalogue. The complete work catalogues approximately **17,240 stars**, doubles, nebulae, and clusters. The “830 stars in 109 groups” phrase in the supplied brief is not the number of Apus stars and appears to describe a separate Bode-collection indexing or grouping system; it could not be securely matched to Bode’s own Apus entry.

Bode retained both strands of the historical name: Latin **Apus** and German **Paradiesvogel**. His work also illustrates why the figure’s original dazzling tail was visually awkward after Lacaille: the new Octans occupies ground formerly available to it.

### 1822: Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* retained Apus in its southern maps. Jamieson’s atlas was a major pictorial intermediary between Bode-era scientific art and popular nineteenth-century constellation teaching.

### 1824–1825: *Urania’s Mirror*

*Urania’s Mirror*, a set of pierced constellation cards now attributed to the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam, copied much of Jamieson’s imagery. Its southern planisphere receives Apus as one element among many rather than devoting a separate card to it.

The cards were intended to be held against light so that pinholes represented stars. Their imagery is sometimes described as derived directly from Bode, but the immediate model was Jamieson. [Royal Astronomical Society](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror)

### 1826–1835: Dunlop, Herschel, and Burritt

James Dunlop discovered NGC 6101 in 1826 from Parramatta. John Herschel confirmed it in 1835 during his southern observing program.

Elijah Burritt’s American *Atlas, Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens* appeared in 1833 and in rapidly revised editions, including 1835. Apus appears on the southern circumpolar map as part of a schoolroom rather than navigational atlas tradition.

### 1843: Argelander

Friedrich Wilhelm August Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* lists Apus and indicates its limits but suppresses the bird picture. This marks the nineteenth-century shift from figural uranography to observational charting.

### Heis and later nineteenth-century atlases

Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* belongs to the same scientific trend: constellation names and lines were retained as locational conventions, while elaborate animal bodies lost authority.

Richard A. Proctor’s *New Star Atlas* (1887) treated “Apus, the Bird of Paradise” as a recognized constellation. Winslow Upton’s 1896 atlas retained it but incorrectly attributed its invention simply to Bayer.

### 1899: Allen

Richard Hinckley Allen’s *Star Names and Their Meanings* gathered the competing names and popularized several durable claims:

- that *Apis Indica* was an engraver’s or printer’s error;
- that the true name was *Avis* or *Apus*;
- that early Chinese observers knew a “Little Wonder Bird” here;
- that the constellation had sometimes been mistaken for a swallow or swift.

Allen is valuable as a historiographical witness but not always reliable as a primary-source guide.

### 1922: Rome

At the first IAU General Assembly in Rome, the Commission on Notations adopted Latin names and three-letter abbreviations for the “88 principal constellations.” **Apus—Aps** was retained.

This did not yet establish its borders. [Original-list discussion](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html)

### 1925–1930: Delporte

At Cambridge in 1925, Eugène Delporte proposed systematic constellation boundaries. The IAU required that known variable stars remain, as far as possible, in the constellations whose designations they already bore.

The boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* in 1930. For the southern sky Delporte rectified boundaries inherited from Benjamin Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina*, replacing oblique arcs with B1875 meridians and parallels.

Apus thereby became not merely a picture or star group but a precise area of sky. Its present neighbors are Ara, Triangulum Australe, Circinus, Musca, Chamaeleon, Octans, and Pavo.

### 2025–2026: Paradys

The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Paradys** for α Apodis on 18 May 2025 and announced it in the 2025–2026 naming cycle. This creates a deliberate modern link between the brightest star and the oldest Dutch constellation name.

## Invention, purpose, and reception

### What did Apus honor?

**Documented context plus scholarly reconstruction:** it did not honor a monarch, saint, patron, machine, or individual voyage commander. It represented one of the spectacular exotic animals entering European collections and literature through the East Indian trade.

The paradise bird was particularly suitable because it joined:

- a genuinely unfamiliar Papuan animal;
- the Moluccan and Malay commercial world encountered by the Dutch;
- the mythology of a creature supposedly never touching earth;
- a far-southern region of sky newly accessible to European seafaring.

There is no evidence that the constellation was intended to commemorate Magellan personally. Pigafetta’s birds belong to the natural-history prehistory of the image, while Apus’s stellar construction belongs to the later Dutch voyages.

### Portuguese versus Dutch sky

Portuguese pilots pioneered practical European navigation in southern latitudes and supplied early reports of southern stars. Vespucci, Corsali, and others described features including the Southern Cross and Magellanic Clouds.

But the evidence for **Apus as this specific bird-shaped constellation** begins with the Dutch Keyser–de Houtman–Plancius complex. Bayer’s broad credit to earlier navigators does not prove that Vespucci or Corsali invented Apus.

No surviving Portuguese chart has been found that shows the same bird before Plancius.

### Was Apus a navigational constellation?

**Qualified fact:** the catalogue arose in a navigation-driven observational project, but Apus itself is faint and lacks a star comparable to Canopus, Achernar, or the Southern Cross. It was more useful for organizing a chart than as a primary steering mark. Claims that explorers routinely “steered by Alpha Apodis” are unverified and implausible in the absence of logbook evidence.

## Myths and legends

## Classical Greek and Roman myth

There is none.

Apus is absent from:

- Aratus, *Phaenomena*
- Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* and its Epitome
- Germanicus’s *Aratea* and scholia
- Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* and *Fabulae*
- Ovid
- Manilius
- Nonnus
- Pseudo-Apollodorus
- Pausanias
- Ptolemy’s constellation catalogue

It therefore has:

- no Greek hero, god, king, nymph, bird, or metamorphosis;
- no catasterism;
- no competing classical identity;
- no ancient author who placed it in the sky;
- no Roman etiological myth.

Modern retellings that give it a Greek myth simply because its name is Greek are **modern inventions**.

## The paradise-bird legend

The closest thing Apus has to a founding myth is early-modern natural history.

According to the legend, the birds:

- had no feet;
- never landed;
- lived on air or dew;
- nested or incubated on the male’s back;
- fell to earth only at death;
- were incorruptible or continually regenerated feathers;
- came from the earthly paradise and were therefore “birds of God.”

These claims first circulated in Europe through reports surrounding the Magellan voyage and through physically footless trade skins. They were elaborated by naturalists and collectors.

The empirical basis was real but misunderstood: Papuan and intermediary traders prepared skins for ornamental trade by removing feet and sometimes wings. The resulting specimens looked like pure masses of celestial plumage.

Pigafetta is often cited as the source of “footless birds.” That is wrong: his surviving account explicitly describes legs. The marvelous footless account is more closely associated with Maximilianus Transylvanus and later natural-history transmission.

## Christianized myth: Eve and the Apple

Schiller’s 1627 transformation placed the Apus region into an Old Testament image identified in concordances as **Eve and the Apple**. This is a seventeenth-century Christian allegory, not a traditional biblical constellation and not evidence of Jewish star lore. It failed to displace Apus.

## Literary afterlife of the footless bird

John Keats’s unfinished *The Eve of St. Mark* contains the phrase “legless birds of Paradise,” cited by Allen as evidence that the early-modern legend survived poetically into the nineteenth century. The phrase refers to the zoological marvel, not demonstrably to the constellation.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian and Egyptian

No secure antecedent exists. These declinations were invisible from the principal observing latitudes. Any proposed mapping is speculative and geometrically untenable without invoking travel far south of the documented traditions.

### Greek and Roman

No constellation or unformed Ptolemaic stars securely correspond to the modern Apus region.

### Arabic and Bedouin

Al-Sufi’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib* reproduces and corrects Ptolemy’s 48 constellations. It has no Apus.

No indigenous Bedouin asterism in these precise stars could be verified. Most of the constellation never rises from Arabia. Arabic names attached to Apus stars in popular modern lists are therefore translations or recent naming projects, not al-Sufi names.

### Persian

No entry corresponding to Apus occurs in the Persian recension of the Ptolemaic tradition or in Ulugh Beg’s catalogue. Modern Persian “bird of paradise” terminology is a translation.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic

No Hebrew biblical constellation or classical Jewish calendrical asterism corresponds to Apus. The identification of Schiller’s figure with Eve does not constitute Hebrew precedent.

No premodern Kabbalistic mapping of these stars was found. Web pages assigning Apus to a sefirah, angel, tribe of Israel, Tarot card, or “Hebrew zodiac” are modern esoteric inventions unless accompanied by a datable premodern manuscript—which the searched examples were not.

### Indian astronomy

No nakshatra reaches these declinations; the lunar mansions follow the ecliptic. Apus has no Vedic, Siddhāntic, or classical Indian constellation equivalent.

### Chinese astronomy: Yìquè

The ancient Chinese system could not include these stars because they lie below the horizon from the main Chinese observing centers.

During the late Ming Jesuit reform of astronomy, Xu Guangqi and European Jesuit collaborators introduced far-southern stars into Chinese uranography. The region became one of the **23 Southern Asterisms**, **異雀 Yìquè**, “Exotic Bird,” “Strange Sparrow,” or “Unusual Bird.”

Its nine reconstructed stars are:

1. Yìquè 1 — ζ Apodis
2. Yìquè 2 — ι Apodis
3. Yìquè 3 — β Apodis
4. Yìquè 4 — γ Apodis
5. Yìquè 5 — δ Octantis
6. Yìquè 6 — δ¹ Apodis
7. Yìquè 7 — η Apodis
8. Yìquè 8 — α Apodis
9. Yìquè 9 — ε Apodis

The crossing into Octans preserves, in a striking way, a stellar grouping closer to pre-Lacaille Apus than to Delporte’s boundary.

**Important qualification:** Yìquè is not evidence that ancient Chinese astronomers independently knew Apus. It is a Chinese accommodation of imported European southern-sky data in the late Ming/early Qing scientific encounter.

Modern Chinese calls the Western constellation **天燕座, Tiānyànzuò**, “Heavenly Swallow Constellation.”

### “Little Wonder Bird”

Allen reported that Chinese tradition knew Apus as the “Little Wonder Bird,” drawing on nineteenth-century authorities such as Ideler and Semler.

**Disputed/likely garbled transmission:** the verifiable Chinese name is Yìquè, “Exotic/Strange Bird.” “Little Wonder Bird” appears to be a picturesque nineteenth-century translation or paraphrase rather than a separate ancient asterism. Its apparent similarity to the European bird is unsurprising because the southern Chinese asterisms were created after European charts arrived.

### Malay and Indonesian context

The Dutch name refers to a real bird group centered in New Guinea and nearby islands, not a known pre-European Malay constellation.

Pigafetta’s *bolon divata/dinata* was glossed “bird of God.” Spellings vary with manuscripts and early transcription of local speech. It is a name for the animal, not demonstrably for the stars.

### Alchemical, Hermetic, and astrological readings

No classical astrological doctrine belongs to Apus because:

- it is far from the ecliptic;
- it was unknown to Ptolemy;
- Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* assigns no planetary nature to its stars;
- no lunar mansion crosses it;
- no star in it is among the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars.

Later occult writers sometimes assign Apus themes of transcendence, flight, exotic beauty, freedom from earthly attachment, or “rising above materiality.” These are **modern symbolic inventions**, usually derived from the footless-bird legend.

Claims that Apus has an ancient zodiacal influence are unsupported.

## Iconography

### Ancient monuments and manuscripts

Apus does not appear on:

- the Farnese Atlas;
- Greco-Roman celestial globes;
- medieval Aratea manuscripts;
- al-Sufi manuscripts;
- Dürer’s 1515 northern and southern celestial maps;
- medieval zodiac calendars;
- cathedral zodiac portals;
- ancient coins as an identifiable constellation.

Birds on ancient monuments cannot be identified as Apus merely because they are birds.

### Plancius–Hondius globe, Amsterdam, 1598

The earliest secure image presents a bird-of-paradise near the south celestial pole. The globe’s form compressed the long ornamental plumage into the available polar space. It established the iconographic type inherited by Bayer.

### Blaeu globe, Amsterdam, 1602/1603

Blaeu reproduced the Plancian southern constellations. Surviving globes and gores are critical witnesses because Keyser’s original notebook is lost.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer’s south-polar plate shows the bird in profile with extravagant plumage. The visible bird contradicts the literal label *Apis Indica*. The plate is the first secure printed atlas depiction, as opposed to a globe.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

The stars were absorbed into the Christianized image of Eve and the Apple. The engravings were associated with Johann Matthias Kager, Lucas Kilian, and Caspar Schecks. Schiller also issued a concave or geocentric counterpart.

### Hevelius, Danzig, 1690

Hevelius shows a richly rendered, footless bird. As elsewhere in his atlas, the celestial viewpoint reverses the orientation familiar from Earth-view charts.

### Bode, Berlin, 1782 and 1801

Bode’s 1782 atlas retains the paradise bird in a reduced, instructional form.

The 1801 *Uranographia* gives a more elaborate natural-history image laid across a dense scientific star field. Apus is subordinate to no mythological scene: it remains an exotic bird, positioned between the newer Octans and older-established southern figures.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson retained the picturesque bird but simplified the scientific density for an educated popular audience.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/1825

Apus appears on the southern planisphere among multiple southern constellations. The card imagery ultimately belongs to the Jamieson-derived chain, not an independent field observation.

### Burritt, United States, 1833–1835

Burritt’s hand-colored southern circumpolar map introduced generations of American students to Apus. Its folk-art style is simpler than Bode’s monumental engraving.

### Argelander and Heis

Their mid-nineteenth-century maps retained the name and stellar convention while stripping away most or all figural art. This change foreshadows the IAU conception of a constellation as territory rather than picture.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

No securely identified coin, medieval ceiling, cathedral portal, or pre-1598 calendar depicting Apus was found. Modern planetarium ceilings and decorative zodiac cycles sometimes include all 88 constellations, but these are receptions of the IAU sky, not independent historical witnesses.

## People, events, and works

### Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser

Chief pilot and principal early observer. He died in Java in 1596 before publishing the work. His Latinized name, *Petrus Theodorus*, caused later writers to mistake him for a different person.

### Frederick de Houtman

Observer, compiler, and publisher. His 1603 catalogue is the earliest surviving printed star list securely connected to the southern twelve. Confusion with his brother Cornelis is common.

### Petrus Plancius

Cartographic organizer and probably a major participant in selecting the constellation figures. He trained Keyser and transformed observational points into a usable globe.

### Jodocus Hondius and Willem Blaeu

Globe makers who physically disseminated Apus. Their objects are evidence independent of later prose accounts.

### Johann Bayer

Made the figure internationally familiar through *Uranometria*. He adopted rather than invented it.

### Johannes Kepler and Jakob Bartsch

Helped normalize **Apus/Avis Indica** in place of Bayer’s *Apis Indica*.

### Lacaille

Gave Apus its enduring Bayer-letter system and reduced its tail to make Octans.

### Bode

Produced the most ambitious artistic-scientific representation of the constellation around 1800 and preserved the historical bird through the transition to telescopic cartography.

### James Dunlop

Discovered NGC 6101 from Parramatta in 1826 with a homemade reflecting telescope. His work created the first substantial independent catalogue of southern deep-sky objects.

### Literature

- Pigafetta’s *Relazione del primo viaggio intorno al mondo* supplies the animal’s earliest famous European eyewitness description.
- Natural histories by Gessner, Aldrovandi, and Clusius developed and challenged the footless-bird tradition.
- Keats’s *The Eve of St. Mark* preserves the poetic “legless birds of Paradise.”
- Richard Hinckley Allen’s *Star Names and Their Meanings* shaped most twentieth-century popular accounts of Apus, including some questionable Chinese material.

### Ships, missions, buildings, and music

“Apus” is used independently in zoology, taxonomy, commercial organizations, and acronyms, making simple name matches unreliable.

No historically important ship or spacecraft could be verified as having been named specifically for the constellation rather than for the swift genus, the acronym APUS, or an unrelated classical word. Claims about an “Apus mission” must therefore be evaluated case by case.

The Apus Supernova Search Program associated with American Public University System is an acronymic institutional use, not evidence for a mission named after the constellation.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Who invented Apus?

**Disputed historically; reconstructed with high confidence.**

- Bayer credited several navigators.
- Smyth and older writers credited Andrea Corsali or “Peter Theodore.”
- Blaeu credited Frederick de Houtman.
- Modern histories often credit Keyser and de Houtman jointly.
- Plancius created the first surviving mapped figure.

The most defensible wording is: **Apus was formed by Plancius from observations made principally by Keyser and transmitted or supplemented by Frederick de Houtman.**

### 2. Was Bayer the inventor?

No. This is a popular error caused by the visibility and influence of *Uranometria*. The Plancius–Hondius globe predates Bayer by about five years.

### 3. Was the first globe dated 1597 or 1598?

Both dates occur. The globe was designed in late 1597 or early 1598 and conventionally cited as **1598**. Exact dating depends upon whether design, engraving, publication, or surviving state is meant.

### 4. Apis or Avis?

*Apis Indica* is documented, but “Indian Bee” conflicts with the image and with the separate constellation Apis/Musca.

Calling it a simple typographical error is very likely but still a reconstruction. The precise responsible person and stage are unknown.

### 5. Did Kepler “correct Bayer”?

Kepler’s 1627 catalogue has *Apus, Avis Indica*, so it corrects the semantic reading in practice. It cannot be proven that Kepler personally diagnosed a printer’s error rather than adopting another existing name.

### 6. Does Apus mean bird-of-paradise?

Not literally. Greek *apous* means “footless.” The bird-of-paradise meaning comes from the Dutch figure and its early-modern natural-history context. Treating *Apus* itself as Latin for bird-of-paradise is erroneous.

### 7. Did Pigafetta say it had no feet?

No. This is the most important popular error. Pigafetta describes long, thin legs. Other reports and the mutilated trade skins carried the footless legend.

### 8. Were all early skins accidentally damaged?

The feet were removed deliberately during local commercial preparation. Reasons included ornament manufacture, compact preservation, and trade practice. Europeans then mistook the prepared skin for the natural body.

### 9. Was it an American bird?

No. Some popular pages say the constellation honored birds discovered “in the Americas.” Birds-of-paradise are centered in New Guinea and nearby islands. The constellation emerged from East Indian voyage culture.

### 10. Did Keyser and de Houtman chart it “around Australia”?

That phrasing is misleading. The observations were made on voyages through southern seas and the East Indies, particularly the route toward Java and Sumatra. They were not a systematic circumnavigation or mapping expedition around the Australian continent.

### 11. Ancient Chinese “Little Wonder Bird”

The phrase is not secure evidence of an ancient independent Chinese Apus. The verifiable Yìquè belongs to the Jesuit-era incorporation of European southern stars. “Little Wonder Bird” appears to be an embellished nineteenth-century translation.

### 12. Did Schiller omit the southern twelve?

No. The lead was disproved. Schiller incorporated Apus into his Christianized Old Testament sky, where concordances associate it with Eve and the Apple.

### 13. House swallow or swift

Some nineteenth-century atlases and dictionaries rendered Apus as a house swallow or black martin, influenced by the classical/zoological use of *apus* for a swift-like bird. This is lexical cross-contamination, not the intended Plancian species.

### 14. Did Lacaille invent Apus?

No. Lacaille retained and reshaped it, assigned Greek letters, and removed part of the tail. He did not originate the bird.

### 15. Did Bode invent or restore it?

Neither. Bode inherited an established constellation. His contribution was iconographic and cartographic reception.

### 16. Did Apus lose stars at the IAU reform?

No important named-star transfer is documented at the 1928–1930 stage. The major loss occurred earlier, when Lacaille formed Octans from the south-polar region and shortened Apus’s tail. Delporte formalized boundaries largely compatible with Gould’s southern system.

### 17. “No Caldwell objects”

False: NGC 6101 is Caldwell 107.

### 18. “No named stars”

This was true before 2025 but is now obsolete. α Apodis is officially **Paradys**.

### 19. R Apodis

The designation survives although the star is not convincingly variable. This is not a hoax; variable-star nomenclature is not normally erased when later observations overturn the original classification.

### 20. Hoaxes

No constellation-specific historical hoax was found. The principal falsehoods are cumulative popular errors: Bayer as inventor, Pigafetta as author of the footless claim, an ancient Chinese origin, an American zoological referent, and supposed classical mythology.

## Open questions

1. **Exact authorship of the figure:** how much of the animal selection was Keyser’s or de Houtman’s and how much Plancius’s remains unresolved because the original notebooks and instructions are lost.

2. **The entry point of *Apis Indica*:** no document yet conclusively identifies whether the error began on a globe manuscript, in engraving, in Bayer’s copy, or in printing.

3. **Exact species intended:** “bird of paradise” was a trade category rather than a modern ornithological determination. The image is not reliable enough to establish *Paradisaea apoda*, *P. minor*, or another species.

4. **Plancius globe dating:** “1597” and “1598” refer to overlapping stages of production; a universally accepted day or month has not been recovered.

5. **The lost Keyser catalogue:** de Houtman’s 1603 publication and early globes permit reconstruction, but not a word-for-word recovery of Keyser’s original.

6. **Bode collection phrase “830 stars in 109 groups”:** this could not be tied securely to the published *Uranographia* or its 17,240-entry companion catalogue. It may belong to a collection-management record, an exhibition dataset, or a separate chain/list supplied outside the digitized books.

7. **“Little Wonder Bird”:** the exact first European source and Chinese phrase behind this translation require a dedicated examination of Semler, Ideler, and nineteenth-century sinological editions.

8. **Schiller’s precise stellar division between Eve and the Apple:** the broad concordance is secure, but a star-by-star mapping requires direct collation of plates LI–LII against Bayer’s and modern coordinates.

9. **Navigation logs:** the observational expedition was navigation-driven, but direct evidence that mariners used Apus itself as a practical steering constellation remains lacking.
