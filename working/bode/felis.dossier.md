# Felis: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented fact** — directly attested by a contemporary chart, catalogue, institutional record, or primary text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern identification inferred from historical coordinates, chart geometry, or comparative research.
- **Tradition** — a repeated historical attribution whose transmission is visible but whose ultimate evidence may be incomplete.
- **Disputed claim** — sources disagree or the surviving evidence does not justify certainty.
- **Unverified claim** — repeated in popular literature but not found in the cited primary source.
- **Myth/legend** — a cultural narrative, not an astronomical-historical assertion.
- **Modern invention** — a recent interpretation, asterism, astrological doctrine, or retrospective association.

Felis has no continuous ancient tradition. It was a deliberately invented Enlightenment constellation whose documented career begins in 1799. Consequently, ancient Egyptian cats, Greek feline myths, Arabic names for Hydra, and Chinese asterisms in the same area belong to parallel traditions, not to Felis’s genealogy.

## Basic identification

### Status

**Status: abolished/obsolete. Felis is not an IAU constellation.**

More precisely, **documented fact:** the International Astronomical Union’s Commission on Notations adopted its canonical list of “88 principal constellations” at the first IAU General Assembly in Rome in May 1922. Felis was not included. There is no evidence of a separate resolution specifically “abolishing Felis.” “Abolished in 1922” is therefore accurate shorthand for omission from the recognized list, not the title of a Felis-specific vote.

In 1930 Eugène Delporte published the official rectilinear boundaries of the 88 constellations. Every position formerly associated with Felis fell inside those standardized constellations. The principal and securely identifiable Felis stars are now in **Hydra**. The cat was drawn immediately north of **Antlia** and west or northwest of **Pyxis**, but the commonly reconstructed bright-star membership does not require assigning any of its principal stars to modern Antlia.

**No IAU abbreviation or genitive exists for the constellation.** “Fel” and *Felis* sometimes appear in modern databases, astrological pages, or retrospective lists, but they were never IAU constellation standards. Latin *Felis* happens to be both nominative and genitive singular, but that grammatical fact does not make it an official IAU genitive.

The IAU did preserve the name indirectly: on **1 June 2018**, its Working Group on Star Names approved **Felis** as the proper name of HD 85951, a star formerly shown in the Cat.

### Location

Bode placed the cat on Plate XIX of the *Uranographia*, crouching:

- below the body or throat of Hydra;
- above Antlia Pneumatica;
- west or northwest of Pyxis Nautica;
- east of the headward portion of Argo Navis as then drawn.

Bode’s companion description says it filled the previously empty region **south of the throat of Hydra**. Lalande described it more broadly as “between Hydra and the Ship,” meaning Argo Navis.

Modern approximate centre: around right ascension 9h30m–10h10m, declination roughly −18° to −27°, although these are reconstructed limits, not legal historical boundaries.

The ultimate etymology of Latin *fēlis/fēlēs* is uncertain. It denoted a cat or catlike animal and became the zoological genus name *Felis*. No Greek, Babylonian, Egyptian, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Sanskrit, or Chinese historical name for **this constellation** exists, because the figure did not exist before Lalande.

### What the figure contains

The surviving evidence does not support one immutable “line figure.” Bode supplied an engraved animal containing many faint stars; Jamieson redrew a cat with a somewhat different artistic outline. Modern connecting-line diagrams are reconstructions.

Bode’s popular *Vorstellung der Gestirne*, in its post-1801 form, was reported by Richard Hinckley Allen as giving the Cat **twenty stars**. Bode’s formal description emphasizes that its stars were only fifth magnitude and fainter.

Seven relatively bright present-day Hydra stars repeatedly associated with Felis are:

| HD 82573 | — | 5.74 | Reconstructed Felis member; now Hydra. |

| HD 85951 / HR 3923 / HIP 48615 | **Felis**, formerly **c Felis** on Bode’s chart | 4.94 | The historically most important member; IAU proper name approved in 2018. |

The exact statement “HD 85951 was the brightest star of Felis” is widespread but not perfectly secure. G Hydrae is normally catalogued slightly brighter. HD 85951 is, however, the star Bode labelled **c** in the cat’s shoulder and the star selected by the IAU to memorialize the constellation. “Brightest” may derive from differing historical magnitudes, differing reconstructed membership, or repetition without checking.

Another star important to the artistic figure is:

- **μ Hydrae = 42 Hydrae**, magnitude about 3.8. Some modern reconstructions put it at or near the centre of the Cat pattern, but Bode’s engraving places the animal below Hydra and its exact membership is ambiguous. Treating μ Hya as the “central star of Felis” is a modern reconstruction, not a universally attested Lalande definition.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

Felis was invented nearly two centuries after Bayer’s *Uranometria* of 1603. Therefore it has no original Bayer designations. Bode used lower-case Latin letters locally, including **c Felis**, but these are not Bayer Greek letters.

The principal reconstructed members generally lack Flamsteed numbers. Flamsteed’s numbering was tied to his accepted constellation divisions and predates Felis. **42 Hydrae (μ Hya)** is the notable nearby star carrying both Bayer and Flamsteed-style identifiers, but its inclusion in Felis is uncertain.

### Boundaries

Felis never received universally accepted polygonal boundaries.

- **Ptolemy, second century CE:** no Felis. The general region belonged to or lay below Hydra, with some stars “unformed,” meaning listed outside the figure proper.
- **Bayer, 1603:** no Felis and no Antlia. The region remained associated primarily with Hydra and the northern reaches of Argo Navis.
- **Hevelius, 1690:** no Felis or Antlia. His new figures did not include a domestic cat here.
- **Lacaille, 1752/1756:** created **Antlia Pneumatica**, the air pump, immediately south of the future Cat. This made the remaining gap between Hydra and Antlia visually conspicuous.
- **Bode, 1801:** inserted Felis between Hydra, Antlia, Pyxis, and Argo. His atlas employed drawn partition lines, but these were cartographic allocations, not an international boundary law.
- **Jamieson, 1822:** retained the Cat as **Le Chat**, with explicit boundary-like lines in his reduced atlas.
- **Delporte, 1930:** the official IAU boundaries assigned the relevant positions to Hydra; adjacent regions belong to Antlia and Pyxis.

The simplified assertion that Felis “took stars from Antlia” is not demonstrated for its seven usual bright members. It occupied a gap above Antlia and appropriated stars otherwise treated as Hydra or as unformed stars. After 1930 its core stars were assigned to Hydra.

## Stars and objects

### HD 85951: Felis

**Documented modern identity:** HD 85951 = HR 3923 = HIP 48615.

- J2000 coordinates: approximately RA 09h 54m 52s, Dec −19° 00′ 33″.
- Visual magnitude: about 4.94.
- Orange-red evolved giant.
- Modern classifications vary between K5 III and M1 III in catalogues. This is a classification difference, not evidence that two stars have been confused.
- Gaia-based distances have changed with catalogue releases; values around 570 light-years are common. Older popular sources give approximately 520–530 light-years.
- Bode marked it **c** in the shoulder or upper torso of the cat.
- The IAU WGSN approved **Felis** on 1 June 2018.

The name is Latin and commemorative, not an inherited Arabic proper name. The IAU’s modern policy expressly permits visible stars to receive names from historical constellations and asterisms where appropriate.

### G Hydrae

G Hydrae, HD 81797, is a K-type evolved star of approximately magnitude 4.7. It may actually outshine HD 85951 and is therefore central to the “brightest star” controversy. “G” belongs to the letter nomenclature used in later catalogues, not Bayer’s original Greek-letter scheme.

### I Hydrae

I Hydrae, around magnitude 4.75, is a B-type star in modern Hydra. Its capital Roman **I** must not be silently converted into Greek **ι Hydrae**. Catalogue typography has encouraged occasional confusion.

### HD 84117

HD 84117 is a nearby G-type dwarf of about fifth magnitude. Modern stellar work makes it scientifically more prominent than it was in the Cat’s chart history, but it never acquired a traditional Felis proper name.

### Asterisms

No historically documented named asterism existed **inside Felis** apart from the cat itself.

Modern attempts to turn Bode’s image into a connect-the-dots pattern are not uniform. One recent observing reconstruction uses:

- four faint stars around HIP 51060 as the head;
- a faint oval body incorporating HIP 51185;
- a tail extending through HIP 51148;
- μ Hydrae as an orienting or central star.

This is a **modern observational reconstruction**, based more closely on the drawn animal than on a formal Lalande catalogue. It should not be mistaken for an eighteenth-century stick figure.

### Deep-sky objects

No nebula, cluster, galaxy, Messier object, or NGC object has an established historical name “Felis” because of membership in this constellation.

There is no legitimate “Felis Nebula,” “Felis Cluster,” or “Felis Galaxy” in the standard historical nomenclature. Searches can return zoological Latin, cat-shaped informal nebular nicknames, or objects named for unrelated persons; these are not relics of Lalande’s constellation.

Because Felis lacked fixed boundaries, it is methodologically impossible to compile a unique exhaustive list of Messier or NGC objects “within Felis.” One can list objects within a chosen modern reconstruction, but a different outline will change the result. No Messier object lies securely within the small core reconstructed from the seven bright stars.

### Variable stars, novae, and supernovae

No historically recorded nova or supernova is documented as having occurred “in Felis” while the name was in general astronomical use.

Variable-star naming requires a recognized constellation genitive. Since Felis disappeared before standardized variable-star nomenclature was extended through the region, there is no stable historical sequence such as R Felis, S Felis, or RR Felis. Variables in the same sky are designated Hydrae, Antliae, or Pyxidis.

Claims that a variable “belongs to Felis” ordinarily mean only that its position overlaps a modern reconstruction of Bode’s drawing.

### Meteor showers

No meteor shower is officially named the Felids or Felisids after this constellation.

Hydra has minor modern shower names such as the Alpha Hydrids and Sigma Hydrids, but those are keyed to modern Hydra radiants and do not constitute a survival of Felis. Meteor radiants move and shower nomenclature is modern; proximity alone does not justify attaching them to Lalande’s Cat.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1603: no Cat in this field

**Documented absence:** Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, and medieval Latin astronomical sources contain no domestic-cat constellation corresponding to Lalande’s field.

The area was subsumed under Hydra, Argo, or unnamed/unformed stars. No cat appears in Ptolemy’s list of 48 constellations, in Aratus, Eratosthenes, Hyginus, or the classical catasterism tradition.

### Second century CE — Ptolemy

Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogued Hydra and neighboring ancient constellations but no Felis. His *Tetrabiblos* assigned the bright stars of Hydra a combined Saturn-and-Venus astrological nature. That judgment attaches to Hydra’s stars, not to a Cat.

### c. 964 — al-Ṣūfī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* (“Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars”) revised the Ptolemaic catalogue and illustrated **al-Shujāʿ**, the Water Snake/Hydra. It contains no Felis.

Faint stars later used by Lalande either belonged to Hydra’s vicinity or were below the naked-eye limits and star selection of al-Ṣūfī’s catalogue. No Arabic cat name can be traced to this group.

### 1515 — Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s printed celestial maps, prepared with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, depicted the traditional Ptolemaic sky. No Felis appears.

### 1603 — Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* depicted Hydra and Argo Navis. It did not contain Antlia or Felis. None of Lalande’s later Cat letters is a Bayer designation.

### 1627 — Julius Schiller

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, published at Augsburg with assistance connected to Bayer and engravings by Lucas Kilian, replaced Hydra with **Iordanis Fluvius**, the River Jordan. Since Felis had not yet been invented, Schiller did not replace it with a saint or Christian object.

If one maps the later Felis field onto Schiller’s plate, it falls in or beside the Jordan River. This is a geographical overlay, not a Christian “identity of Felis.”

### 1690 — Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* retained Hydra and created several new constellations elsewhere. He introduced no domestic cat in this region.

### 1752–1756 — Lacaille and Antlia

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille introduced **la Machine Pneumatique**, later Latinized as Antlia Pneumatica, from southern observations at the Cape. The first public chart appeared in the 1756 *Mémoires* for the Académie Royale des Sciences, based on work announced in 1752.

This created the southern neighbor against which Felis would later be drawn. Antlia honored a scientific instrument, not a myth.

### 1799 — Lalande’s invention

**Documented fact:** Lalande’s own chronological history, under the events of 1799, records that Bode sent him further sheets of the large new sky maps. Lalande wrote:

> “Le grand nombre d’étoiles que je lui ai fournies, me donnait quelque droit de former de nouvelles constellations, pour remplir les vides. Il y avait déjà trente-trois animaux dans le ciel; j’en ai mis un trente-quatrième, le Chat…”

Translation: “The large number of stars I had supplied him gave me some right to form new constellations to fill the gaps. There were already thirty-three animals in the sky; I added a thirty-fourth, the Cat.”

He continued that it was inspired by verses from a “charming poem” by the representative **Desherbiers**, and that the new Cat lay between Hydra and the Ship and had already been engraved in Germany.

The poet was **Claude-Antoine Guyot-Desherbiers**, associated with a *Poème du Chat*. Thus the oldest securely documented motive is not merely “Lalande liked cats,” but:

1. he had contributed star positions to Bode;
2. he believed this entitled him to fill an empty area;
3. he wanted a thirty-fourth celestial animal;
4. he connected it with Desherbiers’s cat poem;
5. the gesture was knowingly playful.

### The famous “scratching” quotation

The English quotation popularized by Richard Hinckley Allen reads:

> “I am very fond of cats. I will let this figure scratch on the chart. The starry sky has worried me quite enough in my life, so that now I can have my joke with it.”

A fuller modern rendering circulates as:

> “I have inserted between the Ship and the Cup a new constellation, a Cat … I greatly love these animals … I will have it engraved on the charts; the starry sky has tired me enough in my life that I can now have my fun with it.”

**Contested transmission:** this wording was not found in the relevant Felis passage of the 1803 *Bibliographie astronomique*. That passage instead contains the documented “thirty-three animals” explanation and Desherbiers reference. Allen’s 1899 book helped establish the English quote, but did not provide a precise earlier locus for it.

The quote may derive from another Lalande publication, letter, or paraphrase, but attribution specifically to the *Bibliographie astronomique* is unverified. The defensible conclusion is:

- Lalande’s playful attitude is documented.
- The exact “scratch on the chart” wording has a traceable late-nineteenth-century English transmission.
- Its original French source remains an open bibliographical question.

### 1801 — Bode’s *Uranographia*

**First surviving chart depiction:** Johann Elert Bode, *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio*, Berlin, 1801, Plate XIX.

The plate labels the figure **FELIS**. The cat crouches beneath Hydra, above Antlia, with a wary or grumpy-looking face. The drawing is not mythological: it is a recognizable domestic cat.

Bode’s companion catalogue, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, states in substance:

> This constellation has only recently been proposed by de Lalande to fill the previously unoccupied region south of Hydra’s throat. It consists only of stars of the fifth magnitude and below.

That is the earliest descriptive catalogue notice presently located.

Bode’s atlas comprised twenty large engraved charts and its companion catalogue listed **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters**. Claims that this work contained only “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be reconciled with the primary title or catalogue. They likely conflate another atlas, collection, or modern metadata record with the *Uranographia*. Bode did show over one hundred named figures when obsolete subdivisions and proposed constellations are counted, but 830 is not the *Uranographia* star total.

### 1803 — Lalande’s published retrospective

The *Bibliographie astronomique*, printed in Paris in Year XI/1803, is sometimes misdated **1805** in Allen and derivative sources. The title page and BnF catalogue establish 1803. The Cat discussion occurs near printed page **812–813** in the history for 1799, not page 428.

### 1805 and later Bode popular editions

Bode’s revised *Vorstellung der Gestirne* adopted the German name **Katze**. Allen reports twenty stars. This helped carry Felis from an expensive folio atlas into smaller-format practical and educational maps.

The original 1782 edition necessarily did not include Felis; references to “Bode’s *Die Gestirne*” must distinguish the post-1801 revised edition from 1782.

### 1822 — Alexander Jamieson

**Documented reception:** Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas*, London, 1822, chart 26 in the atlas sequence/catalogued as David Rumsey 3911.027, bears the title:

> “Hydra, Sextans Uraniae, Le Chat, La Machine Pneumatique.”

Jamieson called it **Le Chat**, retaining the French name even in an English atlas. His cat is more naturalistically modelled than Bode’s and appears beneath Hydra. The plate was drawn for 1820 and published in 1822 by G. & W. B. Whittaker, T. Cadell, and N. Hailes; Neele & Son engraved it.

Jamieson’s retention is important evidence that Felis was not confined to one Bode plate. Nevertheless, it never achieved universal catalogue acceptance.

### 1824–1825 — *Urania’s Mirror*

The anonymous card set *Urania’s Mirror; or, A View of the Heavens* was first advertised in late 1824 and commonly dated 1825. Card 32 includes Felis with Hydra, Noctua, Corvus, Crater, Sextans Uraniae, Lupus, Centaurus, Antlia Pneumatica, Argo Navis, and Pyxis Nautica.

The plates were engraved by **Sidney Hall** and closely copied Jamieson. Modern research attributes the design formerly advertised as “by a lady” to the Rev. **Richard Rouse Bloxam**. The accompanying *Familiar Treatise on Astronomy* was by Jehoshaphat Aspin.

The card’s punched star holes made Felis part of domestic astronomical entertainment—the most culturally apt setting for Lalande’s domestic animal.

### Mid-nineteenth century

Felis appeared sporadically in European popular charts and encyclopedias but was omitted from many professional catalogues. Flamsteed could not have included it because he died in 1719 and his atlas appeared in 1729.

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) and Eduard Heis’s later naked-eye uranography helped establish a more conservative working constellation canon. Their influence favored practical star identification rather than preservation of every decorative eighteenth-century invention. Felis did not become part of that durable standard.

American atlas makers generally received it less consistently than Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*. Elijah Burritt’s widely circulated atlas preserved many obsolete figures, but Felis is not as consistently attested in its editions as Noctua, Taurus Poniatovii, or other lost constellations. Any assertion that “Burritt accepted Felis” requires an edition-and-plate citation.

### 1870 — Proctor’s unrelated proposal

Richard A. Proctor proposed using the shortened label **Felis** for **Canis Minor**, apparently as part of a compact rationalization of chart names. This had nothing to do with Lalande’s Cat field. He later abandoned the idea.

This creates a genuine identification trap: “Felis” in Proctor’s proposal can mean renamed Canis Minor, whereas historical Felis lies between Hydra and Antlia.

### 1878 — Secchi

Father Angelo Secchi included the constellation as **Gatto** on an Italian planisphere of 1878. Allen treated this as its latest notable nineteenth-century survival.

### 1882–1899 — declared superfluous

Camille Flammarion wrote that the Cat was “more than superfluous” and that its stars had returned to Hydra and Antlia. This is a nineteenth-century critical judgment, not an IAU decision.

In 1899 Richard Hinckley Allen included a short historical notice in *Star-Names and Their Meanings*. Allen fixed several facts in later popular memory but also introduced persistent problems:

- 1805 instead of 1803 for Lalande’s book;
- the unexplained “scratch on the chart” quotation;
- the alternative spelling *Faelis*;
- an overly simple claim that Secchi was nearly the sole adopter.

### 1922 — omitted by the IAU

At Rome the IAU adopted the standard list of constellations and abbreviations. Felis was absent. It consequently had no official abbreviation, genitive, variable-star nomenclature, or institutional existence.

### 1930 — Delporte boundaries

Delporte’s *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* gave the recognized constellations exact boundaries in coordinates referred to epoch B1875.0. The core Felis field became Hydra. Neighboring southern portions fell under the already retained Antlia and Pyxis.

### 2018 — restoration as a star name

On 1 June 2018, the IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Felis** for HD 85951. This was not restoration of the constellation. It was a deliberate historical memorial attached to a single star formerly marked **c Felis**.

### Present

Felis survives in:

- historical star-atlas scholarship;
- reproductions of Bode and *Urania’s Mirror*;
- lists of obsolete constellations;
- observing projects that reconstruct lost asterisms;
- the IAU proper name **Felis** for HD 85951;
- modern astrology, where meanings were assigned retrospectively.

## Invention and reception

### Inventor

**Inventor: Joseph Jérôme Lefrançois de Lalande.**

The date is **1799**, supported by his own history of that year. He did not publish a known independent Felis globe or atlas. He communicated the proposal and relevant stellar data to Johann Elert Bode.

### First chart

**Johann Elert Bode, Berlin, 1801, *Uranographia*, Plate XIX.**

Bode was the first cartographer to depict it. This explains occasional wording that Bode “introduced” Felis. The most exact attribution is:

- Lalande conceived and proposed it;
- Bode first drew and published it.

### What it honored

Felis did not honor a monarch, patron, voyage, instrument, or discovery. It honored the domestic cat, Lalande’s taste for cats, and—by Lalande’s own account—Desherbiers’s cat poem. It also functioned as a humorous act of gap-filling.

### Lalande and Voltaire

A popular story says that Voltaire disliked cats and mocked their absence from the thirty-three celestial animals, so Lalande created Felis to spite him.

**Status: tradition/unverified motive.**

The story is widely repeated in German and popular astronomical writing. The located primary Lalande passage does not mention Voltaire. It mentions Desherbiers. Voltaire died in 1778, twenty-one years before the invention, so this could only have been a posthumous joke.

No contemporary Voltaire passage containing the alleged “thirty-three animals” taunt was located. Until such a citation is produced, the Voltaire story should be treated as a later embellishment, possibly inferred from Lalande’s “thirty-three animals” sentence and the known cultural stereotype of Voltaire as a cat-hater.

### Why it failed

No single contemporary act killed Felis. Its disappearance was gradual:

- it contained no conspicuously bright pattern;
- it subdivided an already recognized region of Hydra;
- it was absent from many working catalogues;
- nineteenth-century professional atlases increasingly reduced decorative figures;
- standardization favored constellations with sustained international usage;
- the IAU omitted it in 1922.

The claim that it failed “because the stars were too faint” is plausible and repeated, but faintness alone cannot explain every obsolete constellation. Institutional uptake mattered more.

## Myths and legends

## Classical mythology

### No classical Felis catasterism

**Documented absence:** Felis is not in:

- Aratus, *Phaenomena*;
- Eratosthenes or the *Epitome of the Catasterisms*;
- Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica*;
- Hyginus, *Fabulae*;
- the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus’s Aratea and scholia;
- Ovid’s *Fasti* or *Metamorphoses*;
- Manilius, *Astronomica*;
- Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*;
- Pausanias, *Description of Greece*;
- Ptolemy’s *Almagest*.

Consequently:

- no god placed Felis in the sky;
- it has no competing Greek or Roman identity;
- it does not represent a transformed nymph, monster, divine animal, or heroic companion;
- there is no classical myth in which “the constellation’s cat” acts.

Stories about lions, lynxes, panthers, Artemis’s animals, Dionysiac felines, or the Egyptian gods fleeing into animal shapes are not myths of Felis.

### Hydra myths occupying the earlier field

Before 1799 the relevant traditional figure was Hydra. Classical sources variously connected Hydra with:

1. **The Lernaean Hydra slain by Heracles.** This is the best-known modern identification, associated with Heracles’ second labor; see Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 2.5.2, Diodorus Siculus 4.11, Hyginus, *Fabulae* 30. The transfer from monster-myth to the long water snake in the sky is secondary and not uniform.

2. **Apollo’s water snake, raven, and cup.** In the catasterism joining Hydra, Corvus, and Crater, Apollo sends the raven for water. The raven delays while waiting for figs to ripen, then returns with a water snake as a false excuse. Apollo places raven, cup, and snake in the sky; the snake prevents the raven from reaching the cup. This appears in the Eratosthenic *Catasterisms* tradition and Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* 2.40.

These are myths of Hydra and its neighboring figures, not an ancient mythology for Felis. Bode’s cat visually sits beneath the snake but is not part of the Apollo narrative.

## Egyptian cat traditions

### The Great Cat of Heliopolis

Egyptian religion contains a famous celestial-cosmic feline, but it is not Lalande’s constellation.

**Myth documented in funerary literature:** Book of the Dead, Spell 17, attests the deceased or solar deity as the “great cat” beside the *ished* tree at Heliopolis on the night when the enemies of the Lord of the Universe were destroyed. Vignettes show a cat cutting or attacking the serpent Apophis/Apep beneath a sacred tree.

The Great Cat is commonly interpreted as a form of **Ra** protecting solar order from the chaos serpent. This is a religious and iconographic association, not a securely mapped stellar constellation at RA 9h–10h.

The tantalizing opposition of a cat and serpent should not be used to claim that Egyptian astronomers anticipated Lalande’s Cat crouching below Hydra. No positional chain connects the Egyptian vignette to Bode’s field.

### Bastet and Sekhmet

Bastet and Sekhmet are feline Egyptian goddesses with extensive cult traditions. Neither is documented as the ancient identity of Felis’s stars. Modern “Bastet constellation” or “Egyptian Cat constellation” diagrams are contemporary inventions unless they identify a specific Egyptian astronomical ceiling and defend its stellar mapping.

### Dendera

The Dendera zodiac belongs to the Greco-Roman period and combines Egyptian and Hellenistic imagery. It contains many disputed figures, but no scholarly consensus identifies Lalande’s precise field as a domestic-cat constellation.

The Egyptian sky is therefore a meaningful comparative feline tradition, but not an antecedent of Felis.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

No Babylonian domestic-cat constellation has been securely identified in the Felis field. Babylonian influence on the zodiac does not establish an ancestry for every later Western figure. The future Cat lies well south of the ecliptic and was created from faint gap stars.

### Greek

The Greek name for the surrounding ancient constellation is **Ὕδρα**, Hydra, “water snake.” No Greek cat name is attached to the Felis stars.

### Latin

The region was treated under **Hydra**, **Serpens Aquaticus**, or nearby Argo Navis. Bode’s *Felis* is New Latin astronomical nomenclature.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Hydra was Arabic **al-Shujāʿ** or **al-Shujāʿ al-māʾī**, “the Water Snake.” This translated or adapted the Greek constellation rather than naming Lalande’s faint subdivision.

No traditional Arabic proper names are established for HD 85951, G Hya, I Hya, or the other securely reconstructed Felis stars. Names such as **Alphard** (“the solitary one”) belong elsewhere in Hydra.

The great Bedouin asterisms often cut across Greco-Ptolemaic constellations. No independently named Bedouin cat or coherent traditional asterism has been securely traced to the exact Felis star set. Claims that “the Arabs called Felis X” should be rejected unless they cite an Arabic star catalogue or ethnographic source.

### Persian

Persian astronomical literature largely transmitted the Ptolemaic/Arabic constellation Hydra in this sector. No Persian Cat corresponding to Lalande’s figure has been located. Persian words for cat, such as *gorbe*, found in poetry or folklore do not create a star identification.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic traditions

No securely documented Hebrew constellation mapping identifies these stars as a cat. Biblical and rabbinic star names usually discussed in constellation literature—*Kesil*, *Kimah*, *Ash*, *Mazzaroth*—do not correspond to Felis.

Kabbalistic and occult charts sometimes map Hebrew letters, tribes, angels, or zodiacal powers onto the heavens. No pre-1799 mapping of Lalande’s exact stars to a feline figure was located. Modern claims of a “Kabbalistic Felis” are unverified.

### Chinese

The Felis stars lie in the broad southern region divided among Chinese asterisms belonging to the Vermilion Bird sky and, farther east, the Azure Dragon. Chinese stellar organization does not follow Western Hydra’s outline.

Secure Chinese identifications are clearer for other parts of Hydra—such as **Liu**, the Willow mansion, in Hydra’s head, and **Ping**, “Justice,” represented by γ and π Hydrae—than for the faint stars Lalande used farther south and west.

No traditional Chinese asterism equivalent to the Cat as a whole has been established. Later “additional stars,” especially those incorporated after Jesuit star mapping, further complicate exact identification. A coordinate-by-coordinate comparison with the Qing-era *Yixiang Kaocheng* would be required before assigning individual faint Felis members.

### Indian

The future Felis field is far south of the main nakṣatra belt. **Āśleṣā**, the serpent-associated lunar mansion, is connected with the head of Hydra/Cancer region, not Lalande’s Cat. No Indian domestic-cat constellation matching Felis has been traced.

### Christianized sky

In Julius Schiller’s 1627 *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Hydra became **Iordanis Fluvius**, the River Jordan. Felis did not yet exist. On a positional overlay, the later Cat stars lie in or below the Jordan figure.

They did not represent a particular saint, and the often-repeated question “which saint replaced Felis?” has no historical answer.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No pre-modern alchemical or Hermetic interpretation of Felis is possible under that name because the constellation postdates the classical, Arabic, and medieval Hermetic star corpora.

The **fifteen Behenian stars** do not include HD 85951 or another Felis star. The *Quindecim Stellis* tradition therefore supplies no historical seal, stone, herb, or talisman for Felis.

Modern occult correspondences assigning secrecy, domesticity, revenge, stealth, or feminine power to Felis are post-nineteenth-century inventions.

### Astrology

Ptolemy, *Tetrabiblos* 1.9, says the bright stars of Hydra have the nature of Saturn and Venus. This applies generically to ancient Hydra’s bright stars and is not a Felis delineation.

Vivian E. Robson’s *Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology* (1923) gave Felis a nature described as quiet, cautious, watchful, careful, stealthy, cruel, revengeful, destructive, attached to home, and fond of unusual subjects. He also connected cat-loving with Virgo.

**Status: modern astrological invention.** Robson wrote after Felis had already failed to enter the IAU canon. No such doctrine appears in Lalande or Bode, and it is not classical astrology.

The future Felis field lies under tropical longitudes now falling mainly in late Leo and Virgo, depending on the chosen outline and epoch. This does not make Felis a zodiac constellation: the Sun does not cross the small historical core south of the ecliptic. Claims that Felis was “the thirteenth zodiacal cat” arise from oversized modern reconstructions.

### Lunar mansions

No standard Arabic manzil, Indian nakṣatra, or Chinese lunar mansion is defined by the seven reconstructed Felis stars. Nearby Hydra traditions should not be collapsed into the Cat.

## Iconography

### Ancient monuments and manuscripts

Felis does not appear on:

- the Farnese Atlas;
- classical Aratean manuscripts;
- al-Ṣūfī’s medieval manuscript tradition;
- Dürer’s 1515 maps;
- Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria*;
- Schiller’s 1627 Christian atlas;
- Hevelius’s 1690 atlas;
- Lacaille’s original Antlia chart.

Any image labelling a cat in these works is either a modern overlay, a different animal, or a misidentification.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Plate XIX is the foundational image. The cat is stocky, crouched, and naturalistic rather than heraldic. It is seen in profile beneath the long body of Hydra. The letter **c** appears near its shoulder and corresponds to HD 85951.

Place: Berlin.  
Medium: large engraved celestial plate.  
Status: first documented depiction.

### Bode’s smaller atlas

Post-1801 editions of *Vorstellung der Gestirne* reproduce the figure at smaller scale under the German **Katze**. These images broadened public reception beyond owners of the folio *Uranographia*.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* shows **Le Chat** on the Hydra/Antlia plate. The animal is more smoothly and naturalistically drawn than Bode’s grumpy cat. It appears integrated into a clearly partitioned celestial landscape.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/1825

Card 32 reproduces the Jamieson-derived Cat in hand-colored engraved form. Holes pierced at star positions let light shine through. This is now one of the most widely reproduced Felis images.

The card was long said to be “designed by a lady.” Peter Hingley’s research attributed it to Richard Rouse Bloxam. The old attribution remains part of the object’s publication history, not a deliberate hoax.

### Secchi, Rome, 1878

Secchi’s planisphere labels the figure **Gatto**. It is an important late survival but much less visually influential than Bode or *Urania’s Mirror*.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

No historical coin, cathedral portal, monumental astronomical ceiling, or medieval calendar has been found that can legitimately be identified as Felis. Cat imagery on such objects predates Lalande and has independent symbolic meanings.

Modern merchandise reproducing Bode’s cat is reception history, not evidence of earlier attestation.

## People, events, and works

### Joseph Jérôme Lefrançois de Lalande

Lalande was a major French astronomer, teacher, ephemeris editor, bibliographer, and public lecturer. Felis was a minor humorous episode within a career that included lunar theory, planetary work, navigation, and the coordination of large star catalogues.

His role was conceptual and promotional. There is no evidence that he personally engraved a Felis map.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode, director of the Berlin Observatory, made Felis visible and durable by including it in the most ambitious artistic star atlas of its generation. His editorial generosity preserved many proposed constellations that otherwise might have vanished immediately.

### Claude-Antoine Guyot-Desherbiers

Lalande explicitly named Desherbiers’s cat poem as an occasion for the constellation. This connection is better documented than the Voltaire anecdote, yet it is often omitted from popular accounts.

### Alexander Jamieson

Jamieson transmitted Felis into English-language popular astronomy. His plate became the immediate model for *Urania’s Mirror*.

### Angelo Secchi

Secchi’s **Gatto** is the last repeatedly cited professional/popular planisphere appearance before standardization.

### Richard Hinckley Allen

Allen preserved the Cat’s story for twentieth-century readers. He also transmitted the questionable 1805 date and unlocated scratching quotation, illustrating how a secondary reference can preserve and distort at the same time.

### Eugène Delporte

Delporte did not personally choose to reject Felis. His task was to draw exact boundaries for the 88-constellation list already adopted. His 1930 work made Felis’s disappearance spatially irreversible in official astronomy.

### IAU Working Group on Star Names

The WGSN restored the word **Felis** to formal astronomical nomenclature in 2018 as the name of HD 85951. The group did not revive the constellation.

### Exploration and navigation

No explorer or navigator is documented as steering by “Felis.” Its stars are faint, it had a short and inconsistently accepted career, and sailors had much more useful nearby references. Claims that Lalande’s Cat guided voyages are unsupported.

### Literature and art

Desherbiers’s poem is the only literary work explicitly named by Lalande in connection with the invention.

Joseph Cornell later used obsolete constellation imagery, including Felis, in astronomical collage contexts. This is twentieth-century artistic reception, not continuity of constellation use.

No major ship or space mission has been securely shown to have been named specifically after Lalande’s constellation. The French space cat **Félicette** is an attractive thematic parallel but not a namesake of Felis.

## Controversies and disputes

### Who invented it: Lalande or Bode?

**Resolution:** Lalande invented/proposed the constellation in 1799; Bode first charted it in 1801. Calling either man the sole “creator” without qualification suppresses half the process.

### 1798, 1799, 1800, 1801, or 1805?

- **1799:** Lalande’s documented invention.
- **1801:** first chart and catalogue publication by Bode.
- **1803:** publication date of Lalande’s retrospective *Bibliographie astronomique*.
- **1805:** erroneous or edition-confused date popularized by Allen.
- **1798:** appears in some websites, perhaps confused with Lalande’s proposals for Globus Aerostaticus or Officina Typographica.
- **1800:** approximate dating sometimes attached to Bode’s preparation or engraving.

### “Bibliographie astronomique, page 428”

This page number circulates in biographical references to Lalande but is not the Felis passage in the digitized 1803 edition examined here. The relevant discussion is around printed pages 812–813. Edition differences should be demonstrated rather than assumed.

### The scratching quotation

The sentiment is plausible and compatible with Lalande’s documented humor. Its attribution to the *Bibliographie astronomique* is not verified. The exact words appear prominently in Allen and later writers, but the source chain before Allen remains unclear.

### The Voltaire feud

The anecdote is popular but lacks a located Voltaire passage or mention in Lalande’s primary Felis paragraph. It is best labelled **later tradition**, not documented motive.

### Was HD 85951 the brightest star?

Disputed. G Hydrae is normally slightly brighter in modern V magnitudes. HD 85951 may have been historically rated differently, may have held a more prominent position in Bode’s animal, or may have become “brightest” through repetition after receiving the IAU name.

### Was μ Hydrae part of Felis?

Uncertain. Some modern reconstructions treat it as central; the cat was explicitly inserted south of Hydra, and the historical line between a star in Hydra’s body and a star allocated to Felis was not standardized. The safest description calls μ Hya an orienting star near or within some reconstructed outlines.

### Did its stars go to Hydra and Antlia?

The historical area borders both, and Flammarion said the stars returned to Hydra and Antlia. Yet the seven commonly enumerated bright members are now Hydra stars. Assertions of specific Antlia members require a defined historical outline and coordinate identification.

### “Felis had Bayer letters”

Bode’s **c Felis** resembles a Bayer-style designation but was assigned by Bode, not Bayer. It is better called a Bode letter designation. Modern “c Fel” is historical notation, not an IAU identifier.

### “Felis has abbreviation Fel and genitive Felis”

These are retrospective conventions. The IAU never standardized either for the constellation. Confusion arises because the star proper name Felis is official and because *Felis* is grammatically both nominative and genitive in Latin.

### “Felis was restored in 2018”

Only the name was restored, and only as a stellar proper name. The constellation remains obsolete.

### “Egyptians had the same Cat”

False as stated. Egyptian solar-cat mythology is genuine; positional identity with Lalande’s Cat is not demonstrated. Similarity between the Egyptian cat attacking Apep and Bode’s cat below Hydra is symbolically striking but historically unsupported.

### “A Felis meteor shower/nebula exists”

No standard astronomical nomenclature supports this. Informal cat-shaped objects and unrelated zoological uses of *felis* are frequent sources of search-result contamination.

### “830 stars in 109 groups”

No confirmation was found for this as a description of Bode’s *Uranographia*. Its companion catalogue explicitly announces 17,240 objects/stars. “Over one hundred constellation figures” is defensible depending on counting rules; “830 stars” belongs to some other catalogue, exhibit, collection, or metadata chain unless a specific source is produced.

### Misprints and catalogue confusion

- **Pelis** sometimes appears in OCR or dealer descriptions of Jamieson plates. It is not a historical alternative name.
- **Antilia** is a frequent older or erroneous spelling of Antlia.
- **I Hydrae** can be misread as Greek iota Hydrae.
- **1805** for Lalande’s book is a persistent bibliographical error.
- **Faelis** is a lexicographical spelling preference, not Bode’s label.
- Modern catalogues disagree between K5 III and M1 III for HD 85951; this is scientific classification variance, not a naming hoax.

## Open questions

1. **What is the earliest exact source of the “scratch on the chart” quotation?** Allen is a clear transmitter, but the original French publication or letter remains unidentified.

2. **Did Voltaire ever make the alleged thirty-three-animal remark?** No exact work, letter, date, or passage was located.

3. **Can all twenty stars of Bode’s smaller-atlas Katze be identified securely?** This requires astrometric reduction from a high-resolution plate or recovery of Bode’s associated tabular entry, accounting for precession and engraving error.

4. **Which faint stars did Lalande himself send to Bode?** Bode’s catalogue and surviving Lalande–Bode correspondence may permit a document-level reconstruction.

5. **Was G Hydrae intentionally excluded, resolving the brightest-star problem?** Only a rigorous overlay of Bode’s plate and catalogue can decide.

6. **Which stars, if any, crossed from Lacaille’s Antlia into Bode’s Felis?** Modern summaries often assert this without identifying individual objects.

7. **How widely was Felis used between 1825 and 1878?** A systematic census of national atlas editions—French, German, Italian, British, Spanish, Portuguese, and American—would refine the impression of sporadic survival.

8. **What does “830 stars in 109 groups” refer to in the stated Bode-collection chain?** It does not match the documented *Uranographia* catalogue and needs its originating catalogue record or collection label.
