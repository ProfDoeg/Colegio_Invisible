# Leo Minor: Research Dossier

## Status and evidentiary key

**Status: kept by the International Astronomical Union.** Leo Minor is one of the 88 official constellations. Its modern abbreviation is **LMi**, its Latin genitive is **Leonis Minoris**, and its boundaries are those prepared by Eugène Delporte, approved by the IAU in 1928 and published in 1930. None of its territory was abolished or reassigned after 1930. It now borders Ursa Major, Lynx, Leo, and—at one corner—Cancer. [IAU constellation list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), [IAU Style Manual, table 11](https://iauarchive.eso.org/static/publications/stylemanual1989.pdf)

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact**: stated in, or directly recoverable from, a contemporary text, catalogue, chart, manuscript, or official scientific record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction**: a modern identification or interpretation based on positional, textual, or comparative evidence.
- **Tradition**: genuinely transmitted lore, but not necessarily an empirically testable fact.
- **Disputed**: competing readings survive or a commonly repeated claim conflicts with primary evidence.
- **Myth/legend**: a narrative intended as mythic or legendary rather than historical reporting.
- **Modern invention**: a recent story, symbolic system, or naming practice without evidence of ancient transmission.
- **Unverified claim**: found in secondary or popular sources but not traceable here to adequate primary evidence.

Because Leo Minor was created in the seventeenth century, **there is no ancient Greek or Roman myth, catasterism, Babylonian constellation, Egyptian constellation, biblical figure, Indian nakṣatra, or medieval Arabic Ptolemaic constellation called Leo Minor**. Ancient evidence concerns the stars now inside its boundaries, not Hevelius’s lion.

## Basic identification

### Official identity

| Field | Information |
|---|---|
| Official name | **Leo Minor** |
| Meaning | “Lesser Lion” or “Smaller Lion” |
| IAU abbreviation | **LMi** |
| Genitive | **Leonis Minoris** |
| Status | Official IAU constellation, retained |
| Creator | Johannes Hevelius |
| First securely documented presentation | Catalogue and atlas dated 1687, published posthumously at Gdańsk/Danzig in 1690 |
| Area | Approximately **232 square degrees**, 64th-largest of the 88 |
| Approximate modern span | RA about 9h 22m–11h 06m; Dec about +22.8°–+41.4° |
| Neighbours | Ursa Major, Lynx, Leo, and a point contact with Cancer |
| Brightest star | 46 Leonis Minoris, officially named **Praecipua**, V ≈ 3.8 |
| Messier objects | None |
| Traditional outline | Usually a shallow triangle or four-star angular figure, although no one official stick figure exists |

The IAU defines a constellation as a bounded **area**, not as one mandatory line drawing. Delporte’s boundaries follow right ascensions and declinations referred to the equinox B1875.0; modern plotted coordinates are precessed from those lines. Leo Minor’s outline is a sixteen-segment polygon in the usual count. [IAU](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### Names and etymologies

**Latin — documented fact.** *Leo Minor* consists of *leo*, “lion,” and the comparative adjective *minor*, “smaller/lesser.” The possessive form is *Leonis Minoris*. Hevelius also used or was reported as considering **Leo Junior**, “the younger lion.” *Junior* would emphasize age; *minor* emphasizes comparative size or rank.

**Greek — negative finding.** No ancient Greek name corresponds to this constellation. Greek *λέων* (*leōn*, lion) belongs to the ancient zodiacal Leo. The region’s unattached stars could be termed *ἀμόρφωτοι* (*amorphōtoi*), “unformed” or “not incorporated into the figure,” in the Ptolemaic catalogue tradition. Translating Leo Minor mechanically as *Mikròs Léōn* is modern Greek usage, not an ancient constellation name.

**German — documented modern vernacular.** *Kleiner Löwe*, “Little/Lesser Lion.” Bode’s accompanying 1801 text is German with a parallel French translation; the atlas plates retain Latin constellation labels.

**French.** *Petit Lion*.

**Italian.** *Leone Minore*; older English antiquarian literature also records *Leoncino*, “little lion.”

**Spanish.** *León Menor*. This is a translation of Hevelius’s Latin, not an independent Iberian asterism.

**English.** Lesser Lion, Smaller Lion, Little Lion.

**Dutch.** *Kleine Leeuw*.

**Polish.** *Mały Lew*.

**Persian — negative finding.** Modern Persian can translate the IAU name as *Šir-e Kuček* (“little lion”), but no independent Persian constellation of this name has been traced. Al-Ṣūfī was ethnically Persian but wrote his astronomical synthesis in Arabic and retained Ptolemy’s 48 figures.

**Arabic — two distinct systems must not be conflated.**

1. **Ptolemaic Arabic:** al-Ṣūfī’s constellation here remained *Ṣūrat al-Asad*, “the figure of the Lion,” for ancient Leo; he had no Leo Minor.
2. **Indigenous Arabian/Bedouin star lore:** stars in and around modern Leo Minor, Lynx, and southern Ursa Major belonged to complexes called **al-Ẓibāʾ**, “the gazelles,” **awlād al-ẓibāʾ**, “the young of the gazelles,” and **qafazāt al-ẓibāʾ**, “the leaps of the gazelles.” The mapping is not identical to Delporte’s Leo Minor and varies among Ibn Qutayba, al-Marzūqī, al-Ṣūfī, globes, and later interpreters. [All Skies Encyclopaedia, al-Ẓibāʾ](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Ziba), [Arab navigation study](https://rutter-project.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/acevedo_benard_tn8.pdf)

**Hebrew — negative finding.** Modern Hebrew uses a translation such as *Aryeh Qatan*, “little lion.” No ancient Hebrew or rabbinic constellation securely corresponding to Hevelius’s Leo Minor was found. Biblical lion symbolism belongs chiefly to Judah and to zodiacal Leo in later Jewish astrology; transferring it to Leo Minor is modern.

### A useful uniqueness claim

**Substantially correct, with qualification.** Leo Minor is the only surviving constellation named as a “minor” counterpart that was itself introduced in early-modern European astronomy. Ursa Minor and Canis Minor are ancient. This does not mean no other later cartographer ever proposed a “minor” figure—Hevelius himself used Triangulum Minus—but Leo Minor is the only such modern “Minor” constellation retained by the IAU.

## Stars and objects

## The stars forming the figure

There is no IAU-prescribed line pattern. Common modern diagrams use some combination of:

- **46 LMi (Praecipua)**
- **β LMi**
- **21 LMi**
- **10 LMi**
- sometimes **37 LMi**, **30 LMi**, or other Flamsteed stars

**Documented historical catalogue:** Hevelius catalogued **18 entries** under Leo Minor, H 902–H 919. Seven were cross-referenced to stars in the Kepler/Brahe tradition; none was merely copied as a Ptolemaic “Secunda Classis” entry. A modern machine-readable study confirms the count and identifies the old positions against Hipparcos. [Verbunt and van Gent, “The Star Catalogue of Hevelius”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.3841)

Hevelius did not simply discover eighteen previously unseen stars. “New constellation” means that he reorganized known and newly measured stars into a new figure. His measurements generally had an accuracy of about two arcminutes, although occasional errors were much larger.

The conventional claim that only three stars are brighter than magnitude 4.5 depends on rounding and passband: 46 LMi and β LMi are safely brighter; 21 LMi and 10 LMi lie close to the threshold.

### Why there is no Alpha Leonis Minoris

**Documented anomaly; cause partly reconstructed.** Francis Baily’s 1845 British Association catalogue regularized Greek letters for Hevelius’s newer constellations. He assigned **β** to the second-brightest principal star but left the brighter 46 LMi without α. Baily died before the catalogue appeared and could not correct proofs.

The familiar statement “Baily accidentally forgot Alpha” is plausible, but the surviving evidence does not disclose his intent. It is therefore better stated as:

- **Fact:** Leo Minor has β LMi but no α LMi.
- **Fact:** Baily’s catalogue is the source of β.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** α was probably intended for 46 LMi.
- **Unproved explanation:** Baily’s death prevented correction of an omission.

Leo Minor is frequently called the only northern constellation without an alpha star. Norma, Puppis, and Vela also lack a current α designation, but their histories differ: the last two inherited lettering from the dismantled Argo Navis.

### Praecipua: a name transferred through error

This is Leo Minor’s most important nomenclatural controversy.

**Documented fact.** *Praecipua* is Latin, feminine singular, meaning “the principal” or “chief [star].” The IAU now recognizes **Praecipua** for **46 LMi**.

**Disputed historical attribution.** R. H. Allen wrote that Hevelius applied the adjective to the constellation’s lucida and Piazzi turned it into a proper name. Direct inspection reported by later researchers has not found *Praecipua* in Hevelius’s Leo Minor catalogue. The secure printed appearance is in Giuseppe Piazzi’s 1814 Palermo catalogue, where it pertains to **37 LMi**, which Piazzi rated brighter than 46 LMi.

**Documented transmission error.** Allen connected the Piazzi name with the actual brightest star, 46 LMi. Later catalogues repeated that transfer, and the IAU standardized the inherited modern usage rather than restoring Piazzi’s original referent.

Thus:

1. 1814: *Praecipua* is attached to 37 LMi in Piazzi.
2. Late nineteenth century: Allen associates it with 46 LMi.
3. Modern period: usage settles on 46 LMi.
4. IAU Working Group on Star Names: Praecipua becomes the standardized name of 46 LMi.

The often-seen alternative **Praecipula** is a diminutive or corrupt variant, “the little principal one,” not the current IAU spelling.

### Bode’s “o” and the false Omicron

**Documented typographical ambiguity.** Bode marked 46 LMi with a lower-case Roman **o** in his lettering scheme. Later compilers sometimes interpreted it as Greek omicron, producing “ο LMi.” That is not a genuine Bayer designation. The distinction matters because Leo Minor’s sole accepted Greek-letter designation is β LMi.

### Flamsteed numbers and “Bayer” letters

The stars numbered **1–46 Leonis Minoris** are conventionally called Flamsteed designations because they belong to the British catalogue tradition organized by right ascension. Yet there is a bibliographical complication: one online reconstruction of Flamsteed’s version of Hevelius reports that the reprinted catalogue inexplicably omitted Leo Minor’s eighteen Hevelian entries altogether. The later numbered series nonetheless became standard. [Paul Barbier’s reconstruction](https://www.pbarbier.com/hevelius/hevelius.html)

Calling β LMi’s letter “Bayer’s” means a **Bayer-style Greek-letter designation**, not that Johann Bayer printed it in 1603. Bayer’s *Uranometria* predates Leo Minor.

## Variables and eruptive stars

- **R Leonis Minoris** — a Mira variable ranging approximately V 6.3–13.2 over about 372 days, spectral types roughly M6.5e–M9e. It is a major amateur observing target. [R LMi summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_Leonis_Minoris)
- **RZ Leonis Minoris** — a cataclysmic variable with repeated outbursts; ultraviolet excess drew attention in the early 1980s. [RZ LMi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RZ_Leonis_Minoris)
- **SX Leonis Minoris** — SU Ursae Majoris-type dwarf nova/cataclysmic variable, studied in quiescence and superoutburst.
- **VW Leonis Minoris** — an eclipsing/contact system embedded in a hierarchical quadruple system. [VW LMi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VW_Leonis_Minoris)
- **11 LMi / SV LMi** — a nearby multiple star used in photometric work; the variable designation does not imply dramatic naked-eye changes.
- **46 LMi** — suspected low-amplitude variability of only a few hundredths of a magnitude; this remains much less conspicuous than R LMi.

No pre-telescopic guest star, classical nova, or historical naked-eye supernova is securely recorded in this sky area.

## Exoplanet systems

**Documented modern science.** Planet-bearing stars within the modern boundary include:

- **HD 87883**, a K-type star with the giant planet HD 87883 b; its published orbit is long and eccentric. [NASA Exoplanet Catalog](https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanet-catalog/hd-87883-b/), [discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.1596)
- **HD 82886**, another evolved planet-hosting star commonly listed in Leo Minor.

These objects “belong” to Leo Minor only by their apparent direction from Earth; the stars are at unrelated physical distances.

## Deep-sky objects

Leo Minor lies away from the Milky Way and is consequently rich in galaxies but poor in bright Galactic nebulae and open clusters. It has **no Messier object**. A modern catalogue query returns more than one hundred NGC/IC entries in the boundary, mostly faint galaxies. ### Hanny’s Voorwerp

**Documented fact.** In 2007 Dutch schoolteacher Hanny van Arkel noticed an anomalous green object beside IC 2497 while participating in Galaxy Zoo. “Voorwerp” simply means “object” in Dutch. Follow-up showed a large cloud glowing strongly in ionized oxygen. The leading interpretation is that it is gas illuminated by a quasar whose nucleus has greatly faded, allowing astronomers to study rapid changes in active galactic nuclei. [NASA APOD](https://apod.nasa.gov/apod/ap110210.html), [NASA/Hubble data page](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/hubble/compass-and-scale-image-of-hannys-voorwerp/)

Published distance figures vary because some pages confuse the galaxy’s distance with image scale or use different cosmological estimates. Approximately **650 million light-years** is the commonly cited value for IC 2497; a NASA asset page that prints 65 million light-years appears inconsistent with the redshift-based literature and with NASA’s own APOD explanation.

### Supernovae and impostors

- **SN 1998cf**, in NGC 3504 — genuine extragalactic supernova.
- **SN 2001ac**, also associated with NGC 3504 — reported supernova in the same host.
- **SN 2019np**, in NGC 3254 — Type Ia supernova discovered 9 January 2019. [Historical supernova list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_supernovae)
- **SN 2000ch**, in NGC 3432 — **not a terminal supernova in the ordinary sense**. Discovered on 3 May 2000 around unfiltered magnitude 17.4, it underwent further eruptions and is classified as a luminous blue variable or “supernova impostor.” [Discovery study](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0404035), [later-outburst study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.0504)

The last case is a useful warning: an “SN” discovery designation records the initial reporting channel, not an irrevocable physical classification.

## Meteor showers

- **Leonis Minorids (LMI)** — an established shower name in older IAU/IMO usage, with activity in late October and a radiant in Leo Minor. Discovery is attributed to Dick McCloskey and Annette Posen in 1959 through the Harvard Meteor Program.
- **December Leonis Minorids (DLM, shower 32)** — long listed from early December into winter, with weak activity. Modern meteor analyses have questioned whether activity assigned to DLM and the Comae Berenicids is separable; the 2026 IMO calendar recommends consolidating the confused radiant complex under Comae Berenicids.
- **November Leonis Minorids (NLM, 440)** — appears in the IAU Meteor Data Center working list rather than necessarily the established list.

The shower nomenclature is consequently dynamic. “Radiates from Leo Minor” is an apparent-geometrical statement, not evidence that meteoroids originate near its stars. [IAU MDC working list](https://iaumdc.astro.sk/working), [IMO 2026 calendar](https://imo.net/files/meteor-shower/cal2026.pdf)

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the constellation

**Second millennium–first millennium BCE — Babylonian and Egyptian skies.**  
**Negative finding.** No cuneiform constellation identifiable as Leo Minor has been demonstrated. Mesopotamian lion imagery is attached to the zodiacal Lion and royal/solar symbolism, not to Hevelius’s northern patch. No securely identified Egyptian “little lion” occupies this region.

Claims that the stars form an Egyptian scarab or that the area was specially sacred to Ptah appear in nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century star-lore compilations. They depend on speculative readings of the Dendera zodiac, whose Crab is displaced relative to modern maps. They are **antiquarian conjectures**, not an Egyptian attestation of Leo Minor.

**Third century BCE — Aratus, *Phaenomena*.**  
The stars beneath the Great Bear’s feet are described as inconspicuous or unnamed in later paraphrases of Aratus. This is evidence that the region lacked a canonical Greek figure, not that Aratus recognized a small lion.

**Second century CE — Ptolemy, *Almagest* VII–VIII.**  
Ptolemy did not list Leo Minor among his 48 constellations. Some stars in or near the modern area were catalogued as **unformed stars of Leo**, i.e. outside the drawn lion’s body. Other nearby stars belonged to the vicinity of Ursa Major. “Unformed” did not mean unknown; it meant outside the conventional iconographic outline. [Ptolemaic catalogue study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.0628), [Almagest catalogue edition](https://starlinesofalmagest.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Almagest-Peters-Knobel-1.pdf)

**964 — al-Ṣūfī, *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita*.**  
Al-Ṣūfī retained Ptolemy’s 48 figures. He therefore had *al-Asad*, the Lion, but no Lesser Lion. His major contribution for this region was to record indigenous Arabian asterisms in addition to Ptolemaic star positions. [Al-Ṣūfī overview](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_constellations.htm)

**1009–1010 — Oxford, Bodleian MS Marsh 144.**  
One of the oldest surviving illustrated copies of al-Ṣūfī shows the ordinary Ptolemaic Lion. Formed stars are differentiated from exterior ones by ink color and numbering. It cannot show Leo Minor because the figure had not yet been invented. [Bodleian Leo image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Book_of_the_Fixed_Stars_Auv0275_Leo.jpg)

**Thirteenth century — so-called Borgia globe tradition.**  
A medieval Arabic globe later owned by Cardinal Stefano Borgia was read by Christian Ludwig Ideler as placing *al-Ẓibāʾ wa-Awlāduhā*, “the Gazelle and her Young,” in this general region. The identification with precisely the stars of Leo Minor is **Ideler’s reconstruction**, not the name of a bounded Arabic constellation equivalent to Leo Minor.

**1515 — Nuremberg, Dürer–Stabius–Heinfogel northern map.**  
Albrecht Dürer engraved the figures, Conrad Heinfogel plotted the stars, and Johannes Stabius organized the map. It shows the Ptolemaic northern sky and has no Little Lion. Its region is blank or occupied by exterior stars between Leo and Ursa Major. The four corner authorities are Aratus, Ptolemy, Manilius, and al-Ṣūfī. [Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/358366), [BnF](https://expositions.bnf.fr/monde-en-spheres/grand/mes_055.php)

**1603 — Augsburg, Johann Bayer, *Uranometria*.**  
No Leo Minor. Bayer’s atlas preceded Hevelius’s invention, so no Greek letters originated there for the constellation.

**1627 — Augsburg, Julius Schiller, *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*.**  
No Leo Minor. Schiller Christianized Bayer’s existing constellations, replacing northern pagan figures with New Testament subjects. Since the Little Lion did not yet exist as a figure, asking which saint “replaced Leo Minor” has no literal answer. Its stars remained distributed across the plates replacing Leo and Ursa Major or as unformed background stars. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/), [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720)

### Hevelius’s invention

**Before 1687 — manuscript and observational phase.**  
Hevelius measured the sky at Danzig using large naked-eye instruments. Edmond Halley visited in 1679 and tested his observing methods. The constellation emerged from Hevelius’s attempt to organize stars between the traditional figures.

**1687 — printed date.**  
The atlas and catalogue bear 1687 dates in portions prepared before Hevelius died on 28 January 1687.

**1690 — Gdańsk/Danzig, publication.**  
Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius completed and published the three-part project:

1. *Prodromus Astronomiae*
2. *Catalogus Stellarum Fixarum*
3. *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia*

The catalogue contains eighteen Leo Minor entries. The atlas plate depicts a crouching or reclining young lion between Ursa Major and Leo. [University of Tartu copy](https://dspace.ut.ee/items/e0e0d294-bf84-4218-8f7c-0188ed3ff4c0), [Hevelius plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Johannes_Hevelius_-_Prodromus_Astronomia_-_Volume_III_%22Firmamentum_Sobiescianum%2C_sive_uranographia%22_-_Tavola_Z_-_Leo_minor.jpg)

### What did Hevelius intend?

**Documented explanation, but not a myth.** He placed the Little Lion between the Great Bear and the larger Lion because the new animal was, in the wording transmitted by later authors, “of the same nature” as its powerful animal neighbours. It was not created to honor a king, patron, voyage, instrument, or classical hero.

The phrase sometimes implies a celestial menagerie rather than zoological taxonomy: the faint patch lay between two famous beasts, so another beast provided iconographic continuity. Leo Minor has no individual name, deed, or story in Hevelius.

A modern study adds an important complication: Hevelius’s figure may partly reorganize material from Petrus Plancius’s river constellations, Jordan and Tigris/Euphrates, rather than springing wholly from untouched sky. The study says Hevelius divided portions of those Plancian inventions among Canes Venatici, Leo Minor, Lynx, and Vulpecula. This is a **scholarly cartographic genealogy**, not a claim that Plancius had already invented Leo Minor. [Verbunt and van Gent](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.3841)

### Eighteenth-century reception

**1729 — London, Flamsteed, *Atlas Coelestis*.**  
Leo Minor was received into the post-Hevelian atlas tradition, although the accompanying catalogue history is untidy. A reconstructed comparison reports that Flamsteed’s reprint of Hevelius inexplicably omitted the eighteen Leo Minor entries even while the constellation survived cartographically. Later Flamsteed-number usage nevertheless ran to 46 LMi.

**Mid-eighteenth century — Lacaille.**  
Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille’s work was primarily decisive for southern constellations. He neither invented nor abolished Leo Minor. Popular templates that mechanically list Lacaille among all modern-constellation inventors can obscure this.

**1782 — Berlin, Bode, *Vorstellung der Gestirne*.**  
Bode included the Hevelian figure in his revised and enlarged presentation of the Flamsteed/Fortin sky.

### Bode’s 1801 Uranographia

**Documented fact.** Bode’s monumental *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* was published in Berlin in 1801 at his own press/expense, with financial support acknowledged to Friedrich von Hahn. It comprises twenty large copperplate charts and a separate German–French descriptive and catalogue volume. [ETH-Bibliothek facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [Bode catalogue volume](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

Leo Minor appears with Ursa Major on the plate usually numbered **VI** in modern plate sequences, although reproductions sometimes call it plate 6 or 7 depending on whether front matter and planispheres are counted. The engraved title is **LEO MINOR**; Bode’s running vernacular is German *der kleine Löwe*, paralleled by French *le Petit Lion*.

Bode shows the lion immediately beneath the Great Bear, head toward the west/right or left depending on whether one reads the chart as external globe view or sky view. The figure is far more densely surrounded by plotted stars than in Hevelius because Bode attempted to include objects down to about eighth magnitude and incorporated double stars, nebulae, clusters, and discoveries from Lacaille, Lalande, Messier, Herschel, and others.

The companion volume announces **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters**, not 830. It includes descriptions in German and French, coordinates, and acknowledgments of contradictions among source catalogues. Bode’s work contains more than one hundred constellation figures, including many later rejected ones. [Bode plate record](https://www.geographicus.com/P/AntiqueMap/ursamajorleominor-bode-1801-2), [Rumsey index](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~283904~90056539%3AIndex--Index-Tabularum-et-Astrorum-)

**Finding on “830 stars in 109 groups/on chain.”** No credible 1801 bibliographical or astronomical source consulted supports “830 stars in 109 groups” as a description of Bode’s *Uranographia*. It conflicts with the work’s title volume and catalogue total of 17,240. It may describe a modern digital collection, tokenized image series, metadata subset, or a different star-map project. Without identification of the “Bode collection on chain,” the claim remains **unverified**.

### Nineteenth-century standardization

**1814 — Palermo, Piazzi.**  
Piazzi’s second catalogue uses *Praecipua* for what is now identified as 37 LMi. [INAF catalogue record](https://openaccess.inaf.it/server/api/core/bitstreams/8b4053ca-4fce-4a56-9ab1-8efcb531de38/content)

**1822 — London, Alexander Jamieson, *A Celestial Atlas*.**  
Jamieson depicted Leo Minor with freer artistic handling than Bode or Fortin. His atlas was the immediate visual source for *Urania’s Mirror*. [Jamieson overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Celestial_Atlas)

**1824–1825 — London, *Urania’s Mirror*.**  
Plate 20, engraved by Sidney Hall, shows “Leo Major and Leo Minor” as two naturalistic lions. The card’s pierced stars shine when held to light. An advertisement appeared in 1825; the English set itself was issued in November 1824, while surviving cards are often catalogued as 1825. Attribution to “a lady” was promotional concealment; Peter Hingley later argued that the author was probably Rev. Richard Rouse Bloxam. [Smithsonian](https://www.si.edu/object/uranias-mirror%3Anmah_1460911), [Powerhouse Museum](https://collection.powerhouse.com.au/object/192015), [plate image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sidney_Hall_-_Urania%27s_Mirror_-_Leo_Major_and_Leo_Minor.jpg)

**1843 — Bonn, Argelander, *Uranometria Nova*.**  
Argelander assigned 21 visible stars to Leo Minor. He retained the constellation while simplifying pictorial astronomy into observational charting.

**1845 — London, Baily catalogue.**  
Baily’s β-without-α anomaly entered standard nomenclature.

**1870 — Richard A. Proctor.**  
Proctor renamed the constellation **Leaena**, “Lioness,” in a program of shortening constellation names for charts. This was an idiosyncratic reform and did not displace Leo Minor.

**1872 — Cologne, Eduard Heis, *Atlas Coelestis Novus*.**  
Heis retained Leo Minor and plotted about forty of its naked-eye stars, within an atlas containing 5,421 stars. Its practical observational style helped stabilize the northern constellation inventory later used by the IAU. [Heis historical description](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/dd/An_introduction_to_the_study_of_variable_stars_%28IA_introductiontost00furnrich%29.pdf)

### IAU settlement

**1922 — Rome.**  
The IAU adopted the standard list of 88 constellation names and three-letter abbreviations. Leo Minor was retained as **LMi**. Henry Norris Russell’s published list disseminated the system.

**1925 — Cambridge.**  
Delporte presented a method for exact boundaries along celestial coordinate lines.

**1928 — Leiden.**  
The IAU approved Delporte’s boundaries.

**1930 — publication.**  
*Délimitation scientifique des constellations* fixed Leo Minor’s legal astronomical territory. A requirement to preserve existing variable-star designations influenced some awkward boundary shapes. [IAU history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

## Myths and legends

## Classical Greek and Roman material

**Negative finding, documented by chronology.** Leo Minor was invented some fifteen centuries after Ptolemy. Therefore:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* and its Epitome contain no Leo Minor.
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* and *Fabulae* contain no Leo Minor.
- Aratus and the scholia to Aratus contain no little-lion catasterism.
- Germanicus’ *Aratea* contains none.
- Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, and Pausanias never identify this group as a separate lion.
- No classical author says that a god placed Leo Minor in the sky.

Consequently there are no competing classical identities, no ancient account of who killed or rescued it, and no classical “every myth in which the figure acts.” The person or animal represented by Leo Minor has no identity beyond “a smaller lion.”

### Retrofits from the myths of Leo

Popular websites sometimes attach the **Nemean Lion** to Leo Minor. That is a **modern confusion** with zodiacal Leo.

The ancient Nemean Lion was killed by Heracles as his first labour; Hera or Zeus was later said to place the lion among the stars. Sources and variants belong to **Leo**, not Leo Minor: Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 2.5.1; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 30; Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* 2.24; Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 12. None names a second or juvenile lion.

Likewise, myths involving Cybele’s lions, the lion of Thisbe, Atalanta and Hippomenes transformed into lions, or Dionysiac lions in Nonnus have no ancient astronomical connection to Leo Minor.

### Hevelius’s “same nature” statement

This is sometimes inflated into a tale that the Little Lion is Leo’s cub, companion, offspring, or guardian.

- **Fact:** Hevelius chose a smaller lion beside Leo and Ursa Major.
- **Reasonable iconographic inference:** proximity to the larger lion invited a parent–cub reading.
- **No early text found:** Hevelius did not give the animal parents, exploits, a death, or a catasterism.
- **Modern invention:** elaborate stories in which Zeus, Hera, or Heracles places a lion cub in the sky.

### Egyptian hoofprints claim

A current popular page states that Egyptians saw these stars as “hoof prints left by gazelles fleeing from Leo.” This is **misattributed**. Gazelle leaps belong to indigenous Arabic lore associated with Ursa Major’s feet and adjacent stars. The Egyptian claim could not be traced to an Egyptian text or securely interpreted monument and appears to be a modern conflation. [Example of the modern claim](https://www.underthenightsky.com/constellations/leo-minor/)

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Indigenous Arabian sky

This is the strongest pre-Hevelian cultural material.

The old Arabian complex is spatial rather than congruent with the IAU polygon:

- **Qafazāt al-ẓibāʾ**, “Leaps of the Gazelles”: three pairs of stars beneath Ursa Major’s feet.
- **al-Ẓibāʾ**, “the Gazelles”: a longer line below the leaps.
- **Awlād al-ẓibāʾ**, “the young of the gazelles”: small/faint stars between the gazelles and their leaps.
- **al-Ḥawḍ**, “the Pool” or “Watering Trough”: a curved group near the third leap.

Ibn Qutayba’s ninth-century *Kitāb al-Anwāʾ* describes small stars between the gazelles and the leaps and a lengthwise row below them. Al-Marzūqī, in the eleventh century, preserves related descriptions. Al-Ṣūfī explains that the gazelles fled or leapt away from the rough hair of the Lion’s tail toward the watering trough.

The best-known three leap-pairs are now identified mostly in Ursa Major:

1. ν and ξ UMa — first leap; later star names Alula Borealis and Alula Australis preserve *al-ūlā*, “the first.”
2. λ and μ UMa — second leap; Tania Borealis/Australis preserve *al-thāniya*, “the second.”
3. ι and κ UMa — third leap; Talitha derives from *al-thālitha*, “the third.”

The extended gazelle herd and young reach toward modern Lynx and Leo Minor. Christian Ludwig Ideler’s nineteenth-century equation of the Leo Minor stars with **al-Ẓibāʾ wa-Awlāduhā** is therefore suggestive but too tidy. The Arabic figures cross modern boundaries and changed between authorities. [All Skies Encyclopaedia](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Ziba)

## Chinese astronomy

The modern Leo Minor polygon cuts across older Chinese systems rather than matching one figure. Relevant asterisms include:

- **Nèipíng (內平), “Inner Judge” or “Inner Equality”** — four stars associated with administration or adjudication within the Supreme Palace Enclosure; 22 LMi is commonly identified as Nèipíngyī, its first star.
- **Shǎowēi (少微), “Junior Officers”** — a four-star group spanning modern Leo and Leo Minor, representing lesser dignitaries or scholars.
- Parts of **Xuānyuán (軒轅)**, the Yellow Emperor/imperial lineage or chariot complex, extend through Leo and Lynx near the region.
- Some popular Western summaries call a Leo Minor group “Nuy Ping” or “Seaou Wei”; these are obsolete Wade–Giles-style or corrupted romanizations of Nèipíng and Shǎowēi.

The statement that Chinese astronomers combined Leo Major and Leo Minor into a giant “Dragon mounting to heaven” derives from nineteenth-century European summaries and should not replace the specific asterism evidence. Chinese uranography did not recognize the two European lions as native units. [Historical mapping summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Minor_in_Chinese_astronomy), [study mentioning Shaowei](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstreams/50b1c7e5-1922-48c8-ae5f-734f669024a0/download)

## Indian astronomy and lunar mansions

**Negative finding.** The 27/28 Indian nakṣatras follow the ecliptic. Leo Minor lies substantially north of it, so no primary nakṣatra is constituted from its stars. Maghā, Pūrva Phalgunī, and Uttara Phalgunī concern zodiacal Leo/Virgo, not Leo Minor.

Modern Indian-language star charts translate the Western constellation name, but that is adoption of the IAU system.

## Hebrew, Jewish, and Kabbalistic mappings

No early Hebrew constellation corresponding to Leo Minor was found. Later Jewish astrology commonly maps the zodiacal Lion to **Aryeh** and sometimes to Judah, based on Genesis 49:9, but that concerns Leo.

Kabbalistic diagrams that map letters, tribes, angels, or *Sefer Yetzirah* correspondences onto Leo Minor are **modern extensions** unless they cite a post-1690 source. Leo Minor cannot be original to ancient or early medieval Kabbalah under that identity.

## Christianized sky

Schiller’s 1627 atlas predates Leo Minor, so no saint replaced it. In Schiller’s scheme, zodiacal Leo was replaced by the apostle **Thomas** in commonly reproduced tables, while Ursa Major became **St Peter’s boat**. The stars later assigned to Leo Minor would consequently lie near or between those Christianized subjects rather than inside a dedicated saint.

Any statement such as “Schiller made Leo Minor Saint X” is chronologically impossible.

## Astrological, Behenian, alchemical, and Hermetic material

**Ptolemy’s fixed-star astrology.** *Tetrabiblos* I.9 assigns planetary natures to the stars of the ancient figures. Since Leo Minor did not exist, Ptolemy gives it no nature. Applying Leo’s solar or Mars/Jupiter qualities to Leo Minor is later analogy.

**Behenian fixed stars.** The canonical medieval list of fifteen Behenian stars contains none in Leo Minor. Praecipua is not a Behenian star.

**Lunar mansions.** Neither the Arabic nor Indian mansion systems are defined by Leo Minor as a unit. The indigenous Arabian gazelle material appears in discussions surrounding the fourteenth Arabic mansion, but that does not make Leo Minor itself a lunar mansion.

**Alchemy and Hermetism.** Searches found generic modern lion symbolism—gold, sulfur, solar force, royal power—and online occult systems assigning such meanings to Leo Minor. No early-modern alchemical or Hermetic text was found that singles out Hevelius’s constellation. These readings should be labelled **modern invention or unverified esotericism**, not traditional Hermetic doctrine.

**Modern natal astrology.** Because the constellation is north of the ecliptic and not a zodiac sign, mainstream tropical and sidereal astrology assigns it no rulership. “Leo Minor personality” pages are recent symbolic inventions.

## Boundary history

### Ptolemy

There was no bounded Leo Minor. Stars here were exterior or “unformed” stars of Leo or lay near Ursa Major. Constellations were catalogued figures, not exhaustive legal territories.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer preserved the older figures. The space remained between Leo and Ursa Major; no stars carried “Leonis Minoris” Bayer letters.

### Hevelius, 1687–1690

Hevelius converted the intervening region into a recognizable lion and catalogued eighteen stars under it. This effectively removed stars from the miscellaneous exterior-star categories of Leo and from areas associated with the Bear’s feet. A modern cartographic study further connects part of the territory to abandoned Plancian river figures.

### Flamsteed and eighteenth-century atlases

Leo Minor was generally accepted, but its catalogue population and visual edges varied. Flamsteed numbers extended the constellation’s star inventory. No exact universally binding border existed.

### Lacaille

Lacaille made no decisive northern boundary change here.

### Bode, 1782 and 1801

Bode preserved Hevelius’s lion and plotted many more faint stars. His Uranographia is significant because it drew boundary-like divisions between figures, but these were still cartographic conventions, not internationally legal limits.

### Argelander and Heis

Argelander counted 21 component stars; Heis approximately 40. These differing totals show that “which stars belong to the figure” remained catalogue-dependent.

### Delporte, 1930

The modern polygon made every point of sky belong to one and only one constellation. Leo Minor received territory bordered by:

- Ursa Major to the north
- Lynx to the west
- Leo to the south
- Cancer at a southwest corner

Post-1930, stars are assigned by coordinates, not by whether an artist draws them inside the lion’s body. The boundary preserved established variable-star constellation suffixes where possible.

## Iconography

### Antiquity

**Farnese Atlas, Roman copy of a Hellenistic prototype, second century CE.** No Leo Minor. It depicts the classical globe of 48-constellation tradition.

**Dendera zodiac, Ptolemaic/Roman Egypt.** No demonstrable Leo Minor. Attempts to find a scarab or little lion here are retrospective interpretations.

### Islamic manuscripts

**al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, from 1009–1010 onward.** No Little Lion. The relevant visual evidence consists of the Lion, Great Bear, and their exterior stars. Al-Ṣūfī’s standard paired images show the view from outside a globe and the reversed view from inside the celestial sphere. [Manuscript census](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_manuscripts.htm)

### Renaissance Europe

**Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515.** Blank/unformed region; no lion.

**Bayer, Augsburg, 1603.** No Leo Minor.

**Schiller, Augsburg, 1627.** No separate Christian replacement.

### Hevelius

**Gdańsk, 1690.** The first surviving canonical image: a small naturalistic lion, crouched between the much larger Leo and Ursa Major. Like figures on celestial globes, Hevelius’s constellation plates are often viewed from outside the celestial sphere and therefore reverse the apparent sky orientation.

The *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* frontispiece, engraved by Charles de La Haye after Andrzej Stech, shows Hevelius presenting his astronomical creations to Urania and the canonical astronomers. The surviving description emphasizes Sextans and Scutum and the procession of constellation animals; Leo Minor participates in this broader self-presentation of Hevelius’s new sky. [Wilanów Palace Museum](https://wilanow-palac.pl/sobiesciana/karta-przedtytulowa-firmamentum-sobiescianum-sive-uranographia-sobiesciana)

### Bode

**Berlin, 1801.** Bode’s lion is anatomically naturalistic, embedded in an exceptionally dense scientific chart. The figure lies under Ursa Major, accompanied by coordinate grids, magnitude-coded stars, double stars, nebulae, and constellation divisions. It is a culmination of the artistic atlas tradition rather than the origin of the figure.

### Popular nineteenth-century imagery

- **Jamieson, London, 1822:** naturalistic lion, compositionally freer than Bode.
- **Urania’s Mirror, London, 1824–1825:** two hand-colored lions on plate 20, with holes punched through the star positions.
- **Burritt, United States, nineteenth century:** educational atlases describe the Little Lion as formed from the *stellæ informes* between Leo and Ursa Major; some editions give it 53 stars and label its chief star with a Roman *l*.
- **Argelander, Bonn, 1843:** observational rather than mythographic depiction.
- **Heis, Cologne, 1872:** still more stars and less emphasis on elaborate figure art.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

**Negative finding.** No ancient coin, cathedral portal, medieval ceiling, or manuscript calendar can securely depict Leo Minor as such, because it did not yet exist. Lions in those media may represent Leo, Christ, Mark the Evangelist, Judah, royalty, heraldry, or generic lion symbolism.

Post-1690 celestial ceilings and calendars could include Leo Minor if copied from Hevelius or Bode, but no specific monumental example was verified strongly enough for attribution here. Claims based solely on the presence of “a small lion” are iconographically unsafe.

## People, events, and works

### Johannes and Elisabeth Hevelius

Johannes created and measured the constellation; Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius helped with observations and brought the catalogue and atlas through publication after his death. The exact division of observational labor is not fully documented, but modern scholarship regards her contribution as significant rather than merely editorial.

### Francis Baily

Baily’s catalogue produced Leo Minor’s famous missing-alpha problem. The anomaly is now part of the constellation’s identity.

### Giuseppe Piazzi and R. H. Allen

Piazzi created the secure early printed *Praecipua* label for 37 LMi; Allen helped transfer it to 46 LMi. This makes the current proper name a product of both observation and bibliographical error.

### William Herschel

Herschel discovered or catalogued many of Leo Minor’s brighter galaxies, including NGC 2859, NGC 3344, NGC 3432, NGC 3486, and NGC 3504 under his own class-and-number system. Bode’s atlas incorporated Herschelian nebular discoveries into its dense plotting.

### Hanny van Arkel

Her 2007 Galaxy Zoo discovery made Leo Minor the location of one of citizen science’s best-known objects. The event is culturally more significant for the constellation than any purported ancient myth.

### Exploration and navigation

**Negative finding.** No major voyage or traditional navigation system is known to have relied specifically on Leo Minor. Its stars are faint and it lacks a pole star or first-magnitude marker. Arab navigators used the neighbouring Ursa Major leap-pairs and other bright stars, but this should not be rewritten as navigation “by Leo Minor.”

### Literary and artistic works

No canonical classical poem can name Leo Minor. Aratus-derived lines about unnamed stars beneath the Bear concern the region before Hevelius, not the later lion.

Nineteenth-century astronomical poetry and educational prose occasionally personify “the Smaller Lion,” as in William Tyler Olcott’s *Star Lore of All Ages* (1911), but these are literary embellishments derived from the atlas tradition. [Olcott text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Star_Lore_Of_All_Ages/The_Minor_Constellations/Leo_Minor)

No major painting, symphony, historic ship, or space mission demonstrably named for the constellation was found. A University of Arizona telescope is currently called **Leo Minor**, but that is a modern instrument name rather than a historical cultural tradition. [Space4 instrumentation](https://s4.arizona.edu/labs-instrumentation)

### Leo Minor I

A recently reported ultra-faint Milky Way satellite candidate is called **Leo Minor I** because it lies in the constellation. The discovery literature classifies it as consistent with an ultra-faint dwarf galaxy. It is unrelated to the better-known Leo I and Leo II dwarf galaxies, which lie in Leo. [DELVE discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12422)

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was Leo Minor first presented in 1687 or 1690?

**Resolution:** both dates describe different bibliographical facts. Portions of the work and title pages were printed or dated 1687; the completed *Prodromus*, catalogue, and atlas were published posthumously in 1690. “Invented in 1687” and “introduced in 1690” are both defensible if qualified.

### 2. Did Hevelius call 46 LMi Praecipua?

**Disputed; primary-text evidence weighs against the usual story.** Allen says yes, but modern checks report no such entry in Hevelius. Piazzi 1814 is the secure source, and his name belongs to 37 LMi. The IAU-approved application to 46 LMi represents later customary usage.

### 3. Why is there no Alpha?

**Fact:** none exists.  
**Likely reconstruction:** Baily omitted it by mistake.  
**Unproved embellishment:** that he certainly intended α for 46 LMi and would have corrected it had he lived.

### 4. Is Bode’s “o” omicron?

No. It is a lower-case Roman letter in Bode’s scheme. Treating it as ο LMi creates a false Bayer designation.

### 5. Did Hevelius invent the stars as well as the figure?

No. Many were already catalogued or visible; the innovation was their grouping and pictorial identity. Seven of his eighteen Leo Minor entries correspond to material in the Kepler/Brahe catalogue tradition.

### 6. Was the Arabic equivalent “the Gazelle and her Young”?

**Partly, but not exactly.** Ideler’s equation is a historical reconstruction. Indigenous Arabic gazelle figures extend across Leo Minor, Lynx, and Ursa Major and do not respect the later European outline.

### 7. Were the “gazelle’s leaps” inside Leo Minor?

The famous three paired leaps are principally the paired feet of modern Ursa Major. Associated gazelles, young, and watering-place stars extend southward. Saying simply “Leo Minor was the Gazelle’s Leaps” is misleading.

### 8. Is there an Egyptian scarab or hoofprint constellation here?

No securely documented Egyptian equivalent was found. The scarab reading of Dendera and “Egyptian hoofprints” story are antiquarian speculation or modern conflation with Arabic lore.

### 9. Does Leo Minor have a classical myth?

No. The Nemean Lion belongs to Leo. Stories of Leo Minor as its cub are modern inventions.

### 10. Did Flamsteed omit or accept Leo Minor?

The visual atlas tradition accepted the figure, but a reconstruction of the catalogue transmission reports that Flamsteed’s reprint omitted Hevelius’s eighteen Leo Minor records. Later Flamsteed-number designations nevertheless became standard. “Flamsteed accepted it” and “Flamsteed omitted it” can therefore refer to different components of his work.

### 11. Did the IAU adopt the boundaries in 1930?

The name and abbreviation were standardized in 1922; Delporte’s system was proposed in 1925, approved in 1928, and published in 1930. “Adopted in 1930” is useful shorthand but compresses three separate decisions.

### 12. Is SN 2000ch a supernova?

Probably not in the terminal-explosion sense. Recurrent outbursts identify it as a luminous blue variable/supernova impostor. Its designation survives from the initial classification process.

### 13. Are the December Leonis Minorids a secure independent shower?

Currently disputed. The name remains in databases and older calendars, but recent IMO analysis recommends merging confused activity in this radiant region with the Comae Berenicids.

### 14. Is Leo Minor “too faint to deserve existence”?

Patrick Moore’s remark that it has “dubious claims to a separate identity” is opinion, not an IAU controversy. Constellation retention reflects historical standardization, not a brightness threshold.

### 15. Commercially named stars

Private star registries sell names in Leo Minor, sometimes describing them as official or “verified.” The coordinates may be real, but the purchased personal name has no IAU standing. Such services do not alter astronomical nomenclature. The IAU recognizes standardized proper names, catalogue identifiers, and scientific naming procedures—not private dedications.

### 16. “830 stars in 109 groups”

Unverified. Bode’s documented catalogue says 17,240 objects and his atlas shows more than one hundred constellations. The proposed figures may belong to a contemporary digital or blockchain collection rather than the 1801 book.

## Open questions

1. **Hevelius’s exact verbal rationale:** Later writers quote “of the same nature” as Leo and Ursa Major, but a complete critical transcription of the relevant Latin passage, keyed to page and copy, would settle precisely whether this is Hevelius’s wording or a compressed later paraphrase.

2. **The eighteen Hevelian stars:** Modern Hipparcos cross-identifications exist in the machine-readable catalogue, but a publication-quality table aligning H 902–919 with Hevelius’s anatomical descriptions, Flamsteed numbers, HIP identifiers, and current IAU boundaries would be valuable.

3. **Praecipua’s transfer:** Piazzi-to-Allen is clear in outline, but the exact first publication that reassigned the name from 37 to 46 LMi deserves a bibliographical audit across Smyth, Burritt, Allen, and early twentieth-century catalogues.

4. **Flamsteed’s omission:** Why Leo Minor and Corvus disappeared from the particular reprint form of Hevelius used in *Historia Coelestis Britannica* remains unexplained.

5. **The Borgia globe:** Ideler’s gazelle identification should be rechecked against modern photographs, inscriptions, and a current catalogue of the globe rather than inherited through Allen.

6. **Chinese star identifications:** Nèipíng and Shǎowēi mappings vary slightly among Sun–Kistemaker, Schlegel, modern planetaria, and Chinese-language lists. Proper motions, textual star counts, and chart traditions should be considered separately.

7. **Bode’s plate numbering:** Repositories number the Ursa Major/Leo Minor sheet differently according to whether they count title, index, and planisphere leaves. Citation by engraved sheet title is safer than an unqualified plate number.

8. **“Bode collection on chain”:** No identifiable scholarly collection matching “830 stars in 109 groups” was located. A project title, contract address, or collection URL would permit a separate provenance audit.

9. **Material iconography after 1690:** Specific painted ceilings, decorative globes, and architectural programs copied from Hevelius or Bode remain to be catalogued object by object. A small lion alone is insufficient identification.
