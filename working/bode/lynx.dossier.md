# Lynx: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **Kept by the International Astronomical Union.** Lynx is one of the 88 official constellations. Its standard abbreviation is **Lyn**, its Latin genitive is **Lyncis**, and its present territory is the 20-segment polygon standardized from Eugène Delporte’s work and published in 1930. Its stars therefore did not “go” to another constellation, although Delporte’s boundaries left several historically numbered stars on the opposite side of a modern border—notably **10 Ursae Majoris in Lynx**, while **41 Lyncis is in Ursa Major**.

## Basic identification

### Identity and status

- **IAU Latin nominative:** *Lynx*
- **Latin genitive:** *Lyncis*
- **IAU abbreviation:** **Lyn**
- **English:** Lynx
- **German:** *Luchs*; Bode’s chart label itself is the Latin **LYNX**
- **Spanish:** *Lince*
- **French:** *Lynx*
- **Italian:** *Lince*
- **Polish:** *Ryś*
- **Modern Arabic:** الوشق, *al-wašq*, “the lynx,” when translating the Western constellation
- **Modern Hebrew:** שונר, *šunar*, “lynx”
- **Modern Persian:** سیاه‌گوش, *siyāh-gūš*, literally “black-ear,” the ordinary Persian animal-name
- **Modern Chinese:** 天貓座, *Tiānmāo zuò*, “Celestial Cat constellation”

These Arabic, Hebrew, Persian, and Chinese expressions are modern translations or adaptations of the European constellation, not evidence that those cultures had an indigenous Lynx figure in precisely the same stars.

**Documented fact.** The IAU recognizes Lynx, *Lyncis*, and **Lyn**. The three-letter abbreviation belongs to the 1922 standardization; Delporte’s coordinate boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* in 1930. They were originally specified for the mean equator and equinox B1875.0. The IAU’s modern downloadable coordinates are precessed equivalents. [IAU constellation table](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), [IAU Style Manual](https://iauarchive.eso.org/static/publications/stylemanual1989.pdf), [IAU Lynx chart](https://iauarchive.eso.org/static/public/constellations/pdf/LYN.pdf).

### Meaning and etymology

Latin *lynx, lyncis* comes from Greek **λύγξ, λυγκός**, the wild cat. The ultimate pre-Greek or Indo-European history of the word is uncertain. The constellation’s genitive follows the Latin third declension: *Lyncis*, “of the Lynx.”

**Documented fact.** Hevelius’s own explanation is a play on the animal’s proverbial eyesight. His Latin says that an observer examining these fifth- and sixth-magnitude stars must not be shortsighted but possess strong vision; therefore he established the Lynx because one must be a **Lynceus** or have **lynx-like eyes**:

> *Proinde Lyncem ibidem constituit, quod nemo non necessario sit Lynceus, vel oculos habeat Lynceos.*

A restrained translation is: “He therefore established the Lynx there, because anyone [who would inspect it] must necessarily be a Lynceus, or have lynx-like eyes.” [Hevelius, *Prodromus Astronomiae*, p. 115](https://www.e-rara.ch/download/pdf/207915.pdf).

The construction deliberately exploits the overlap between *lynx/Lynx* and *Lynceus*. It does **not**, by itself, state that the constellation represents the Argonaut Lynceus.

### Location and boundaries

Lynx is an extensive but inconspicuous northern constellation between:

- **Camelopardalis** to the north;
- **Auriga** to the west;
- **Gemini** to the southwest;
- **Cancer** to the south;
- **Leo** to the southeast/east;
- **Ursa Major** to the northeast.

It covers approximately **545 square degrees**, making it the 28th-largest modern constellation. The field lies largely away from the bright central band of the Milky Way. This, rather than exceptional physical emptiness, explains its sparse naked-eye appearance.

The modern figure is usually drawn as a long, irregular chain running from **α Lyncis** northward through 38, 31, 21, 15, 12, and 2 Lyncis. This stick figure is a modern observing convention, not a standardized IAU image.

### What stars “make” the figure?

There are three different answers:

1. **Hevelius’s constellation:** nineteen fifth- and sixth-magnitude *stellulae* in the gap between Ursa Major and Auriga.
2. **The pictured animal:** selected stars positioned upon the body of Hevelius’s elongated lynx; later artists altered the anatomy and selection.
3. **Modern Lynx:** every celestial object inside Delporte’s boundary, whether or not connected by a line or included by Hevelius.

The IAU standardizes territories and names, **not constellation stick figures**.

## Stars and objects

### Principal stars

| Star | Visual magnitude, approximately | Historical designation and notes |
|---|---:|---|
| **α Lyncis** | 3.14 | Brightest star; Flamsteed **40 Lyncis**; the only generally used Greek-letter designation in Lynx; an orange K giant |
| **38 Lyncis** | 3.8 | Attractive visual double; sometimes given the unapproved modern name “Maculosa” |
| **10 Ursae Majoris** | 3.96 | F-type binary physically inside modern Lynx despite its Flamsteed name |
| **31 Lyncis** | 4.25 | **Alsciaukat**, also BN Lyncis; orange semiregular variable |
| **15 Lyncis** | 4.3–4.4 | Close multiple/double system |
| **2 Lyncis** | 4.4 | UZ Lyncis; eclipsing and δ-Scuti-type variability is reported |
| **21 Lyncis** | 4.6 | White A-type star; suspected variability in some catalogues |
| **27 Lyncis** | 4.8 | A-type star |
| **12 Lyncis** | 4.9 | Well-known multiple star |
| **16 Lyncis** | 4.9 | A-type star |

**Documented fact.** Johann Bayer could not originally assign Bayer letters to Lynx in 1603 because Hevelius had not yet invented it. The letter **α** was added much later, conventionally attributed to Francis Baily’s nineteenth-century rationalization of stellar nomenclature. Consequently Lynx’s brightest stars are mostly known by Flamsteed numbers rather than Greek letters.

### Proper names

#### Alsciaukat — 31 Lyncis

**Documented tradition; officially standardized in 2017.** The IAU Working Group on Star Names recognizes **Alsciaukat** for 31 Lyncis. It derives from Arabic **الشوكة**, *al-šawka/al-shawka*, “the thorn.” The form passed through inconsistent European transliterations, producing spellings such as *Alsciaukat*.

The star was also called **Mabsuthat**, from Arabic **المبسوطة**, *al-mabsūṭa*, “the outstretched [paw].” These Arabic names belong to the traditional Arabic conception of the feet or paws of the Great Bear and its surrounding stars—not to an Arabic lynx constellation. The choice of Alsciaukat as the official name does not validate “Mabsuthat” as a second IAU proper name. [IAU WGSN bulletin](https://www.iau.org/static/science/scientific_bodies/working_groups/280/WGSN_bulletin2.pdf).

#### “Elvashak” — α Lyncis

**Disputed/unofficial.** “Elvashak” circulates on modern star-name websites but is not an IAU-approved name. It appears to be a distorted transliteration or misapplication of Arabic lexical material associated with *al-shawka*. No secure medieval Arabic text consulted here establishes “Elvashak” as an old name of α Lyncis. It should not be presented as equivalent in standing to Alsciaukat.

#### “Maculosa” — 38 Lyncis

**Modern invention/unverified tradition.** “Maculosa,” Latin for “spotted” or “mottled,” appears in popular modern lists. It is absent from the IAU’s approved names and lacks a demonstrated continuous historical pedigree. It may have been coined as an adjective suitable for a spotted cat and retrofitted to the star.

### Flamsteed-number anomalies

Flamsteed numbers were assigned under pre-Delporte constellation concepts and normally increased with right ascension. Exact modern boundaries did not yet exist. Thus:

- **10 Ursae Majoris** is now in Lynx.
- **41 Lyncis** lies in modern Ursa Major.
- Other Flamsteed designations near the Auriga–Lynx–Ursa Major margins likewise retain their historical names even when a modern line places them elsewhere.

This is not an error in present catalogues: designations are identifiers, not guarantees of current territorial residence.

### Multiple and variable stars

Lynx has long been prized more for double stars than for bright single stars.

- **38 Lyncis:** a readily divisible pair in moderate apertures; nineteenth-century observers variously described the component colors as white, blue-white, lilac, or greenish. Color descriptions are subjective traditions.
- **12 Lyncis:** a close hierarchical multiple.
- **15 Lyncis:** a close double or multiple whose principal components are yellowish-white.
- **19 Lyncis:** multiple-star system.
- **31 Lyncis / BN Lyncis:** semiregular orange giant, varying only by a few hundredths of a magnitude.
- **2 Lyncis / UZ Lyncis:** variable in a multiple or binary system.
- Numerous catalogued eclipsing binaries—including RV, AA, AH, CD, CF, DR, EK, and FS Lyncis—have been studied photometrically.

No naked-eye historical nova comparable to those of Tycho or Kepler is securely recorded in the present Lynx territory. Claims that “a famous ancient nova occurred in Lynx” could not be traced to a positional study or primary chronicle.

### Deep-sky objects

Lynx contains **no Messier object**. This absence is partly historical: Charles Messier’s list was not a systematic survey of every northern constellation.

#### NGC 2419 — the Intergalactic Wanderer

- Type: globular cluster
- Herschel designation: H I.218
- Discovery: **William Herschel, 31 December 1788**
- NGC number: 2419
- Caldwell number: **Caldwell 25**
- Distance: roughly 275,000–300,000 light-years from Earth in modern estimates

**Documented fact.** Herschel discovered it before Bode’s 1801 atlas. It is one of the Milky Way’s most remote luminous globular clusters.

**Historical nickname and corrected interpretation.** “Intergalactic Wanderer” arose because its enormous distance encouraged the idea that it might be independent of, or passing outside, the Galaxy. Modern dynamical and chemical work treats it as a Galactic-halo globular cluster, though an accreted origin remains scientifically plausible. The nickname survives; the literal claim embedded in it is obsolete. [NGC 2419 historical record](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/ngc24.htm), [Bellazzini et al., “Intergalactic wanderer or Galactic globular cluster?”](https://arxiv.org/abs/0712.0729).

#### NGC 2683 — the “UFO Galaxy”

- Type: highly inclined spiral galaxy
- Discovery: **William Herschel, February 1788**
- Herschel designation: H I.200
- Nickname: “UFO Galaxy,” a modern descriptive sobriquet

Its narrow, spindle-like presentation produces the flying-saucer comparison. “UFO Galaxy” is not a historical Herschel or NGC name.

#### NGC 2537 — the Bear’s Paw Galaxy

A blue compact dwarf or peculiar star-forming galaxy. “Bear’s Paw” is a modern visual nickname, probably based on its knotty appearance; it does not preserve a Great Bear myth.

#### NGC 2500

A nearby barred spiral galaxy, discovered by William Herschel. It belongs to the loose NGC 2841 galaxy group.

#### NGC 2541

A late-type spiral galaxy used in extragalactic distance-scale work, including Cepheid observations.

#### NGC 2770 — the “Supernova Factory”

NGC 2770 acquired this journalistic nickname after several core-collapse supernovae occurred in a short observational interval:

- **SN 1999eh**
- **SN 2007uy**
- **SN 2008D**
- later **SN 2015bh**, an unusual eruptive/transient event whose exact classification has been debated

**Documented fact.** While monitoring SN 2007uy, the *Swift* satellite detected the X-ray outburst associated with **SN 2008D** on 9 January 2008, providing an exceptionally early view of a supernova. Its subtype has been described as Ib or Ib/c in successive notices and analyses. “Observed while exploding” is useful shorthand but can misleadingly imply a direct optical view of the exact core-collapse instant. [NASA GCN archive for SN 2008D](https://gcn.nasa.gov/circulars/events/sn-2008d), [Berkeley supernova record](https://w.astro.berkeley.edu/bait/2008/sn2008D.html).

#### NGC 2782

A peculiar post-merger galaxy with tidal structure and active star formation.

#### Lynx galaxy clusters, supercluster, and Lynx Arc

The names **Lynx Cluster**, **Lynx Supercluster**, and **Lynx Arc** are positional modern names: they indicate that the objects are viewed in the direction of Lynx.

The **Lynx Arc** is a strongly gravitationally lensed, intensely star-forming background object seen through a foreground cluster. It is not a stellar arc forming part of Hevelius’s cat. [NASA, Lynx Arc](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/hubble/the-lynx-arc-stretched-magnified-light-from-a-supercluster-of-blue-white-stars/).

#### APM 08279+5255

A very distant gravitationally lensed quasar in Lynx, notable for extreme apparent luminosity. Its inclusion in Lynx is purely a consequence of Delporte’s sky boundaries.

A modern NGC/IC compilation counts more than one hundred catalogued galaxies and clusters within Lynx; the brightest include NGC 2683, 2419, 2500, 2782, 2537, 2776, 2541, 2770, 2549, 2344, 2712, and 2552. [NGC/IC objects in Lynx](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/lynx-deepsky-objects).

### Meteor showers

- **September Lyncids — 081 SLY:** a weak northern shower or radiant complex, with an outburst observed in 2005 and subsequent video evidence for recurring activity.
- **September–October Lyncids — 424 SOL:** retained on the IAU Meteor Data Center working list.

**Documented but classification-sensitive.** Meteor-shower catalogs are actively revised; radiant solutions can be merged, divided, or demoted. The International Meteor Organization’s recent calendars advise treating the difficult complex broadly as the September Lyncids. It is not a major annual visual shower. [IAU Meteor Data Center](https://iaumdc.astro.sk/working), [IMO 2026 calendar](https://imo.net/files/meteor-shower/cal2026.pdf).

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the seventeenth century: no Lynx constellation

#### Babylonian and Egyptian skies

**Negative finding.** No Babylonian or pharaonic-Egyptian constellation securely identified as a lynx occupied Hevelius’s exact territory. The field falls north of the zodiac and consists mainly of faint stars; evidence from cuneiform lists, Dendera, Egyptian decanal lists, or classical testimonia does not establish an antecedent.

This does not mean that no individual star was ever observed. It means that continuity from a Babylonian or Egyptian lynx figure to Hevelius cannot be reconstructed.

#### Greek and Roman astronomy

**Documented negative.** Lynx is absent from:

- Eudoxus and Aratus;
- the *Catasterismi* associated with Eratosthenes;
- Hipparchus’s surviving astronomical commentary;
- Ptolemy’s 48 constellations in the *Almagest*;
- Hyginus’s *Poetica Astronomica*;
- Manilius’s *Astronomica*;
- the scholia to Aratus and Germanicus.

Ptolemy catalogued nearby stars under Ursa Major, Auriga, Gemini, Cancer, Leo, or as **ἀμόρφωτοι**, “unformed” stars outside a constellation picture. There is no classical Lynx catasterism and no ancient author says Hera, Zeus, Artemis, Athena, or another god placed a lynx in this part of the sky.

#### Arabic and Persian astronomy

**Documented negative with surviving star lore.** Al-Sufi’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* follows Ptolemy’s constellation framework and therefore contains no constellation corresponding to Hevelius’s Lynx. Stars now within Lynx could be described as unformed stars or as extensions of the Great Bear’s feet. Arabic names such as *al-shawka* and *al-mabsūṭa* belong to that ursine star-lore.

No medieval Persian translation consulted supplies an independent Persian lynx constellation. Persian astronomy inherited the Ptolemaic-Arabic structure, with Persian animal terminology appearing principally in translation rather than as evidence of an ancient native Lynx.

### Sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries

#### Dürer, 1515

Albrecht Dürer’s northern star map, prepared with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, depicts the Ptolemaic northern sky. It has no Lynx. Stars in the future Lynx region remain associated with adjacent ancient figures or unfigured space.

#### Bayer, 1603

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* likewise predates Lynx. Consequently no “original Bayer letters of Lynx” exist. Later authors who speak loosely of Bayer’s α Lyncis are anachronistic.

#### Jordanus Fluvius

**Documented predecessor, but not an ancestral lynx.** Petrus Plancius introduced **Jordanus**, the River Jordan, on early-seventeenth-century globes or charts. It wound through the broad northern gap among Ursa Major, Leo, Gemini, Auriga, and adjacent figures. Parts of the future Lynx field were consequently absorbed into Jordanus on some maps.

**Qualification.** Statements that “all nineteen Lynx stars formerly belonged to Jordanus” are too definite. Jordanus was an overlay on a region whose allocation varied by cartographer; it had neither modern borders nor stable universal membership. Hevelius did not simply rename Jordanus.

#### Julius Schiller, 1627

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* predates Lynx by sixty years. It therefore could not replace “Lynx” with a particular saint.

In the future Lynx area, Schiller redistributed Bayer’s surrounding stars among his Christian equivalents of Ursa Major, Auriga, Gemini, Cancer, and Leo. Most conspicuously:

- Ursa Major became the **Barque of Saint Peter**;
- Auriga became **Saint Jerome** in Schiller’s northern Christian scheme;
- Gemini became the apostle **Saint James the Greater** in the zodiacal replacement.

The precise Christian figure covering an individual modern Lynx star depends on which pre-Hevelian constellation Schiller assigned that star to. A claim that “Schiller transformed Lynx into Saint X” is therefore chronologically impossible. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/), [Linda Hall Library discussion](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/12-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum/).

### Hevelius: 1687/1690

Johannes Hevelius completed the *Prodromus Astronomiae*, *Catalogus Stellarum Fixarum*, and *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* in Danzig/Gdańsk. The engraved work bears **1687** in its production history, but Hevelius died that year; his widow and scientific collaborator **Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius** brought it to publication in **1690**.

This explains the two dates commonly encountered:

- **1687:** completion/date carried by parts of the work;
- **1690:** posthumous publication.

Calling either date simply “wrong” obscures the publication history.

#### Hevelius’s own account

**Documented fact.** Hevelius says he placed Lynx:

- between **Ursa Major and Auriga**;
- above Gemini;
- where celestial globes had a vacant space commonly filled with a dedication or inscription;
- after detecting **nineteen small stars**;
- without a telescope, by naked-eye observation with his large sighting instruments;
- because the fifth- and sixth-magnitude stars required excellent vision.

This is the first secure description of Lynx as a constellation. The operative motive is simultaneously cartographic and witty: fill the conspicuous blank with an animal proverbially difficult to match in eyesight.

Hevelius sometimes used **Tigris**, “Tiger,” as an alternative catalogue heading, but the atlas image and enduring name are *Lynx*. The alternative does not prove Chinese influence and should not be merged with China’s White Tiger.

[Digitized Hevelius atlas](https://dspace.ut.ee/items/e0e0d294-bf84-4218-8f7c-0188ed3ff4c0), [Hevelius catalogue and explanatory text](https://www.e-rara.ch/download/pdf/207915.pdf).

### Flamsteed: 1712–1729

John Flamsteed accepted Lynx into the British catalogue-and-atlas tradition. His prematurely published 1712 catalogue and authorized posthumous *Historia Coelestis Britannica* (1725), followed by the *Atlas Coelestis* (1729), consolidated numerical designations in the region.

Later summaries often say Flamsteed or Burritt gave Lynx “44 stars.” This refers to catalogue or atlas membership, not forty-four stars visibly outlining the cat. Boundary drift explains why the sequence does not map perfectly onto modern Lynx.

### Bode before 1801

Bode included Hevelian constellations in his increasingly elaborate popular and professional charts, including editions of *Vorstellung der Gestirne* from the 1780s. In German explanatory matter the animal is **der Luchs**; formal chart labels remain Latin.

### Bode’s *Uranographia*, 1801

**Documented fact.** Lynx appears on **Plate V** with **Auriga**, **Camelopardalus**, Perseus at the edge, and the now-obsolete **Telescopium Herschelii**. Bode’s index reads:

> *V. 13. Auriga. 14. Lynx. 15. Telescopium Herschelii.*

Bode retains Hevelius’s long-bodied spotted cat, but plots a much denser telescopic sky around it. His atlas contains over **17,000 stars** and thousands of nebulous objects drawn from contemporary catalogues; the figure is therefore surrounded by far more plotted points than Hevelius’s original nineteen.

The printed label on the animal is **LYNX**. Bode did not rename it after a monarch, patron, instrument, or voyage. The adjacent Herschel telescope, by contrast, commemorated William Herschel and the discovery of Uranus.

Bode’s prefatory index and atlas place Lynx on Plate V; the oft-repeated “Plate VIII” belongs to the later *Urania’s Mirror* card, not Bode’s atlas. [Bode’s Latin atlas and index](https://www.scribd.com/document/733251494/OAM-19-Bode-Uranographia), [NYPL Bode collection](https://old-digitalcollections.nypl.org/collections/joannis-elerti-bode-uranographia-sive-astrorum-descriptio-viginti-tabulis-aeneis), [Plate V description](https://www.sunflower-astronomy.com/Books/Bode_Uranographia/Bode_Uranographia.html), [University of Oklahoma exhibit](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/map-heavens.html).

The brief’s phrase “Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be reconciled with Bode’s published Lynx inventory or with the *Uranographia*’s stated contents. It may describe metadata in a particular digital collection rather than a historical count. No primary Bode source consulted assigns Lynx 830 stars or divides it into 109 internal groups.

### Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*, 1822–1825

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* (1822) received Lynx and made the animal more naturalistic than Flamsteed’s rendition.

Sidney Hall’s hand-colored card **“Lynx and Telescopium Herschilii,” Plate 8** of *Urania’s Mirror* (1824–25), paired the living Hevelian constellation with Bode’s neighboring but ultimately rejected telescope. The cards were pierced at the star positions so that the stars appeared illuminated when held to light. [Adler/Google Arts & Culture catalogue](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/urania-s-mirror-or-a-view-of-the-heavens/tgHfIK26Sl7b4w?hl=en), [card image and provenance](https://pdimagearchive.org/images/39b9f22c-0b74-4189-9a48-a6c7c44d2cbc).

### Burritt, Argelander, Heis, and nineteenth-century pruning

Elijah H. Burritt’s American atlases and schoolbooks retained Lynx, often with **44 stars** assigned to it.

Friedrich Wilhelm Argelander and Eduard Heis increasingly emphasized measured stellar positions and practical naked-eye limits rather than luxuriant animal pictures. Comparative nineteenth-century discussion noted that Lynx’s outline and membership differed markedly among Hevelius, Flamsteed, Bode, Argelander, and Heis. Camille Flammarion remarked in 1882 that Lynx had survived and saw no contemporary reason to remove it. [Flammarion, *Les Étoiles et les curiosités du ciel*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AFlammarion_-_Les_%C3%89toiles_et_les_curiosit%C3%A9s_du_ciel%2C_1882.djvu/183).

### The IAU: 1922–1930

At Rome in 1922 the IAU regularized a list of 88 constellations and their abbreviations; **Lynx was kept**. In 1928 it approved Delporte’s boundaries, published in 1930.

Why did Lynx survive while Telescopium Herschelii, Jordanus, and many other inventions did not?

**Scholarly reconstruction.** No single surviving resolution gives an aesthetic defense of Lynx. Its survival is best explained by established international usage: it had been present in major atlases and catalogues for more than two centuries and supplied useful nomenclature for an otherwise awkward northern field. It did not seriously conflict with a more prestigious ancient constellation pattern.

## Invention, myth, and later legends

Because Lynx is modern, it has no genuine Greek or Roman catasterism. Nevertheless, two bodies of ancient material became attached to it later: the mythology of the animal lynx and the mythology of **Lynceus**.

### Lynceus the far-sighted Argonaut

#### Argonautic catalogue

**Ancient myth; not a constellation myth.** Lynceus, son of Aphareus and Arene and brother of Idas, appears among the Argonauts.

- Pindar mentions the Apharetidae in *Nemean* 10, composed in the fifth century BCE.
- Apollonius of Rhodes, *Argonautica* 1.151–155, third century BCE, assigns Lynceus preternatural eyesight.
- Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9.16, probably first or second century CE, lists Idas and Lynceus among Jason’s crew.
- Hyginus, *Fabulae* 14, first centuries BCE/CE in transmitted form, says Lynceus could see objects hidden underground and was not impeded by darkness.

Apollonius is the earliest surviving extensive text explicitly connecting that penetrating sight with the Argonautic expedition. Lost earlier epic may underlie it.

#### Seeing through the earth

**Ancient myth.** Apollonius says Lynceus had the sharpest eyes of mortals and, if report was true, could see even beneath the earth. Hyginus elaborates that underground objects and darkness could not block his vision.

Later retellings expand the power to seeing through trees, walls, clouds, or skins. These details should be cited separately; they are not all present in each ancient witness.

#### Cattle division and the deaths of Lynceus and Idas

**Ancient myth with competing variants.**

In Pindar, *Nemean* 10.55–90, Lynceus sees Castor and Polydeuces from Mount Taygetus, or detects an ambush associated with a hollow oak. Idas kills Castor; Polydeuces kills Lynceus; Zeus destroys Idas with a thunderbolt and offers Polydeuces the alternating immortality he shares with Castor.

Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 3.11.2, explains the quarrel through disputed division of cattle. Idas divides an ox into four portions and awards half the herd to the first man to eat his share and the remainder to the second; he consumes both his own and Lynceus’s portions, taking the cattle. The Dioscuri later retaliate. Lynceus detects them in ambush; battle follows.

Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 3.13.1 and 4.3.1, knows the Messenian places, tombs, and cult-geography connected with the Apharetidae and Dioscuri.

Theocritus, *Idyll* 22.137–211, gives a different emphasis: the conflict arises around the abducted daughters of Leucippus. Castor fights Lynceus in single combat and kills him.

Ovid, *Fasti* 5.699–720, and later Roman accounts retell the fatal conflict of the paired brothers. Nonnus alludes to the proverbial vision of Lynceus rather than creating a Lynx catasterism.

**Important distinction.** None of these sources says Lynceus was transformed into the constellation Lynx. The ancient celestial immortality in this narrative belongs to the Dioscuri, conventionally Gemini—not to Lynceus.

#### Calydonian boar hunt

**Ancient tradition.** Lynceus appears in some catalogues of hunters, including Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 8.304. His presence reflects the common mythographic practice of assembling heroic catalogues. No surviving ancient source turns this episode into the origin of Lynx.

### Did Hevelius name the constellation for Lynceus?

**Disputed modern interpretation.**

Evidence for:

- Hevelius explicitly uses the adjective/name **Lynceus** in his explanation.
- Lynceus was proverbially far-sighted in classical and Renaissance education.
- The pun between *lynx*, *Lynceus*, and *oculi Lyncei* would have been obvious to a learned Latin reader.

Evidence against a literal identification:

- Hevelius draws an animal, not a man or Argonaut.
- His heading and atlas label are *Lynx*.
- His explanation concerns faint stars and eyesight.
- He never mentions Jason, Argo, Aphareus, Idas, or a catasterism.

**Finding.** The strongest reconstruction is that Hevelius chose the **animal lynx**, while making a learned pun on **Lynceus** and on the conventional phrase “lynx-like eyes.” Claims that “Lynx represents Lynceus the Argonaut” are later interpretive mythology, not Hevelius’s documented statement.

### Animal lore behind “lynx eyes”

Greek and Roman natural history credited the lynx with exceptional sight and other marvelous properties. Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 8 and 37, transmits animal and lapidary traditions, including stories surrounding lynx urine hardening into the gemstone *lyncurium*. Medieval bestiaries repeated variations.

**Tradition rather than zoology.** The Eurasian lynx has effective low-light senses, but the supernatural ability to see through solid objects belongs to myth and proverbial language.

### Classical myths not attached to the constellation

No Lynx entry exists in the surviving *Catasterismi* of Eratosthenes or its Epitome, Hyginus’s *Poetica Astronomica*, the Aratean scholia, Germanicus, Ovid’s constellation etiologies, or Manilius. Consequently there is no honest way to provide competing ancient “who placed Lynx in heaven” accounts. Modern websites supplying Zeus, Hera, Artemis, or Jason as the placer are inventions unless they explicitly describe a new fiction.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Arabic and Bedouin sky

The stars were principally understood in relation to **Ursa Major** and its feet or in the interstitial unformed sky.

- **al-shawka**, “the thorn,” survives in Alsciaukat.
- **al-mabsūṭa**, “the outstretched [paw],” survives as the unofficial Mabsuthat.
- Related Great Bear foot-star names distinguish extended and contracted paws in Arabic uranography.

**Documented limitation.** Al-Sufi did not draw a lynx here. Bedouin star lore was neither identical to Ptolemy nor uniform across the Arabic-speaking world, but no securely sourced Bedouin Lynx asterism in these exact stars emerged from the consulted material.

### Chinese astronomy

The figure-specific warning—“the tiger in Chinese sky? no”—is correct.

**Documented fact.** The modern territory of Lynx overlaps traditional Chinese sectors associated with both the **White Tiger of the West** and the **Vermilion Bird of the South**, but this does not mean the stars formed a tiger. The Four Symbols are vast directional sky-regions, not Western-style pictures covering every star.

Traditional asterisms represented in the modern Lynx area include portions or added stars of:

- **內階, Nèijiē**, “Inner Steps,” in the Purple Forbidden Enclosure;
- **軒轅, Xuānyuán**, the Yellow Emperor, extending principally through Leo and neighboring territory;
- other small courtly, official, or infrastructural asterisms depending on the historical Chinese catalogue and reconstruction.

**Rejected identification.** “The Chinese called Lynx the Tiger” conflates three things:

1. Hevelius’s alternate Latin *Tigris*;
2. the enormous Chinese White Tiger quadrant;
3. a nineteenth- or twentieth-century popular assertion that the same stars made a tiger.

No primary Chinese chart consulted depicts Hevelius’s long cat as a tiger. [Chinese asterisms in modern Lynx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx_in_Chinese_astronomy), [Dunhuang star-atlas study](https://arxiv.org/abs/0906.3034).

### Indian astronomy

The modern Lynx field is well north of the ecliptic. The canonical nakshatras are lunar mansions centered on the Moon’s path and therefore do not create a Lynx equivalent.

Individual stars might appear in later Indian, Islamic-Indian, or modern planetarium catalogues, but no classical Sanskrit *nakshatra* corresponds to Lynx. Modern Indian-language names meaning wildcat are translations of the IAU constellation.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

**Negative finding.** No securely datable rabbinic or biblical constellation identifiable as Lynx occupies these stars. Biblical animal terms sometimes translated “wildcat,” “lion,” or “leopard” have generated speculative modern star maps, but textual resemblance does not establish positional identity.

The Sefer Yetzirah zodiacal correspondences and later Kabbalistic mappings concern the zodiac, planets, letters, months, or traditional constellations. Lynx, lying away from the ecliptic and invented in the seventeenth century, has no ancient Kabbalistic assignment.

Modern “Hebrew constellations” that assign a tribe, letter, angel, or biblical creature to Lynx should be labeled modern esoteric systems unless their precise historical chart is produced.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

**Negative finding.** Classical Hermetica and standard medieval alchemical emblem books predate Lynx. There is no inherited planetary metal, zodiacal dignity, or alchemical operation belonging to it.

A lynx can symbolize penetration, secret sight, or discernment in early-modern emblematic thought; such symbolism may be applied retrospectively to the constellation. That is an allegorical reading, not a documented part of Hevelius’s astronomical scheme.

### Astrology

Lynx is non-zodiacal. Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* assigns planetary temperaments to stars of the 48 ancient constellations but cannot mention Hevelius’s Lynx.

- No classical astrological delineation belongs to “the constellation Lynx.”
- No canonical Babylonian, Arabic, Indian, or Chinese lunar mansion is called Lynx.
- No recognized **Behenian fixed star** lies in Lynx under that name.
- Modern astrologers sometimes extrapolate “keenness,” “secrecy,” or “solitary observation” from the animal. These are modern inventions.

## Iconography

### Ancient art

#### Farnese Atlas

**Negative evidence.** The second-century CE sculpture known as the Farnese Atlas displays the ancient Greco-Roman constellations on a globe. It has no Lynx. Any faint figures in the relevant region should not be retroactively identified with Hevelius’s creation.

#### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

No securely identified ancient coin, cathedral portal, zodiacal calendar, or medieval ceiling represents Lynx as this constellation. Lynx-like animals may appear in hunting scenes, bestiaries, or allegory, but resemblance alone is not astronomical identification.

### Al-Sufi manuscripts

Al-Sufi manuscripts illustrate the Ptolemaic figures twice, as viewed on a globe and as seen in the sky. There is no Lynx. The future territory belongs to the periphery of the Great Bear, Auriga, and unformed stars.

### Dürer, 1515

Dürer’s northern hemisphere map contains no Lynx. Its importance here is evidentiary: it demonstrates how conspicuous the cartographic gap remained before early-modern constellation invention.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer provides no Lynx image and no Lynx Bayer letters. The region is distributed among older figures and unfigured space.

### Hevelius, 1687/1690

The first canonical image is Hevelius’s plate: an elongated, spotted wild cat threaded through the nineteen faint stars between Ursa Major and Auriga. Like the rest of the *Firmamentum*, it is drawn from the **external or globe viewpoint**, reversed from the sky as an Earthbound viewer normally sees it.

The figure has sometimes been mocked as a “weasel-built” creature. That is a later aesthetic judgment, not Hevelius’s zoological claim. [Hevelius Lynx plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Johannes_Hevelius_-_Prodromus_Astronomia_-_Volume_III_%22Firmamentum_Sobiescianum,_sive_uranographia%22_-_Tavola_Y_-_Lynx.jpg).

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s Plate V presents a large, realistically shaded, long-bodied lynx spanning the star field east of Auriga. Its paws and torso overlap the conventional stellar chain. **Telescopium Herschelii** stands immediately below or beside it. Bode’s plate combines scientific density with allegorical engraving: the artwork does not define legal boundaries.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson retained the animal and adapted the artistic style to the early nineteenth-century atlas market. His treatment is generally more naturalistic than Flamsteed’s.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824–25

Plate 8 shows **Lynx and Telescopium Herschilii** in vivid hand coloring. The spelling *Herschilii* is a variant or engraver’s Latinization, not another astronomer. The card’s pierced star holes turned constellation education into a domestic illuminated display.

### Later scientific atlases

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* and Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* reduced or eliminated pictorial distraction. By the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Lynx increasingly became a labeled territory and geometric line rather than an engraved animal.

## People, events, and works

### Johannes and Elisabeth Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius performed the observations and composed the catalogue and atlas. Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius helped with observations and prepared the posthumous publication. Describing Lynx simply as “invented by Johannes in 1690” omits her material role in bringing the work into circulation, although the explanatory first person and design are Johannes’s.

### John Flamsteed

Flamsteed’s adoption made Lynx part of English catalogue practice. His numbering remains embedded in modern stellar identifiers even where Delporte later placed stars across neighboring borders.

### William Herschel

Herschel’s discoveries gave Lynx two of its best-known telescopic objects: NGC 2683 and NGC 2419. Bode could include Herschel’s discoveries as nebulous entries, while simultaneously depicting the adjacent commemorative constellation Telescopium Herschelii.

### Astronomical navigation

**Negative finding.** Lynx’s faint stars were not major traditional oceanic navigation stars. There is no evidence that explorers steered by “the Lynx” before Hevelius, and α Lyncis is too faint to rival Polaris, Capella, Castor, Pollux, Regulus, or Ursa Major’s principal stars. Modern celestial navigators could use catalogued stars in the region, but no explorer-specific Lynx tradition was verified.

### Lynx X-ray Observatory

The **Lynx X-ray Observatory** was a NASA-funded flagship mission concept prepared for the Astro2020 process, later feeding discussion of a future high-resolution X-ray flagship.

Its name plays on extraordinary vision and the ability of X-rays to reveal an otherwise invisible universe. Some institutional material additionally associates the name with Galileo’s **Accademia dei Lincei**, the “Academy of the Lynxes,” whose emblem also signified penetrating sight. It was a concept study, not a launched spacecraft. [NASA Marshall Lynx concept](https://wwwastro.msfc.nasa.gov/lynx/), [NASA concept report](https://wwwastro.msfc.nasa.gov/lynx/docs/LynxConceptStudy.pdf), [CfA account of the name](https://www.cfa.harvard.edu/facilities-technology/telescopes-instruments/lynx-x-ray-observatory), [NASA Lynx2030 group](https://science.nasa.gov/astrophysics/programs/physics-of-the-cosmos/community/lynx2030-sag/).

### Accademia dei Lincei

Founded in Rome in 1603 by Federico Cesi and companions, the academy chose the lynx as an emblem of acute observation. Galileo joined in 1611 and proudly used the title *Lincean*. The academy predates Hevelius’s constellation and supplies cultural context for the early-modern prestige of “lynx-eyed” inquiry, but no evidence establishes that Hevelius named the constellation to honor Galileo or the academy.

### Literary and artistic reception

Unlike Orion, Andromeda, or the Great Bear, Lynx has no large inherited body of poetry or monumental architecture. Its most persistent cultural themes are:

- the nearly invisible constellation;
- the observer with “lynx eyes”;
- the ambiguity between animal and Lynceus;
- NGC 2419 as a wanderer;
- the juxtaposition with Herschel’s telescope in Bode and *Urania’s Mirror*.

Modern poems, paintings, songs, businesses, ships, and vehicles titled *Lynx* ordinarily refer to the animal. Without explicit stellar imagery or authorial evidence, they should not be catalogued as works about the constellation.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. 1687 or 1690?

**Resolution:** both dates have legitimate uses. The work was completed or dated in 1687 and published posthumously in 1690. “First appeared in 1690” describes public issue; “introduced in 1687” describes the atlas’s preparation and internal dating.

### 2. Animal or Lynceus?

**Disputed interpretation.** Hevelius drew an animal and called it *Lynx*. His sentence deliberately invokes *Lynceus*. The evidence supports a pun or double resonance, but not a literal human catasterism.

### 3. “Eyes of a lynx” versus “eyes of Lynceus”

A linguistic tradition, especially visible in French discussions, argues that the proverbial “lynx eyes” arose through confusion with far-sighted Lynceus.

**Uncertain etymological claim.** Ancient literature independently credited both Lynceus and the animal with extraordinary perception. Similar spelling encouraged contamination, but proving that every European “lynx-eyed” idiom derives solely from a mistaken Lynceus reference is difficult. Hevelius’s Latin itself exploits rather than resolves the ambiguity.

### 4. Lynx or Tiger?

**Documented variant.** *Tigris* appears as a Hevelian catalogue alternative; the atlas fixes *Lynx*. “Tiger” is not a separate constellation invented by Hevelius in the same place.

### 5. Chinese Tiger

**Popular error.** Lynx overlaps part of the directional sky governed by the White Tiger, but its stars were not collectively a tiger. The assertion seems to have spread through simplified twentieth-century star-lore books and websites.

### 6. Jordanus as direct predecessor

**Partly true but overstated.** Jordanus occupied much of the gap in some early-modern maps. It did not have Delporte-like boundaries, and Hevelius’s nineteen stars cannot all be assigned to it without checking each historical chart.

### 7. Bayer designations

**Popular error.** Bayer did not designate α Lyncis in 1603. The constellation did not exist. The designation was applied retrospectively.

### 8. “Elvashak” and “Maculosa”

**Unapproved and poorly sourced.** Neither is an IAU proper name. Their repetition on star-name sites should not be confused with documentary evidence of medieval usage.

### 9. Alsciaukat’s meaning

The defensible derivation is Arabic *al-shawka*, “the thorn.” Attempts to translate it as “the lynx,” “the spotted one,” or “the cat’s eye” are errors. The name predates the European Lynx figure and belongs to another stellar context.

### 10. NGC 2419 outside the Milky Way

**Obsolete interpretation embodied in a nickname.** “Intergalactic Wanderer” records a historical suspicion, not the current consensus. It is a distant Galactic-halo cluster, possibly accreted.

### 11. IAU adoption date

It is misleading to give “1930” as the sole date:

- 1922: standardized constellation list and abbreviations;
- 1928: approval of boundaries;
- 1930: publication of Delporte’s definitive tables and charts.

### 12. Constellation images versus boundaries

Hevelius’s animal, Bode’s animal, modern seven-star lines, and the IAU polygon are not interchangeable. The IAU kept the **name and territory**, not a legally prescribed cat drawing.

### 13. “Bode invented Lynx”

**False.** Bode magnificently transmitted and enlarged the chart tradition, but Hevelius invented the constellation more than a century earlier.

### 14. Supernova classifications in NGC 2770

“Three Type Ib supernovae” is common shorthand, but published classifications include Ib, Ib/c, and peculiar transient terminology. SN 2015bh is especially disputed as a true terminal supernova versus an extreme luminous-blue-variable eruption or transitional event.

### 15. Hoaxes

No historically consequential Lynx-constellation hoax was found. Commercial “name a star in Lynx” services sometimes imply official recognition; such purchases do not modify IAU nomenclature. The IAU does not sell stellar names.

## Open questions

1. **Exact star-by-star reconstruction of Hevelius’s nineteen:** Modern identifications can be made from catalogue coordinates, but a definitive table should account for epoch, positional errors, duplicates, and magnitude estimates rather than copying modern popular lists.

2. **The origin of “Maculosa” and “Elvashak”:** Their first printed appearances remain unclear. Digitized nineteenth- and early twentieth-century star-name dictionaries should be searched bibliographically rather than relying on web repetition.

3. **Hevelius’s *Tigris* heading:** A full collation of surviving 1687/1690 copies could clarify whether the Lynx/Tigris variation reflects authorial revision, parallel catalogue terminology, or editorial state.

4. **Arabic paw-star identifications:** Al-Sufi manuscript traditions and later Arabic globes sometimes vary in labels and numbering. A critical Arabic edition should be used to establish exactly which modern component inherited *al-shawka* and *al-mabsūṭa*.

5. **Jordanus overlap:** A plate-by-plate comparison of Plancius, Bartsch, Bayer-derived charts, Hevelius, and Flamsteed would quantify which future Lynx stars were claimed by Jordanus.

6. **Schiller’s individual allocations:** Since Lynx did not exist in 1627, mapping every modern Lynx star to Schiller’s Christian figures requires coordinate-level comparison of his catalogue, not a single saint-name substitution.

7. **“830 stars in 109 groups”:** This figure could not be traced to Bode’s Lynx plate, catalogue, or introductory index. It may be collection metadata or a statement about an unrelated database grouping and should not be treated as a historical property of Lynx without its originating record.
