# Quadrans Muralis: Research Dossier

## Status and evidentiary conventions

**Status: abolished / not retained by the International Astronomical Union.** Quadrans Muralis was omitted from the standardized list of constellations adopted at the IAU’s first General Assembly at Rome in 1922. Eugène Delporte subsequently divided its territory between **Boötes** and **Draco** when he drew the official boundaries: proposals in 1925, approval at Leiden in 1928, publication in 1930. A few broad historical reconstructions let the older figure extend toward Hercules, but no present-day star lies in a constellation called Quadrans Muralis. It therefore has **no official IAU abbreviation or genitive**. The historical neo-Latin genitive is *Quadrantis Muralis*; modern antiquarian abbreviations include **QuM** and, less reliably, **Qua**. Neither is an IAU constellation abbreviation. [IAU history of the constellations](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), [IAU’s original 1922 list transcribed from *Transactions* I, p. 158](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html), [Delporte, *Délimitation scientifique des constellations*](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

The following labels are used throughout:

- **Documented fact** — stated in a contemporary primary source or authoritative institutional record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern identification inferred from historical positions, charts, or comparative evidence.
- **Tradition** — a later, recurrent attribution not necessarily stated by the inventor.
- **Disputed** — competing interpretations exist or the evidence is insufficient.
- **Unverified claim** — repeated in popular literature but not traced to a suitable primary source.
- **Modern invention** — a recent naming, interpretation, or revival.
- **Myth/legend** — a narrative transmitted as myth rather than historical fact.

This is a modern instrument-constellation. It has no indigenous Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Indian, or Chinese identity as a mural quadrant, and no classical catasterism.

Latin *quadrans* is the present participial noun from *quadrare* and in technical usage denotes a quarter—especially a quarter-circle instrument. *Muralis* derives from *murus*, “wall,” and means “of or attached to a wall.” The name describes the instrument rather than a mythical person or object.

### What the figure represented

**Documented fact.** It represented a large astronomical quarter-circle rigidly mounted in a meridian plane against a wall. The telescope or sight rotated along its graduated ninety-degree arc. At meridian transit an observer measured a star’s altitude or zenith distance and hence its declination.

The specific instrument commemorated was not merely “a quadrant of the Paris Observatory.” It was the large John Bird mural quadrant used at the **École Militaire observatory in Paris**:

- commissioned from the London instrument maker **John Bird** in 1772;
- dated 1774;
- radius about 2.45 metres, or roughly 7½–8 French feet depending on the contemporary description;
- obtained through the patronage of the financier Bergeret;
- installed for Lalande’s program in 1778, with the observing program interrupted and subsequently resumed in the rebuilt École Militaire observatory;
- employed especially by Lalande’s relative and collaborator **Michel Lefrançois de Lalande**, with reductions also involving Michel’s wife, **Marie-Jeanne Lefrançois de Lalande**.

The instrument eventually entered the Paris Observatory collection. [Paris Observatory exhibition catalogue](https://observatoiredeparis.psl.eu/IMG/pdf/jerome_lalande_et_maurice_loewy.pdf), [Paris Observatory, “350 years of astrometry”](https://observatoiredeparis.psl.eu/350-years-of-astrometry-at.html), [BnF catalogue record for Lalande’s observations of 8,000 northern stars](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb323404750)

### Location

Lalande placed it, in his words, in an “empty space”:

> “entre le Dragon, le Bouvier & Hercule”  
> “between Draco, Boötes and Hercules.”

Bode repeated essentially the same placement. In visual terms it occupied the faint region north of Boötes’ head and outstretched arm, south of Draco’s body, and east or northeast of the end of Ursa Major’s handle. Historical descriptions vary because the figure never acquired universally accepted boundaries:

- Fortin/Lalande: between Draco, Boötes and Hercules;
- Bode: between Draco, Hercules and Boötes;
- Jacob Green (1824): bounded by Draco, Ursa Major, Boötes and Hercules;
- Hannah Bouvier (1858): immediately north of Boötes;
- R. H. Allen (1899): between Hercules’ right foot, Boötes’ left hand and Draco.

The popular description “between β Boötis (Nekkar) and η Ursae Majoris (Alkaid)” gives the general field, not a defensible historical boundary.

## Stars and objects

## The membership problem

Quadrans Muralis never had Delporte-style borders. “Which stars were in it?” therefore has at least three different answers:

1. the stars Lalande assigned to *Le Mural* in his 1796 table;
2. the stars physically covered by the larger figure on Fortin’s 1795 plate;
3. the smaller set covered by Bode’s revised 1801 image and borders.

Lalande’s printed table is variously reported as containing **nine or ten** stars of approximately fifth through seventh magnitude. The disagreement appears to arise from counting or reading the table rather than from two securely documented editions. John Barentine reports ten; the All Skies reconstruction and several European summaries report nine. The Fortin illustration contains more plotted points than the accompanying catalogue explicitly assigns.

Multiple system; its close B–C pair is a W Ursae Majoris contact/eclipsing variable. The IAU named component A **Quadrans** in 2025. |
| **CL Draconis**, HR 5960, HD 143466, HIP 78180 | Draco | 4.96 | Brightest securely identified star in Bode’s reduced figure; F-type subgiant and low-amplitude δ Scuti variable. |

The useful modern summary of the reconstructed field lists these seven brighter objects. [All Skies Encyclopaedia reconstruction](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Quadrans_Muralis)

### Brightest star and the new name *Quadrans*

Two statements often confused in popular accounts are both defensible in different senses:

- **Scholarly reconstruction:** 44 Boötis was the brightest star in Lalande’s broad version.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** CL Draconis was the brightest star enclosed by Bode’s smaller 1801 rendering.

On 17 February 2025 the IAU Working Group on Star Names assigned **Quadrans** to **44 Boötis A**, GJ 575 / HIP 73695 A. This is a modern commemorative proper name, not a rediscovered ancient name and not a restoration of Quadrans Muralis as a constellation. The 2025 choice also favored a star relatively close to the modern Quadrantid radiant. [IAU announcement and adoption table](https://www.iau.org/IAU/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx), [SIMBAD: 44 Boötis](https://simbad.u-strasbg.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=44+Bootis)

### Proper names near—but not belonging to—the figure

**Nekkar**, β Boötis, and **Alkaid**, η Ursae Majoris, are often invoked as landmarks. They were not principal stars of the quadrant itself.

- **Nekkar** derives through medieval star-name transmission from Arabic designations associated with Boötes as a herdsman or digger. Its exact philological route is more complicated than the simple glosses in popular star-name books.
- **Alkaid** derives from Arabic *al-qāʾid*, “the leader,” in the phrase describing the leader of the daughters or mourners of the bier represented by the Big Dipper.

No historical proper Arabic name has been securely traced for 39, 44 or 47 Boötis, BP Boötis, or CL Draconis as members of a common indigenous asterism.

### Bayer letters and Flamsteed numbers

The figure was created almost two centuries after Bayer’s *Uranometria* (1603), so it had no original Bayer series of its own.

- 44 Boötis is Bayer **i Boötis** and Flamsteed **44 Boötis**.
- 47 Boötis is Bayer **k Boötis** and Flamsteed **47 Boötis**.
- 39 Boötis retains the Flamsteed number **39 Boötis**.
- CL Draconis and BP Boötis are twentieth-century variable-star designations.
- No α, β, γ, and so forth were authoritatively assigned “Quadrantis Muralis.”
- Lists giving “i Quadrantis” or treating *Quadrans* as an old Bayer name are erroneous.

### Internal asterisms

No smaller named Western asterism inside Quadrans Muralis has been traced. The instrument’s plotted stars were visual points along or around the frame, arc, telescope and wall, but those parts did not acquire stable individual star names comparable to the stars in Orion’s Belt.

### Deep-sky objects

No Messier object bears the constellation’s name, and no historically secure Messier object lies in the small Bode figure.

No NGC or IC object is conventionally called the “Quadrans Muralis Nebula,” “Quadrans Cluster,” or “Quadrans Galaxy.” Modern databases naturally assign galaxies and other faint objects in this direction to Boötes, Draco or Hercules. Bode’s plate plotted nebulae and clusters across the surrounding map, but no deep-sky object has been securely demonstrated to have been catalogued as an object *of Quadrans Muralis* in the way that Bode assigned NGC 1184 to the obsolete Rangifer.

Accordingly, claims that Quadrans Muralis has a named nebula, cluster, or galaxy should be regarded as **unverified unless accompanied by a catalogue citation**.

### Variables, novae and supernovae

Variables historically lying in or near reconstructed versions include:

- **44 Boötis B/C**, a W Ursae Majoris-type contact eclipsing binary;
- **CL Draconis**, a δ Scuti variable;
- **BP Boötis**, a chemically peculiar variable;
- **X Herculis**, a semiregular red variable in the broad southeastern fringe.

No historical nova or Galactic supernova is securely recorded as having occurred “in Quadrans Muralis.” The constellation existed for too short a period, and no celebrated transient acquired its obsolete name.

## Boundaries and transfers of stars

### Ptolemy, second century CE

**Documented fact and reconstruction.** Ptolemy knew no Quadrans Muralis. In the *Almagest* the area belonged conceptually to Boötes, Draco and Hercules, with numerous faint points omitted or treated as unformed. Ptolemy’s Boötes catalogue contained 22 figure stars plus Arcturus as an unformed star; the faint northern stars later used for the Quadrant generally lacked a collective figure.

### al-Ṣūfī, tenth century

**Documented fact.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* revised and illustrated the Ptolemaic constellations. Since the quadrant was not created until 1795, his manuscripts depict the surrounding parts of **al-ʿAwwāʾ/Boötes**, Draco and Hercules—not a quadrant. No al-Ṣūfī name for this modern constellation exists.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* left the relevant field sparsely populated. Stars subsequently numbered 39, 44 and 47 Boötis remained conceptually Boötis stars where plotted. Bayer supplied lowercase Latin letters after exhausting or supplementing Greek letters; hence **i** and **k Boötis**.

### Schiller, 1627

Julius Schiller remapped the older figures, not Lalande’s nonexistent future constellation:

- Boötes became **Sancti Silvestri Pontificis Maximi**, Saint Sylvester the Supreme Pontiff.
- Draco became **Sanctorum Innocentum Puerorum**, the Holy Innocent Children.
- Hercules became **Sanctorum Trium Regum**, the Three Holy Kings or Magi.

Thus the eventual Quadrant field straddled those Christian figures. There was no saint “of Quadrans Muralis.” [Library of Congress facsimile record](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/), [ETH Zürich digital edition](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720)

### Hevelius, 1687/1690

Hevelius knew no Quadrant. His *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* and posthumous *Prodromus Astronomiae* show Boötes, Hercules and Draco from an exterior or globe-like viewpoint, reversed relative to Bayer’s interior-sky convention. Much of the later Quadrant field remained faint and visually uncommitted.

### Flamsteed, catalogue and 1729 atlas

Flamsteed’s numbering fixed 39, 44 and 47 as stars of Boötes. His atlas did not show a mural quadrant. These earlier Boötes designations survived Lalande’s attempted reassignment and are the names used today.

### Lacaille, 1752–1756

Lacaille did not create Quadrans Muralis. His relevance is exemplary: after observing at the Cape, he introduced southern instrument constellations. Lalande explicitly cited Lacaille’s practice as his precedent. Any statement calling Quadrans Muralis a Lacaille constellation is false.

### Fortin/Lalande, 1795–1796

Lalande took stars from northern Boötes and the southern edge of Draco, with the figure reaching toward Hercules and Ursa Major in some readings. The 1795 atlas image appeared on the Draco plate but was not consistently carried onto the overlapping Boötes plate, a revealing sign that the revision was added locally rather than through a fully coordinated boundary system.

### Bode, 1801

Bode drew a smaller figure and adjusted his borders to reduce encroachment on the older neighbors. In consequence:

- some stars claimed in Lalande’s broad form remained or returned to Boötes;
- CL Draconis became the brightest conspicuous point of Bode’s version;
- the instrument stood above Boötes’ arm, below Draco, west of Hercules.

Bode’s *Uranographia* contained twenty large copperplate charts and was accompanied by a catalogue of **17,240 stars, doubles, nebulae and clusters**. The user-supplied lead “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be reconciled with Bode’s 1801 catalogue or the surviving bibliographical descriptions and should not be repeated as a property of the *Uranographia*. [Marseille digitization record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035), [David Rumsey collection description](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/view/all/who/Bode%2C%2BJohann%2BElert%2C%2B1747-1826/what/Celestial%2BAtlas?cic=RUMSEY~8~1&os=0&pgs=50&sort=sortid%2Cpub_list_no%2Cseries_no)

### Argelander and Heis

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) did not figure Quadrans Muralis, despite the constellation’s presence in many contemporary popular atlases. Eduard Heis likewise omitted it from his later scientific naked-eye atlas. Their rejection preceded the IAU by generations and shows that Quadrans Muralis did not suddenly disappear in 1922.

### Delporte, 1930

The final historical field was divided principally between **Boötes and Draco**. Broad reconstructions that touch Hercules remain Hercules under the modern boundaries. The modern Quadrantid radiant lies in northern Boötes.

## Chronology of attestation and reception

### Before 1795: no constellation

**Documented negative finding.** No Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, medieval Latin, Renaissance or early-modern constellation called a wall quadrant occupied this field.

The Farnese Atlas, Aratus manuscripts, al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, Dürer’s 1515 maps, Bayer’s 1603 atlas, Schiller’s 1627 Christian sky, Hevelius and Flamsteed all predate the invention.

### 1772–1778: the physical instrument

The large mural quadrant was commissioned from John Bird in London in 1772, completed or dated 1774, and installed for Lalande’s program by 1778. Dagelet began catalogue observations in the 1780s; after interruptions connected with the École Militaire observatory, Michel Lefrançois undertook the sustained program from 1789.

### 1789–1790s: observing campaign

Lalande’s memoir on 8,000 northern stars states that observations began on 5 August 1789. The larger program eventually generated observations of approximately 50,000 stars, many down to about eleventh magnitude. The data were published largely as unreduced observations in the 1801 *Histoire céleste française*. [Paris Academy memoir index and scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bb/M%C3%A9moires_de_l%27Acad%C3%A9mie_royale_des_sciences_%28IA_mmoiresdelacad10acad%29.pdf), [*Histoire céleste française*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Histoire_c%C3%A9leste_fran%C3%A7aise_contenant_l.html?id=f9AMAAAAYAAJ)

### 1795: invention and first picture

**Documented fact.** Quadrans Muralis first appeared as **Le Mural** in the revised 1795 edition of Jean Fortin’s reduced French version of Flamsteed’s atlas, edited or augmented by Lalande and Pierre Méchain.

The accompanying notice said:

> “LE MURAL, ou Quart-de-cercle Mural … est encore une nouvelle Constellation que le citoyen Lalande a placée dans une espace vide, entre le Dragon, le Bouvier & Hercule.”

It continued that Lacaille had made southern constellations from instruments of physics and the arts, and that the precious northern instrument used for observations of 30,000 stars deserved comparable commemoration.

This is the earliest secure attestation. It also proves that the invention honored **the observing instrument and its star catalogue**, not Tycho Brahe, a monarch, or the Paris Observatory as an institution.

### 1796: Lalande’s explanation and star table

In *Connaissance des temps* Lalande wrote, in substance:

> “Aux constellations déjà reçues j’ai ajouté le Mural ou quart de cercle mural … Ce Mural est dans un espace vide, entre le Dragon, le Bouvier et Hercule.”

He expressly cited Lacaille and called the instrument the basis of the “greatest monument of astronomy.” Here the number was about **32,000** observations/stars in progress. Later descriptions rose to 50,000 as the catalogue grew. The figures 30,000, 32,000 and 50,000 therefore reflect different dates or rounding, not necessarily contradiction.

### 1799: Goldbach

Christian Friedrich Goldbach included the figure in his 1799 atlas, evidence of rapid reception before Bode. His maps position it between Draco, Hercules and northern Boötes.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode put it on **Plate VII**, with Boötes, Mons Maenalus and Corona Borealis, under the Latin label **QUADRANS MURALIS**. He reduced the figure relative to Lalande and gave it a more explicitly technical neo-Latin title.

In the companion catalogue Bode wrote:

> “Dies neue Gestirn ist erst im Jahr 1795 von dem Herrn de la Lande zwischen dem Drachen, Herkules, und Bootes zum Andenken des Mauerquadranten eingeführt…”

That is: “This new constellation was introduced only in 1795 by M. de la Lande between Draco, Hercules and Boötes, in memory of the mural quadrant…” Bode adds that Lalande and his nephew Le François used it in Paris to observe a very large number of previously unpositioned sixth-magnitude and fainter stars.

This is Bode’s own description. It does not mention Tycho Brahe as the honoree. [Digitized *Uranographia*, ETH Zürich](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [complete downloadable atlas scan](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/download/pdf/3341734)

### 1801: Lalande’s *Bibliographie astronomique*

Lalande retrospectively described “le Mural, que j’ai placé dans le ciel pour conserver la mémoire de l’instrument précieux qui a servi à la détermination de 50000 étoiles”—“the Mural, which I placed in the sky to preserve the memory of the precious instrument used to determine 50,000 stars.”

This is strong primary evidence against the tradition that the constellation was specifically intended as a monument to Tycho.

### 1805: Bode’s popular atlas

The second edition of Bode’s smaller *Vorstellung der Gestirne* added the Mauerquadrant to the Boötes chart, apparently using an older copperplate with additions. It remained absent from the overlapping Draco plate. That inconsistency parallels Fortin’s and illustrates the loose status of constellation “boundaries” at the time.

### 1807: Thomas Young

Young’s *A Course of Lectures on Natural Philosophy and the Mechanical Arts* included the **Mural Quadrant**, helping establish the English vernacular form.

### 1822: Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas*, Plate VII, depicts the quadrant with Boötes, Mons Maenalus, Canes Venatici and Coma Berenices. The instrument is drawn as a substantial wall-mounted quadrant rather than a handheld navigational device. A surviving catalogue title misspells it **“Quadraus Muralis”**, a bibliographical transcription or engraving error, not an alternative Latin name. [David Rumsey, Jamieson Plate VII](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~34111~1170845%3A-Bootes-and-Mons-Maenalus%2C-Asterion)

### 1824: Jacob Green’s objection

Green acknowledged about nine very small stars, mostly seventh or eighth magnitude, but complained that the figure offered no advantage and merely encumbered catalogues with another name. This is the earliest explicit criticism located in the dedicated contested-material search.

### 1825: *Urania’s Mirror*

Sidney Hall’s pierced, hand-colored card 10, probably adapted from Jamieson, shows **“Boötes, Canes Venatici, Coma Berenices, and Quadrans Muralis.”** The quadrant appears at the top of the card. The cards could be held before a light so the punched star holes shone. [National Museums Scotland description](https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/celestial-charts)

### 1825–1839: discovery of the January meteors

Antonio Brucalassi reported unusually numerous meteors in January 1825. Adolphe Quetelet’s 1839 *Catalogue des principales apparitions d’étoiles filantes*, read to the Brussels Academy on 8 June 1839, cited conspicuous displays on 2 January 1835 and 1838 and inferred a recurring early-January period. Edward C. Herrick independently emphasized its annual character in 1839.

Thus the brief’s “Quetelet 1835” needs qualification:

- **1835** was an important observed apparition;
- **1839** is the publication date of Quetelet’s catalogue and explicit recurrence argument;
- **1825** is the earlier reported observation commonly credited to Brucalassi.

The name “Quadrantids” became natural because the reconstructed radiant lay in the then-current Quadrans Muralis. [Quetelet’s original Academy memoir](https://academieroyale.be/Academie/documents/NM12_1771839QUETELET24840.pdf), [contemporary historical discussion in *Nature*](https://www.nature.com/articles/065198e0)

### 1830s: Burritt

Elijah H. Burritt included the **Mural Quadrant** in his educational geography and atlas of the heavens. Burritt helped carry Jamieson-like iconography into American popular astronomy.

### 1843: Argelander rejects it

Argelander did not include the figure in *Uranometria Nova*. This omission is significant because that work sought a practical scientific naked-eye uranography rather than decorative comprehensiveness.

### 1849: Riedig

A German atlas reduced the label to **Quadrant**, showing that vernacular shortening existed but never displaced *Quadrans Muralis* internationally.

### 1858: Hannah Bouvier

Bouvier’s catechetical *Familiar Astronomy* asked:

> “Are there any CONSPICUOUS stars in the constellation Quadrans Muralis?”  
> “There are not.”

The exchange neatly captures why the figure did poorly in observational culture.

### 1872: Heis

Heis omitted it from *Atlas Coelestis Novus*. By then its rejection by serious naked-eye cataloguers was well advanced.

### 1899: Richard Hinckley Allen

Allen described its origin, vernacular names and meteor stream but said it was “not recognized by modern astronomers.” Its practical extinction therefore predates the IAU.

### 1922: IAU omission at Rome

The IAU Commission on Notations and Units standardized the 88 principal constellations and their three-letter abbreviations. Quadrans Muralis was absent. It was not the subject of a separate dramatic “abolition” resolution; omission from the accepted list made it unofficial.

### 1925–1930: definitive boundaries

Delporte presented his boundary system in 1925. The IAU approved it in 1928; the tables and maps appeared in 1930. The former Quadrant territory went chiefly to Boötes and Draco.

### 2003–2004: proposed parent of the meteors

Near-Earth object **2003 EH1**, discovered by LONEOS on 6 March 2003, was identified by Peter Jenniskens as a compelling dynamical parent of the Quadrantid stream. It is asteroid-like but commonly interpreted as a dormant, extinct, or fragmented cometary object.

### 2025: partial nominal revival

The IAU named 44 Boötis A **Quadrans**, preserving the obsolete figure in an official stellar proper name without restoring the constellation.

## Invention: what was being honored?

### Secure answer

**Documented fact:** Lalande intended the figure to preserve the memory of the mural quadrant and the enormous École Militaire star-observation program performed with it.

His own texts successively mention roughly 30,000, 32,000 and ultimately 50,000 stars. The project culminated in the *Histoire céleste française*.

### Lalande, Michel and Marie-Jeanne

The traditional shorthand says “Lalande and his nephew Michel used the quadrant.” This is broadly correct but incomplete.

- Michel Lefrançois was technically Lalande’s younger relative—often called his nephew in the literature—and principal observational collaborator.
- Michel’s wife Marie-Jeanne participated in calculations and reductions for the catalogue.
- Other observers, notably Dagelet, belonged to the instrument’s longer institutional history.
- Lalande was the public author, sponsor and constellation-maker, but not the sole observer or calculator.

### Lacaille’s role

Lacaille was the explicitly named precedent, not co-inventor. Lalande reasoned that, since Lacaille had populated the southern sky with scientific instruments, a northern instrument constellation was justified.

### Was it for Tycho Brahe?

**Later tradition / unsupported as an account of Lalande’s intention.** Tycho’s mural quadrant was famous, and Tycho’s highly accurate observations contributed to Kepler’s planetary work. Modern writers therefore sometimes say Lalande placed the constellation “probably in honor of Tycho Brahe.”

No such dedication appears in Lalande’s 1795–1796 explanation, Bode’s account, or Lalande’s *Bibliographie*. Those sources explicitly connect it to Lalande’s Paris instrument and catalogue. Tycho is relevant to the broader history of mural quadrants, not demonstrably the honoree of the constellation.

### Was it “the mural quadrant of the Paris Observatory”?

**Imprecise tradition.** The surviving instrument is now in the Paris Observatory collection, but the commemorated observing campaign took place at the **École Militaire observatory**. Calling it simply “the Paris Observatory quadrant” collapses provenance and present custody.

## Quadrantid meteor shower

### Name and radiant

The meteor shower’s established name is **Quadrantids**, official shower code **QUA**. Its radiant lies in modern northern Boötes, near the historical Quadrant field. The radiant is an apparent perspective point, not the physical source of the meteors.

Because the meteor name was already established before the constellation disappeared, it survived the 1922 standardization. This is the most important continuing use of the dead constellation’s name.

The often-repeated phrase “the only abolished constellation with a living annual event” is rhetorically attractive but **not an official astronomical category**. Quadrans Muralis is certainly the best-known extinct constellation preserved in the name of a major annual meteor shower. Proving absolute uniqueness would require defining “living event” and checking every historical shower synonym; no authoritative source was found declaring such a formal uniqueness.

### Activity

The Quadrantids occur around late December to mid-January and peak sharply around 3–4 January. Their maximum can be rich, but the dense core is narrow—often only several hours to roughly half a day—making the shower easy to miss. [NASA Quadrantids guide](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/meteors-meteorites/quadrantids/), [IAU Meteor Shower Nomenclature Working Group](https://www.iau.org/WG276/WG276/Home.aspx)

### Parent body: 2003 EH1

**Strong modern scientific inference:** 2003 EH1 is the leading parent of the young, dense Quadrantid core.

Jenniskens recognized the close orbital relationship in 2003–2004. Subsequent integrations generally support a recent release of the stream core, on the order of several centuries, while identifying a more extended and potentially older background.

### C/1490 Y1

**Disputed scholarly hypothesis:** 2003 EH1 may be related to the comet recorded in Chinese, Korean and Japanese sources in 1490–1491, conventionally designated **C/1490 Y1**.

Williams and collaborators integrated hundreds of orbital clones backward. Some approached an orbit and sky position resembling C/1490 Y1, but planetary encounters and the short observational arc of 2003 EH1 make long backward integrations chaotic. [Williams et al., “The parent of the Quadrantid meteoroid stream and asteroid 2003 EH1”](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/355/4/1171/992416)

A later updated analysis found that robust identification with C/1490 Y1 or the once-proposed C/1385 U1 could not be secured. Conversely, another reconstruction of the East Asian observations obtained orbital elements favorable to a relation. [Updated dynamical analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/0805.2452), [Hasegawa-style orbital reconstruction and claimed verification](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.2547)

The responsible conclusion is:

- 2003 EH1 as the Quadrantid parent: **well-supported**;
- its interpretation as an inactive comet: **probable but inferential**;
- identity with, or descent from, C/1490 Y1: **plausible but disputed**;
- categorical statements that C/1490 Y1 “is” 2003 EH1: **too strong**.

## Myths and legends

## Classical mythology

There are **no classical myths of Quadrans Muralis**.

It is absent from:

- Aratus, *Phaenomena*;
- Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* and its Epitome;
- Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* and *Fabulae*;
- the scholia to Aratus and Germanicus;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias;
- the classical and late-antique mythographers.

No god placed it in the sky; it has no competing identities; it participates in no Greek or Roman myth. Supplying the myths of Boötes, Heracles or Draco as “myths of Quadrans Muralis” would manufacture a pedigree the constellation never possessed.

### Mythic material visible around it on Bode’s plate

Bode’s plate surrounds the instrument with ancient figures:

- **Boötes**, variously Arcas, Icarius, Philomelus or the Bear-guard in classical catasterisms;
- **Hercules**, shown near Draco in the labor of the Hesperides tradition;
- **Draco**, variously the dragon Ladon or another cosmic serpent in later identifications.

Those myths belong to the neighboring ancient constellations. Quadrans Muralis merely intruded into their mapped space in 1795.

## Modern legend: Tycho’s quadrant

A modern quasi-legend identifies Lalande’s figure with Tycho Brahe’s celebrated mural quadrant at Uraniborg and says the constellation commemorated Tycho or Kepler’s laws. It first becomes conspicuous in modern popular retellings rather than in Lalande’s explanation. The association is historically suggestive but is not the documented invention story.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian and Egyptian

No secure Babylonian or Egyptian asterism can be equated with the exact faint group Lalande selected. Broad correspondences involving the ancient figures that became Boötes, Draco or Hercules do not establish an antecedent to Quadrans Muralis.

Claims of a Babylonian “quadrant” here would be anachronistic.

### Greek and Roman

The stars were portions of, or unformed stars around, Boötes, Draco and Hercules. Faint stars not included in ancient catalogues had no recoverable individual Greek names.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī retained the Ptolemaic figures while recording Arabic star lore. There is no collective *Quadrans* in his work.

The well-known Bedouin pattern **Aulād al-Dhiʾāb/Aulād al-Ḍibāʿ**, “the young of the wolves/hyenas,” is associated with another group of northern Boötes stars—commonly θ, ι, κ and λ Boötis—not with Lalande’s core stars 39, 44 and 47 Boötis. It should not be presented as an Arabic Quadrans Muralis.

No securely documented Bedouin asterism consisting specifically of 39, 44, 47 Boötis, BP Boötis and CL Draconis was found.

### Persian

The Persian form *Chārakān/Cârakân* in a modern astronomical etymological dictionary is a contemporary technical equivalent. It is not evidence for a medieval Persian constellation.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No premodern Hebrew or Kabbalistic mapping specifically corresponding to Quadrans Muralis was found. General correspondences between the zodiac, Hebrew letters, tribes, angels or sefirot do not reach this late, non-zodiacal instrument figure.

Claims that Quadrans Muralis represented a biblical measuring device, Solomon’s instrument, an angelic square, or a Kabbalistic quadrant were not traced to historical sources.

### Chinese

The area lies among ancient Chinese polar and northern-enclosure star traditions, but exact identifications of the very faint stars are difficult and vary among reconstructions. No securely sourced classical Chinese asterism containing the whole Lalande group was found.

The modern Chinese name **象限儀座** is a translation of the European constellation, not its Chinese antecedent. It must not be retrojected into the *Shi Shi*, *Gan Shi*, Dunhuang chart, Su Song chart, or Qing star catalogues.

### Indian astronomy

No nakṣatra crosses this high northern field; the lunar mansions follow the Moon’s path near the ecliptic. No Sanskrit mural-quadrant constellation or direct Indian antecedent was found.

### Christianized sky

As noted above, Schiller’s 1627 replacement figures were Saint Sylvester for Boötes, the Holy Innocents for Draco and the Three Magi for Hercules. Since Quadrans did not yet exist, it was not Christianized in its own right.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No pre-IAU alchemical or Hermetic doctrine specifically attached to Quadrans Muralis was located. A quadrant may function generically as an emblem of geometry, measure, cosmic order or the liberal arts, but transferring that symbolism to Lalande’s constellation without a source is a modern interpretive exercise.

### Astrology

Quadrans Muralis is absent from Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*, the classical fixed-star natures, the medieval Behenian star lists and the Arabic or Indian lunar mansions.

Modern tropical astrology websites sometimes assign the reconstructed figure a span in Libra and Scorpio and give it interpretive “influence.” This is **modern invention**, produced by projecting modern ecliptic longitudes of the constituent stars. It is not an ancient or medieval astrological tradition.

## Iconography

### What cannot depict it

Because the constellation dates from 1795, the following cannot contain it:

- the Farnese Atlas;
- ancient celestial globes;
- medieval Aratus manuscripts;
- al-Ṣūfī manuscripts;
- Dürer’s 1515 northern sky;
- Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria*;
- Schiller’s 1627 atlas;
- Hevelius’ 1687/1690 maps;
- Flamsteed’s 1729 *Atlas Coelestis*;
- Fortin’s 1776 and 1778 editions.

Where modern captions say the constellation is “shown” in one of these, they are either marking its future location or confusing neighboring stars with the later figure.

### Fortin, Paris, 1795

The first image, on plate 2 of the revised *Atlas céleste de Flamstéed*, depicts *Le Mural* as a quarter-circle instrument on a supporting wall or frame. It was added to the Draco chart but not consistently to the overlapping Boötes chart.

### Goldbach, Weimar, 1799

Goldbach’s atlas adopted the figure and established that it circulated before Bode’s great atlas.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s Plate VII gives the canonical Latin image. The quadrant is a rigid architectural and scientific object embedded among vigorously drawn mythological bodies. Its straight edges and graduated arc contrast deliberately with Draco’s coils and the human forms of Boötes and Hercules.

Bode’s maps are drawn with extensive constellation boundaries and a dense scientific star field. The figure is smaller than Lalande’s and does not merely copy the Fortin engraving.

### Young, London, 1807

Young presented the Mural Quadrant in an educational context, contributing to its English reception.

### Bode’s smaller atlas, Berlin, 1805

The German-labelled **Mauerquadrant** was added to the Boötes plate but not the Draco plate.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s engraved and hand-colored Plate VII places the instrument above Boötes. Its design strongly influenced later popular depictions.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, c. 1825

Card 10 copies or adapts Jamieson. The instrument survives alongside Boötes, the hunting dogs and Berenice’s Hair. The perforated star holes made the card both an illustration and a domestic teaching apparatus.

### Burritt, United States, 1830s

Burritt’s atlas carried the constellation into American astronomical education, generally in simplified form.

### Later decline

Argelander and Heis omitted it. By the later nineteenth century it persisted more often in derivative popular atlases than in scientific star catalogues.

### Coins, ceilings, portals and calendars

No securely documented ancient coin, cathedral portal, medieval ceiling or traditional calendar depicts Quadrans Muralis. Images of astronomers holding quadrants—especially conventional portraits of Ptolemy—and depictions of Tycho’s instruments belong to the iconography of the instrument, not automatically to this constellation.

No purpose-built architectural ceiling showing Lalande’s Quadrans was located in the consulted sources.

## People, events and works

### Jérôme Lalande

Inventor and public advocate of the constellation; organizer and publisher of the observing program; author of its contemporary rationale.

### Michel Lefrançois de Lalande

Principal observer and calculator in the École Militaire program. Lalande praised him in the preface to *Histoire céleste française* for reaching 50,000 stars.

### Marie-Jeanne Lefrançois de Lalande

Calculator and collaborator in the same astronomical household. Omitting her reproduces the older historiography’s tendency to compress collective computation into Lalande’s name.

### John Bird

Maker of the physical quadrant. The Paris instrument was one of Bird’s last and most elaborate instruments of its type.

### Bergeret

Patron whose support enabled Lalande to obtain the Bird quadrant for the École Militaire.

### Joseph Lepaute Dagelet

Early observer with the instrument before departing on the Lapérouse expedition, from which he did not return.

### Lacaille

Methodological precedent: his southern instrument constellations justified Lalande’s northern addition.

### Bode

Canonical Latinizer and disseminator. His magnificent 1801 plate is the image most often reproduced.

### Quetelet, Herrick and Brucalassi

Observers and historical analysts associated with recognizing the recurring January meteor shower.

### Peter Jenniskens

Identified 2003 EH1 as the likely Quadrantid parent.

### Navigation and explorers

No explorer or navigator is documented as having steered by “Quadrans Muralis” as a practical navigational constellation. Its stars are faint, the group was short-lived, and navigators favored conspicuous circumpolar patterns such as Ursa Major, Ursa Minor and Polaris.

Dagelet’s presence on Lapérouse’s voyage links an observer from the quadrant program to exploration, but it does not prove that the expedition navigated by the constellation.

### Literature, painting and music

No major poem, opera, symphony or canonical painting specifically titled *Quadrans Muralis* was found. Its strongest cultural afterlife is graphical—historical atlas reproductions—and astronomical, through the meteor shower.

### Ships, missions and buildings

No securely documented ship, spacecraft mission or building formally named *Quadrans Muralis* was located. The École Militaire observatory and the Paris Observatory collection are attached to the physical instrument, not named after the constellation.

## Controversies, disputes and errors

### 1. “Invented by Lalande and Lacaille”

**False or misleading.** Lalande invented it in 1795. Lacaille died in 1762 and served only as the stated precedent for instrument constellations.

### 2. “A constellation of Tycho Brahe”

**Later tradition, not documented intention.** Tycho’s mural quadrant is relevant to instrument history. Lalande’s own explanation honors his Paris instrument and catalogue.

### 3. Paris Observatory versus École Militaire

The instrument’s present custody at Paris Observatory has encouraged the phrase “Paris Observatory quadrant.” The observing program commemorated by Lalande took place at the École Militaire observatory. The distinction is historically important.

### 4. Nine versus ten stars

Both counts recur. The map shows more stars than the table assigns. Modern summaries apparently count Lalande’s printed entries differently. A definitive resolution requires a fresh collation of every row and continuation mark in the 1796 table.

### 5. Lalande’s versus Bode’s brightest star

44 Boötis is brightest in the broad Lalande reconstruction; CL Draconis is brightest in Bode’s smaller figure. Declaring either the unique “alpha star” is wrong: the constellation had no official α.

### 6. “Bode invented the constellation”

False. Bode Latinized, resized and popularized it. Fortin/Lalande had printed it six years earlier; Goldbach also preceded Bode.

### 7. “Bode simply copied Fortin”

Too simple. The figure ultimately derives from Lalande/Fortin, but Bode redrew it, altered the extent, and placed it into an independently structured, much denser atlas.

### 8. “Abolished in 1930”

Incomplete. The decisive exclusion from the IAU name list occurred in 1922. Boundary work continued through 1925 and 1928 and was published in 1930.

### 9. “Formally abolished by a special 1922 vote”

No separate targeted abolition has been found. It disappeared through omission from the standardized list.

### 10. “The IAU adopted the list only in 1930”

Misleading. The IAU agreed on the constellation list and abbreviations in Rome in 1922. The boundaries were later approved and published.

### 11. Official abbreviation and genitive

Quadrans Muralis has no IAU abbreviation or genitive. **QuM**, **Qua**, and *Quadrantis Muralis* are historical or modern editorial conveniences.

### 12. 44 Boötis “always had the name Quadrans”

False. **Quadrans** was adopted for 44 Boötis A in 2025. It is a deliberate modern commemoration.

### 13. 2003 EH1 equals C/1490 Y1

Disputed and often overstated. Similarity among some backward-integrated clones does not establish identity. Planetary encounters make the result chaotic.

### 14. “Quetelet discovered the shower in 1835”

Needs qualification. The 1835 display was one of the observations; Brucalassi reported January meteors in 1825; Quetelet and Herrick argued for recurrence in 1839.

### 15. “The Quadrantids were named in 1835”

Not established by the sources consulted. Recognition of a recurring early-January shower and later naming were processes, not necessarily one documented christening in 1835.

### 16. “The only dead constellation with a living event”

A useful popular characterization, but not an IAU finding and difficult to prove under all possible definitions.

### 17. *Quadraus Muralis*

The form occurs in a catalogue title for the Jamieson plate. It is a typographical or transcriptional corruption, not a valid alternative etymology.

### 18. Ancient Arabic, Hebrew, Persian or Chinese Quadrans

No evidence. Modern translations and nearby indigenous asterisms should not be turned into antecedents.

### 19. Astrological fixed-star lore

Assignments of a Libra–Scorpio span and occult “influence” are modern constructions. Ptolemy, the Behenian lists and lunar-mansion traditions do not recognize Quadrans Muralis.

### 20. “830 stars in 109 groups”

This supplied lead could not be traced to Bode’s *Uranographia*. Bode’s companion catalogue advertises **17,240** objects/stars. The numbers 830 and 109 may belong to another collection, indexing system, or exhibition description, but no evidentiary connection was found.

### Dedicated hoax and controversy finding

Searches combining “Quadrans Muralis” with *dispute, controversy, misidentification, misprint, hoax, obsolete,* and *abolished* produced no documented deliberate hoax. The genuine contested material consists instead of:

- attribution to Tycho versus Lalande’s documented purpose;
- the exact star membership and extent;
- nine versus ten catalogue stars;
- 1922 versus 1930 terminology;
- the meteor shower’s discovery date;
- the C/1490 Y1 parentage hypothesis;
- later astrological and multicultural retrojections.

---

## Open questions

1. **Exact 1796 membership.** A diplomatic transcription of Lalande’s circumpolar table is needed to settle the nine-versus-ten count and identify every entry in modern catalogues.

2. **Exact map-to-sky registration.** High-resolution astrometric registration of Fortin 1795, Goldbach 1799 and Bode 1801 would distinguish stars merely plotted inside the artwork from stars intentionally assigned to the constellation.

3. **Bode’s exclusions.** Modern accounts say he reduced the area, but a star-by-star comparison should identify precisely which Lalande stars he returned to Boötes, Draco or Hercules.

4. **First printed use of “Quadrantids.”** The observations of 1825, 1835 and 1838 and the recurrence arguments of 1839 are traceable, but the first publication using the English or Latinized shower name itself requires a systematic nineteenth-century periodical search.

5. **Chinese identifications.** Exact matching of the faint constituent stars to Qing and earlier Chinese catalogues requires a specialist catalogue such as Sun and Kistemaker or a modern critical reconstruction, not name matching from popular lists.

6. **Arabic stellar identifications.** A star-by-star comparison with critical editions of al-Ṣūfī and Bedouin *anwāʾ* traditions might identify individual faint stars, but no collective equivalent of Quadrans should be expected.

7. **The “830 stars in 109 groups” reference.** Its originating catalogue or collection remains unidentified.

8. **2003 EH1’s history.** Better orbital constraints and stream modelling may clarify whether it was the historical comet C/1490 Y1, a fragment of the same progenitor, or an unrelated but dynamically associated remnant.
