# Machina Electrica: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

### Status

**Documented fact.** **Machina Electrica is abolished—not an IAU constellation.** Its former sky area is now divided chiefly between **Fornax** and **Sculptor**. It has no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, boundary, or protected star nomenclature.

The institutional chronology needs precision:

- In **Rome in 1922**, the International Astronomical Union’s Commission on Notations adopted the canonical list and three-letter abbreviations of the “88 principal constellations.” Machina Electrica was not included. Strictly speaking, this omission—not a resolution explicitly naming and “abolishing” the machine—ended any prospect of official status.
- In **Cambridge in 1925**, Eugène Delporte was commissioned to draw exact boundaries.
- Those boundaries were approved at **Leiden in 1928**.
- Delporte published them, for equator and equinox **B1875.0**, in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* in **1930**. Every point formerly occupied by Bode’s machine thereby became formally part of Fornax or Sculptor. [IAU history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), [Delporte, 1930](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ), [original 1922 list and its anomaly](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html).

Accordingly, “abolished by the IAU in 1922” is a defensible shorthand, but “omitted from the canonical list in 1922; its replacement boundaries approved in 1928 and published in 1930” is the documented formulation.

### Names

| English | **Electrical Machine**, **Electric Generator**, occasionally **Electricity Generator** | Nineteenth- and modern descriptive translations. |

| Italian | **Macchina Elettrica** | Modern translation. |
| Dutch | **Elektriseermachine / elektrische machine** | Modern translation, also the ordinary historical name for a frictional electrostatic generator. |
| Modern German | **Elektrisiermaschine** | More exact than *elektrische Maschine*, since it distinguishes an electrostatic friction machine from a motor or dynamo. |
| Modern Russian | **Электрическая Машина** | Translation found in modern historical-constellation lists. |
| Proposed abbreviations | **Mac**, **MaE** | Retrospective, non-IAU forms used by modern obsolete-constellation compilers. They have no official standing. |

**Etymology.** Latin *machina* comes through Greek μηχανή, *mēkhanē*, “device, contrivance, machine.” *Electrica* derives from Neo-Latin *electricus*, coined from Greek ἤλεκτρον, *ēlektron*, “amber,” because rubbed amber displays static attraction. The name therefore meant approximately “the apparatus producing electrical effects.”

### No ancient name

**Documented negative finding.** There is no Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Indian, or medieval Latin constellation called Machina Electrica. It is not an ancient figure translated into Latin. Bode explicitly described it as a **new constellation** and explained his reason for inventing it.

Consequently:

- it has no classical catasterism;
- no passage about it exists in Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Aratus, Germanicus, Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, Pausanias, or the ancient scholia;
- it has no inherited al-Sufi name;
- no ancient myth belongs to the electrical apparatus;
- ancient or medieval names encountered in the same coordinates refer to larger neighboring figures, unformed stars, or wholly different asterisms.

### Location

Bode placed it:

- south of the central part of **Cetus**;
- east of **Apparatus Sculptoris**, the Sculptor’s Workshop;
- west or northwest of **Fornax Chemica/Apparatus Chemicus**, the Chemical Furnace;
- north of **Phoenix**.

Bode’s own description says:

> *Da der wichtigen Erfindung der Electrizität bisher noch kein Sternen-Monument geweihet war, so habe ich deshalb dieses neue Sternbild ostwärts bey der Bildhauerwerkstadt an den Himmel gebracht, und zur Formirung desselben mit letzterm einige Veränderung getroffen.*

A close translation is:

> “Since no stellar monument had yet been dedicated to the important invention of electricity, I therefore placed this new constellation in the sky east of the Sculptor’s Workshop, and in forming it made some alterations to the latter.”

This is preserved in the companion *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, printed at Berlin in 1801 in parallel German and French. The volume describes and catalogs 17,240 stars, doubles, nebulae, and clusters. [Bibliographic record and digital copy](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036).

### Historical and modern boundaries

Machina Electrica never possessed boundaries in the modern legal sense. Before Delporte, a “constellation” could mean a picture, a catalog heading, or a loosely allotted tract whose margins varied among cartographers.

- **Ptolemy, second century CE:** no Fornax, Sculptor, or Machina Electrica. Stars in this southern region were either attached to Eridanus or treated as unformed stars outside a figure.
- **Bayer, 1603:** no machine; the region remained outside his classical figures, adjoining Eridanus and Cetus.
- **Schiller, 1627:** no machine; he worked from the Bayer-era sky.
- **Hevelius, 1690:** no machine.
- **Lacaille, 1750s/1763:** organized the region into his new **le Fourneau**, later *Fornax Chemica*, and **l’Atelier du Sculpteur**, later *Apparatus Sculptoris*.
- **Bode, 1782:** in *Vorstellung der Gestirne*, the relevant stars remained with or between Lacaille’s Sculptor and Furnace. [Heidelberg’s 1782 atlas presentation](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/).
- **Bode, 1801:** cut into the eastern side of Sculptor and, apparently, part of Fornax to create Machina Electrica.
- **Argelander, 1843:** retained its name and chart region in *Neue Uranometrie/Uranometria Nova*, but did not draw the elaborate apparatus.
- **Late nineteenth century:** atlas boundaries and membership varied because the figure was increasingly omitted.
- **IAU/Delporte, 1922–1930:** the former area was divided between Fornax and Sculptor.

The popular statement that Bode took the figure from “southern Cetus” is misleading. The machine lay **south of Cetus**, but Bode’s own text says he altered Sculptor. The recognizable principal stars now lie in Sculptor and Fornax, not Cetus.

## Stars and objects

## Reconstructing Bode’s stars

Bode plotted numerous faint telescopic stars inside the apparatus. Identifying every dot with a modern catalog entry requires measuring Plate XVII against the 1801 catalog; because the constellation had no fixed boundary, later authors do not agree on a complete membership list.

The following identifications are recoverable with reasonable or strong confidence from Bode’s lettering and modern cross-identifications:

| Bode designation | Modern identification | V magnitude, approximately | Notes |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **α Machinae Electricae** | **ε Sculptoris** | 5.3 | Binary; F-type primary. Bode reassigned Lacaille’s ε Scl to his machine. |
| probable **β Machinae Electricae** | **ν Fornacis** | 4.68–4.73 | Brightest reconstructed member, despite not being Bode’s alpha; an α² Canum Venaticorum variable. The exact Bode letter should be checked directly against a high-resolution plate/catalog collation. |
| **γ Machinae Electricae** | **HD 9525 / HIP 7213** | 5.49 | Modern Sculptor; K giant. |
| **δ Machinae Electricae** | **HD 10538 / HIP 7941** | 5.70 | Modern Sculptor; A-type star. |
| **ε Machinae Electricae** | **HD 10481 / HIP 7886** | 6.20 | Modern Sculptor; F-type star. |
| **ζ Machinae Electricae** | **σ Sculptoris** | about 5.5 | Binary or multiple system; formerly Lacaille’s σ Scl. |
| **ι Machinae Electricae** | **τ Sculptoris** | about 5.7–6.1 | Long-period visual binary. |
| **μ Machinae Electricae** | **π Sculptoris** | 5.25 | Orange K giant/bright giant; candidate astrometric binary. |
| unconfirmed letter in the machine | **μ Fornacis** | 5.27 | A0 V rapid rotator, possible debris-disk infrared excess. Clearly associated with the reconstructed figure by modern investigators, but the Bode-letter assignment needs direct plate confirmation. |

The historical cross-identifications for ε Scl, π Scl, σ Scl, τ Scl, HD 9525, HD 10538, and HD 10481 are visible in the notes to the modern [Sculptor star table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stars_in_Sculptor). That compilation is secondary; Bode’s plate and catalog remain the controlling evidence.

### Brightest star

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The brightest readily identified member was **ν Fornacis**, around magnitude 4.7, not α Machinae Electricae. [Modern data for ν Fornacis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nu_Fornacis).

It has no traditional proper name. Its identifiers include ν For, HD 12767, and HIP 9677. It is a chemically peculiar, silicon-rich late-B star and an α² CVn rotational variable.

This illustrates why Bayer letters should not automatically be read as a strict brightness ranking. Bode’s alpha was ε Sculptoris, around magnitude 5.3.

### Other conspicuous members

- **π Sculptoris / μ Machinae Electricae** is a K1 II/III star near magnitude 5.25. It has no traditional Arabic or Greek proper name.
- **μ Fornacis** is an A0 V star around magnitude 5.27, HD 13709/HIP 10320. It has no traditional proper name. [Modern data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu_Fornacis).
- **ε Sculptoris / α Machinae Electricae** is a multiple-star system around magnitude 5.3. It has no traditional proper name.
- **τ Sculptoris / ι Machinae Electricae** is a long-period visual binary. [Modern account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau_Sculptoris).
- **σ Sculptoris / ζ Machinae Electricae** has a history of uncertain variability and chemical classification. Modern studies have questioned older claims that it is an Ap/Am or α² CVn variable.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

There are **no official Bayer letters “of Machina Electrica”** today. Bode’s Greek-letter system was extinguished with the figure. The modern letters belong to Fornax and Sculptor.

There are generally no Flamsteed numbers for these central stars. Flamsteed’s catalog was based on observations from Greenwich and did not systematically number the far-southern Lacaille constellations. Alpha Fornacis, farther east, was once **12 Eridani**, but it was not a principal star of Bode’s machine.

### Internal asterism

No independent traditional asterism inside Machina Electrica is securely recorded. The visual “asterism” was the entire apparatus: glass disk, crank, conductors, experimental table, and associated electrical equipment.

Modern observers sometimes reduce it to a quadrilateral made from ν For, μ For, π Scl, and ε Scl. That is a **modern reconstruction**, not an ancient or IAU-defined pattern.

## Deep-sky objects

### Objects named after Machina Electrica

**Documented negative finding.**

- No Messier object bears its name.
- No NGC or IC object is officially named for it.
- No recognized nebula, open cluster, globular cluster, galaxy, galaxy group, or planetary nebula has “Machina Electrica” as an official astronomical name.
- There is no “Machina Electrica Nebula,” “Electrical Machine Cluster,” or similarly inherited object-name.

### NGC 613

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The most important deep-sky object plausibly inside Bode’s pictured tract is **NGC 613**, at J2000 RA 01h 34m, Dec −29° 25′. It is now in Sculptor.

William Herschel discovered it on **9 December 1798**, shortly before Bode’s atlas appeared, and James Dunlop independently recorded it in 1826. It is a barred spiral galaxy with an active, star-forming nuclear region. [Historical identification](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/ngc6.htm), [modern overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_613).

Whether Bode plotted Herschel’s nebula as part of the machine must be answered from a close reading of Plate XVII and the companion catalog. Its coordinates place it amid the reconstructed star field, but because Bode left no polygonal boundary, “inside Machina Electrica” remains a cartographic reconstruction.

### SN 2016gkg

A supernova, **SN 2016gkg**, occurred in NGC 613. On 20 September 2016, Argentine amateur astronomer Víctor Buso happened to image the supernova during its exceptionally early rise, yielding rare observations of shock breakout/cooling immediately after explosion.

Thus one can say:

- **documented fact:** SN 2016gkg occurred in NGC 613;
- **scholarly reconstruction:** its position falls in or very close to the territory depicted as Machina Electrica by Bode;
- **not historical usage:** astronomers never cataloged it as a “nova in Machina Electrica,” because the old constellation had been obsolete for generations.

### R Sculptoris

**Probable reconstructed member.** **R Sculptoris**, a carbon-rich semiregular variable at RA 01h 27m, Dec −32° 33′, lies near the heart of the old field. Its circumstellar gas forms a remarkable spiral produced by interaction with a binary companion.

It was not one of the obvious naked-eye machine stars and was not known as an object “of Machina Electrica.” Its present name is governed by the variable-star sequence of Sculptor.

## Variables, novae, and supernovae

Likely or possible inhabitants of the historical tract include:

- **ν Fornacis**, an α² CVn rotational variable;
- **R Sculptoris**, carbon-star semiregular variable;
- **ε Sculptoris**, suspected or low-amplitude variable in some catalogs;
- **σ Sculptoris**, historically suspected of chemical peculiarity and rotational variation, with later work casting doubt on the claim;
- **SN 2016gkg** in NGC 613, if NGC 613 is accepted as falling within the reconstructed figure.

No securely documented historical naked-eye nova is known as “Nova Machinae Electricae.” No ancient Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Arabic, or European guest-star notice has been convincingly tied specifically to Bode’s later outline.

## Meteor showers

**Documented negative finding.** There is no recognized **Machina Electricid**, **Machinid**, or equivalent meteor shower. Modern shower names use the IAU constellation containing the radiant. Searches of historical and modern literature did not recover a securely established shower named after Bode’s machine.

This contrasts with the Quadrantids, whose name preserves the abolished Quadrans Muralis.

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity to the seventeenth century: no machine

#### Second millennium BCE to antiquity

The relevant sky was visible low in the south from Mesopotamia and Egypt, but no securely identified Babylonian or Egyptian figure coincides with Bode’s compact electrical-machine outline.

**Scholarly caution.** Reconstructions of Babylonian or Egyptian constellations should not be projected onto eighteenth-century European figures merely because their areas overlap. No cuneiform star list or Egyptian astronomical ceiling names an electrostatic apparatus or a matching compact group here.

#### ca. 150 CE — Ptolemy

Ptolemy’s *Almagest* had no Sculptor, Fornax, or Machina Electrica. The region lay beyond the formed stars of Cetus and near the southern course of Eridanus. Some stars could be “unformed”—cataloged relative to a constellation without being incorporated into its picture.

There are therefore no Ptolemaic astrological natures assigned to “stars of Machina Electrica.”

#### ca. 964 — al-Sufi

Al-Sufi’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita*, the *Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars*, revised Ptolemy’s catalog and drawings but did not invent this constellation. Later writers report that some stars now in Sculptor were discussed among stars south of Eridanus or within a large southern triangle involving β Ceti, Fomalhaut, and α Phoenicis. [Historical discussion](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bb/Astronomical_curiosities_-_facts_and_fallacies_%28IA_astronomicalcuri00gorerich%29.pdf).

No individual al-Sufi name can presently be attached securely to ν For, μ For, π Scl, or ε Scl.

#### 1515 — Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s northern and southern celestial maps transmitted the classical/Ptolemaic sky. They contain no Machina Electrica and no corresponding machine icon.

#### 1603 — Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* likewise contains no electrical machine. Its absence is chronological as well as geographical: the plate-type friction machine represented by Bode belonged to the technological world of the eighteenth century.

#### 1627 — Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, published at Augsburg with assistance from Bayer and engravings by Lucas Kilian, replaced pagan constellations with Christian figures. Cetus became **Saints Joachim and Anne**, parents of the Virgin. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/).

However, the stars later selected for Machina Electrica were not yet a defined constellation. It is therefore inaccurate to claim that Schiller assigned Machina Electrica itself to a particular saint. At most, its northern vicinity fell near his transformed Cetus/Joachim-and-Anne field.

#### 1690 — Hevelius

Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* contains no machine. He could not have transmitted Bode’s figure.

### Lacaille prepares the field

#### 1751–1756 — Lacaille’s Cape observations

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope and created constellations honoring scientific and artistic instruments. In 1756 he published a southern planisphere containing, among others:

- **le Fourneau**, the furnace;
- **l’Atelier du Sculpteur**, the sculptor’s workshop.

Their Latinized forms became *Fornax Chemica* and *Apparatus Sculptoris*. Lacaille’s instrument constellations provided the direct conceptual model for Bode’s later electrical apparatus.

#### 1763 — Lacaille’s catalog published

Lacaille’s *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* was published posthumously. His stars in Sculptor received Greek letters, some of which Bode later displaced into Machina Electrica.

### The electrical context

#### 1745–1746 — Leyden jar

The Leyden jar, an early capacitor, was developed independently in work associated with Ewald Georg von Kleist and Pieter van Musschenbroek’s Leiden circle. It made the accumulation and spectacular discharge of static electricity possible.

#### 1751–1752 — Franklin

Benjamin Franklin’s electrical writings and lightning experiments made the identity of laboratory sparks and atmospheric electricity a defining Enlightenment subject. Franklin’s kite report appeared in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* on 19 October 1752 and was read to the Royal Society on 21 December. The surviving documentary situation is more complex than the schoolbook legend: Franklin proposed the experiment and reported it, while Thomas-François Dalibard successfully performed Franklin’s pointed-rod test in France earlier in 1752. [Franklin Papers](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-04-02-0135), [Royal Society manuscript record](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?AddBasket=L%26P%2F2%2F343&id=L%26P%2F2%2F343&src=CalmView.Catalog).

#### 1766–1768 — Ramsden’s disk machine

Jesse Ramsden developed a widely imitated plate-glass friction machine. A glass disk rotated between rubbing pads; charge was collected by pointed conductors and could be transferred to a prime conductor or Leyden jar. Nineteenth-century French histories date the decisive substitution of a glass plate for earlier globes or cylinders to about 1768. [Figuier’s historical account](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Les_Merveilles_de_la_science/Machine_%C3%A9lectrique), [Science Museum Ramsden apparatus](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co6994/ramsden-electrical-machine).

#### 1784 — van Marum and Cuthbertson

Martinus van Marum commissioned John Cuthbertson to build the enormous double-disk electrostatic generator at Teylers Museum, Haarlem. It was installed in the Oval Room in December 1784 and was used with batteries of Leyden jars for experiments on sparks, gases, plants, animals, and people. [Teylers Museum object record](https://teylersmuseum.nl/nl/ontdek/topstukken/topstukken/instrumenten/fk-0508/grote-elektriseermachine-met-leidse-flessen), [museum history](https://teylersmuseum.nl/en/discover/origin/famous-names/martinus-van-marum).

This instrument embodies the same Enlightenment culture, but it is not identical in construction to Bode’s drawing.

### Bode’s invention

#### 1782 — no Machina Electrica yet

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln* showed the stars below Cetus assigned to or lying between the Chemical Furnace and Sculptor’s Workshop. The machine was absent. This falsifies any claim that it originated in Bode’s first atlas of 1782.

#### 1794–1801 — preparation of *Uranographia*

Bode began work on the great atlas in the 1790s. The twenty enormous copperplates were financed under the patronage of Friedrich von Hahn and engraved by Daniel Berger and assistants. The work was issued in installments, reportedly beginning in 1797 and concluding in 1801. [Utrecht bibliographic history](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/handle/1874/33187).

#### 1798

William Herschel discovered NGC 613 in the region on 9 December 1798.

A later American handbook incorrectly dated Machina Electrica to “Bode, 1798.” No securely located Bode publication from 1798 introducing the figure has been found. The date may reflect the atlas’s production period rather than a first published plate.

#### 1800 — conventional date of invention

Modern histories usually date the constellation to **1800**. This is plausible as the year Bode designed or named the figure during completion of the atlas.

**Evidence level:** traditional/scholarly dating rather than a separately identified 1800 paper. The first indisputable published witness located here is the 1801 atlas and companion description.

#### 1800 — Volta’s pile

On 20 March 1800 Alessandro Volta wrote to Royal Society president Joseph Banks describing his pile of alternating metals and electrolyte. The paper was read on 26 June and published in *Philosophical Transactions*, volume 90, pp. 403–431. [Royal Society paper record](https://explore.openaire.eu/search/result?pid=10.1098%2Frstl.1800.0018), [contemporary proceedings text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AProceedings_of_the_Royal_Society_of_London_Vol_1.djvu/47).

The coincidence is historically striking: Bode’s constellation is conventionally dated to the same year as the first battery.

But:

- **documented fact:** Volta announced the pile in 1800;
- **documented fact:** Bode’s atlas appeared in 1801;
- **unverified claim:** Bode invented Machina Electrica specifically to honor Volta or the pile;
- **contrary visual evidence:** Bode drew a frictional plate generator and Leyden-jar apparatus, not a voltaic pile;
- **best reconstruction:** the constellation honored experimental electricity generally, especially the electrostatic machine already famous before Volta.

#### 1801 — first secure publication

Bode published *Joannis Elerti Bode Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio…* at Berlin. It contained twenty huge copperplates and more than 17,000 plotted objects. Plate XVII depicts Machina Electrica south of Cetus between the altered Workshop and Furnace. [ETH digital atlas](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [Marseille digital copy](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035), [University of Oklahoma exhibition](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/map-heavens.html).

The separate companion, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, supplies Bode’s explicit rationale quoted above.

### Nineteenth-century reception

#### 1822 — Alexander Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson included Machina Electrica on Plate XXIII of *A Celestial Atlas*, London, 1822, alongside Cetus, Sculptor, and Fornax. His machine is based on Bode but graphically simplified or “updated.” [David Rumsey collection record](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/view/all/who/T.%2BCadell/N.%2BHailes/Neele%2B%26%2BSon/Jamieson%2C%2BAlexander/).

#### 1824 — Elijah H. Burritt/Green-era American charts

Early American popular atlases derived heavily from European models and sometimes retained Bode’s obsolete figures. An 1824 geographic description placed the machine “between the Phoenix and the Sea Monster.”

The precise attribution among Elijah H. Burritt, Sidney E. Morse, Richard Green, and associated publishers varies by edition. This should not be collapsed into a claim that Burritt invented the figure.

#### 1824–1825 — *Urania’s Mirror*

The anonymous set *Urania’s Mirror; or, a View of the Heavens*, first advertised in November 1824 and commonly dated 1825, included Machina Electrica on **Card 28** with Cetus, Eridanus, Sculptor, Fornax, and Psalterium Georgii.

The cards were engraved by Sidney Hall and accompanied by Jehoshaphat Aspin’s explanatory book. They closely followed Jamieson. The stars were pierced so that they shone when a card was held to light. [Google Arts & Culture catalog](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/urania-s-mirror-or-a-view-of-the-heavens/tgHfIK26Sl7b4w?hl=en).

Claims that Hall “invented” Machina Electrica are false; his image is a derivative witness more than two decades after Bode.

#### 1835 — Hoffmann

C. F. Hoffmann’s *Himmelsatlas für Freunde und Liebhaber der Sternkunde* at Stuttgart retained and illustrated the figure. [Atlas Coelestis obsolete-constellation gallery](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/desuete%20bode.htm).

#### 1843 — Argelander

Friedrich Wilhelm August Argelander’s *Neue Uranometrie* included the name and chart tract. Its restrained scientific style omitted Bode’s elaborate apparatus while retaining the constellation as a positional category.

This is significant: the constellation briefly survived the transition from pictorial atlas to practical uranometry.

#### 1845 — Kendall

An American educational description placed it “under the Whale, and west of the Chemical Apparatus.” Such descriptions show that the figure was taught, but mainly through books copying Bode-derived atlases.

#### 1850s — movable planispheres and Bouvier

Aspin/Griffith/Wyld-style movable planispheres depicted the hand-operated generator. Hannah M. Bouvier’s *Familiar Astronomy* (1858) located it immediately south of Cetus and north of Phoenix and noted that it contained no bright stars.

#### 1877 — George F. Chambers

Chambers’s *A Handbook of Descriptive Astronomy* still listed Machina Electrica and attributed it to Bode’s maps. This is among its last appearances in a serious general astronomical handbook.

#### 1879

W. H. Rosser listed it among proposed constellations “now generally discarded.” [Rosser text](https://www.microscopemuseum.eu/bookstelescopes/W_Rosser_1879_The_stars_and_constellations_how_and_when_to_find_and_tell_them.pdf).

#### 1889/1899 — Richard Hinckley Allen

Allen wrote that it lay south of central Cetus and was “now generally omitted from the maps and catalogues.” The work first appeared in 1899 as *Star-Names and Their Meanings*; later editions are commonly cited as *Star Names: Their Lore and Meaning*. [Allen text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/home.html).

#### 1892 — Poole Brothers

*Poole Bros.’ Celestial Handbook* retained it in a chronological table, but assigned “Bode, 1798 A.D.” That date is not supported by an identified 1798 Bode publication and appears to be an error or production-date inference.

### Twentieth century to present

#### 1922–1930 — removal from official astronomy

The IAU standardized the accepted constellations and excluded the machine. Delporte’s boundaries returned its territory to Fornax and Sculptor.

#### 1923 — astrological afterlife

Vivian E. Robson’s *The Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology* assigned the obsolete constellation an influence: a scientific spirit, intellectual advancement beyond one’s time, and peculiar or idealistic views.

This is:

- **documented tradition:** Robson printed it;
- **modern invention:** the interpretation postdates Bode by more than a century;
- **not ancient astrology:** it is absent from Ptolemy, Dorotheus, Manilius, medieval Arabic astrology, and Renaissance fixed-star manuals;
- **unsupported empirically:** no historical observational evidence is offered.

#### Late twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Machina Electrica survives in histories of obsolete constellations, reproductions of Bode and *Urania’s Mirror*, planetarium software add-ons, observing columns, podcasts, and modern astrology sites. It has not been restored by the IAU.

## Invention and meaning

## What Bode honored

**Documented fact.** Bode said he wished to erect a “stellar monument” to the important invention of electricity. He did not name an individual inventor, sovereign, patron, or voyage.

Therefore the figure honored:

- electricity as a field of experimental natural philosophy;
- the laboratory apparatus through which electrical effects were generated;
- by implication, the Enlightenment culture of scientific instruments already represented by Lacaille’s telescope, microscope, air pump, furnace, clock, compass, and workshop tools.

It did **not demonstrably** honor:

- Benjamin Franklin personally;
- Alessandro Volta personally;
- Jesse Ramsden personally;
- Martinus van Marum personally;
- the invention of the electric motor;
- a dynamo or electromagnetic generator;
- a king or patron.

## What machine did Bode depict?

The engraving shows a hand-operated **frictional electrostatic generator**, with a rotating glass disk, collecting/prime conductor, and accessory apparatus including a Leyden jar or jars.

### Ramsden identification

**Strong scholarly reconstruction.** Its single plate, crank, friction pads, and conductor resemble the Ramsden class of plate machine, developed in the 1760s and widely copied by 1800.

Calling it “Ramsden’s machine” is reasonable as a type-identification. It is not yet demonstrated that Bode copied a particular Ramsden instrument or illustration.

### Van Marum identification

**Disputed/weak reconstruction.** Van Marum’s famous generator is relevant context but was a very large **double-disk** installation designed by Van Marum and built by John Cuthbertson. Bode’s compact apparatus does not reproduce that machine exactly.

The defensible wording is: “a Ramsden-type plate friction machine, belonging to the same experimental culture as Van Marum’s generator.” Directly calling Bode’s picture “van Marum’s machine” overstates the evidence.

### Leyden jar

The apparatus beside the generator is conventionally identified as a Leyden jar or electrical storage equipment. This is mechanically plausible and consistent with contemporary practice. High-resolution iconographic comparison with Bode’s artistic source would be needed to identify the exact model.

### Later “improvements”

Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror* altered the design. Some modern writers describe these versions as resembling later influence machines. This is an art-historical observation, not evidence that Bode intended post-1801 electromagnetic technology.

## Myths and legends

### Classical myth

**None.** Machina Electrica is a modern instrument constellation. No Greek or Roman god placed it in the sky, and it never acts in an ancient myth.

Any account that supplies it with an Eratosthenian, Hyginian, Ovidian, or Ptolemaic myth is a modern fabrication or confusion with Cetus, Eridanus, Phoenix, Fornax, or Sculptor.

### Enlightenment foundation story

Bode’s own statement functions as the figure’s sole authentic “catasterism”:

1. electricity was an important invention;
2. no stellar monument yet honored it;
3. Bode therefore placed one east of Sculptor;
4. he altered Sculptor to make room.

This is not myth in the classical sense, but it consciously imitates the commemorative logic of catasterism: a terrestrial achievement receives immortality among the stars.

### Franklin story

**Popular tradition, partly misleading.** Modern accounts often place Franklin, his kite, and the key behind the constellation. Franklin unquestionably helped make electricity emblematic of the Enlightenment, but Bode did not mention him.

The schoolbook claim that Franklin “discovered electricity” is false. Electrical phenomena and machines were studied long before him. Franklin investigated electrical charge and lightning and designed decisive experiments.

### Volta story

**Modern associative reading.** Because the pile was announced in 1800, the same conventional year assigned to Bode’s invention, the coincidence is evocative. Yet Bode’s device is not a pile, and no evidence located here shows that news of Volta determined the constellation’s design.

### “First electrical generator” story

**Terminologically disputed.** Popular summaries say Machina Electrica honored “the first electric generator.” The pictured device did generate static charge, so “electrostatic generator” is correct. But modern readers may understand “generator” as a dynamo producing continuous electromagnetic current, which did not yet exist. “Frictional electrostatic machine” is historically safer.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Arabic and al-Sufi

Al-Sufi followed the Ptolemaic constellation system. The relevant stars were not an Arabic equivalent of Machina Electrica.

Some later historical writers place stars of modern Sculptor among al-Sufi’s stars around southern Eridanus, within a large triangle bounded approximately by β Ceti, Fomalhaut, and α Phoenicis. This is a positional reconstruction.

No secure Bedouin asterism has been identified specifically with the four principal reconstructed machine stars. Their faintness and low altitude from much of Arabia make an elaborate traditional group less likely, but that is inference, not proof of absence.

## Chinese

The western sky region belongs to the **White Tiger of the West** in traditional Chinese uranography.

Most importantly, **ν Fornacis**, the brightest reconstructed machine star, is identified as:

- **天庾一, Tiānyǔ yī**, “First Star of Tiānyǔ”;
- Tiānyǔ 天庾 is translated as “Ricks of Grain,” “Heavenly Granary,” or “Heaven’s Temporary Granary”;
- ω For and β For form the second and third stars.

Thus the Chinese counterpart is not an electrical machine but part of a celestial agricultural store. [Fornax in Chinese astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fornax_in_Chinese_astronomy).

This identification applies securely to ν For. It does not establish that all Bode-machine stars belonged to one Chinese asterism.

## Indian astronomy

No nakṣatra figure equivalent to Machina Electrica has been traced. The region lies far south of the ecliptic; lunar mansions are defined around the Moon’s path and their identifying stars lie elsewhere.

Modern tropical astrology may assign the reconstructed machine stars zodiacal longitudes in Pisces or Aries, but this does not make them parts of the ancient Indian lunar mansions.

## Babylonian lunar mansions and zodiac

The machine lies well south of the ecliptic. Statements that it “spans Pisces and Aries” refer to projecting its stars onto zodiacal longitude, not membership in the zodiacal constellations or Babylonian zodiac figures.

No Babylonian name for this asterism is known.

## Egyptian

No secure equivalent has been identified on Egyptian diagonal-star clocks, coffin lids, Dendera, Esna, or other astronomical ceilings. Jamieson reproduced speculative “Egyptian zodiacs” after early-modern authorities, but these are not evidence for an ancient Machina Electrica.

## Persian

No independent Persian constellation corresponding to the apparatus has been located. Persian astronomical texts using the Ptolemaic tradition would treat the stars under neighboring classical headings or as unformed stars. Modern Persian translation is not evidence of an old Persian asterism.

## Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No biblical, rabbinic, medieval Hebrew, or early Kabbalistic constellation securely maps to Bode’s compact figure.

Claims that the electrical machine belongs to a Hebrew tribe, angel, sefirah, or letter appear to be modern syncretism unless supported by a dated pre-1801 text—which has not been found.

## Julius Schiller’s Christian sky

Because Schiller published in 1627, he could not Christianize a constellation invented around 1800. His replacement of Cetus with **Saints Joachim and Anne** covered the neighboring Ptolemaic figure, not Machina Electrica itself.

Any statement that Machina Electrica “became Joachim and Anne” reverses the chronology.

## Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No historical alchemical or Hermetic interpretation specific to Machina Electrica was found in Bode, his companion volume, the principal nineteenth-century atlases, or standard fixed-star literature.

There is an obvious modern temptation to combine:

- electricity as celestial fire;
- Fornax as the alchemical furnace;
- Sculptor as formative art;
- the nearby Phoenix as death and rebirth.

That combination is a **modern symbolic construction**, not an attested early tradition.

## Astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* contains no Machina Electrica and assigns no planetary nature to its stars under that name.

No star in the reconstructed group is one of the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars. No traditional talisman in the *Picatrix*, Agrippa, or Behenian lapidaries is attached to the machine.

Robson’s 1923 description is the first located explicit astrological “influence” for the constellation and should be treated as a twentieth-century invention.

## Iconography

### Ancient monuments and manuscripts

Machina Electrica cannot appear on:

- the Farnese Atlas;
- the Dendera zodiac;
- Egyptian coffin ceilings;
- Babylonian boundary stones;
- al-Sufi’s medieval manuscripts;
- medieval Aratus manuscripts;
- cathedral zodiac portals;
- Dürer’s 1515 star maps;
- Bayer’s *Uranometria*;
- Hevelius’s atlas.

Claims otherwise are anachronisms.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

The primary image is on **Plate XVII** of *Uranographia*. It shows an eighteenth-century experimental setup occupying the gap below Cetus. The surrounding figures include:

- the belly or lower outline of Cetus above;
- Apparatus Sculptoris to one side;
- Apparatus Chemicus/Fornax to the other;
- Phoenix farther south.

Bode’s image is scientific commemoration presented through the traditional language of constellation personification. Unlike an animal or hero, the apparatus is drawn as a technical still life.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s Plate XXIII follows Bode but redraws and simplifies the machine. His atlas was smaller and intended for practical and educational use, helping disseminate the figure in Britain.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824–1825

Card 28 is the best-known colored image. It is derivative of Jamieson and therefore indirectly of Bode. The machine is rendered more decoratively, with its disk, crank, table, conductors, and experimental accessories clearly visible.

Its perforated stars turned the apparatus into a domestic optical entertainment: when held before a light, its selected stars shone through.

### Hoffmann, Stuttgart, 1835

Hoffmann retained an illustrated machine in a popular German atlas. This is evidence of reception, not independent invention.

### Argelander, Bonn, 1843

Argelander’s *Neue Uranometrie* marked the constellation spatially without Bode’s pictorial elaboration. This is the most scientifically serious stage of its reception.

### Later planispheres

Mid-century movable planispheres occasionally showed a hand-operated generator. Because their designs postdate Bode, technical differences cannot be used to identify what exact machine Bode himself intended.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

No securely documented coin, state medal, cathedral portal, church ceiling, palace ceiling, or traditional calendar has been found depicting Machina Electrica as a constellation.

Electricity and electrostatic experiments certainly appear in later scientific allegory, but visual resemblance alone does not establish a link to Bode’s constellation.

## People, events, and works

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode, director of the Berlin Observatory and editor of the *Berliner Astronomisches Jahrbuch*, created the figure. He is also associated with the popularization of the Titius–Bode planetary-spacing rule and with the naming of Uranus.

Machina Electrica belongs to his effort to assemble an encyclopedic sky combining ancient figures, the southern constellations of navigators and Lacaille, patronal inventions, and modern scientific instruments.

### Friedrich von Hahn

Count Friedrich von Hahn supported the expense of engraving the *Uranographia*. The atlas title acknowledges his financial role. The machine did not honor Hahn personally.

### Daniel Berger

Berger and his workshop engraved Bode’s plates. The precise division between Bode’s technical design and the engraver’s artistic realization is not fully documented for the machine.

### Jesse Ramsden

Ramsden’s plate machine is the closest commonly cited technical model. His role is typological, not proven as a personal dedication.

### Martinus van Marum and John Cuthbertson

Van Marum designed and commissioned, and Cuthbertson constructed, the great Haarlem electrostatic machine. It demonstrates the prestige of such apparatus in Bode’s lifetime, although Bode’s drawing is not an exact portrait of it.

### Benjamin Franklin

Franklin’s experiments made electricity and lightning internationally famous. His cultural relevance is strong; a direct dedicatory link is unproved.

### Alessandro Volta

Volta’s pile dates to 1800. It provides a remarkable chronological counterpoint, but the pictured apparatus is electrostatic, not voltaic.

### William Herschel

Herschel discovered NGC 613 in 1798, in the tract Bode soon depicted as the machine. Whether Bode entered the nebula on his plate or catalog under Machina Electrica requires further collation.

### Víctor Buso

Buso’s 2016 imaging of SN 2016gkg in NGC 613 connects a modern transient to the reconstructed old field. This is a retrospective positional association, not a revival of the constellation.

### Navigation

No documented explorer or navigator is known to have steered specifically by Machina Electrica. Its stars are faint and the figure was introduced after the great age of European oceanic charting. Navigators used brighter stars such as Fomalhaut, Achernar, Canopus, and stars of Eridanus and Phoenix.

### Literature, music, ships, missions, and buildings

No major poem, opera, symphony, ship, spacecraft, astronomical mission, or building securely named specifically for the obsolete constellation was found.

Searches are complicated because *machina electrica* was ordinary Latin for electrical apparatus, and “electric machine” is a generic technological phrase. Hits must not be assumed to commemorate Bode.

### “Bode collection on chain: 830 stars in 109 groups”

**Unverified claim.** No authoritative astronomical, museum, bibliographic, or blockchain source was located for a “Bode collection on chain” containing exactly **830 stars in 109 groups**. Those numbers do not describe Bode’s *Uranographia*, which plotted more than 17,000 objects.

The phrase may refer to a contemporary digital collection, a private dataset, or metadata not indexed publicly. It should not be incorporated into the history of Machina Electrica without a contract address, collection URL, or catalog record.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was the constellation invented in 1798, 1800, or 1801?

- **1801:** first securely documented publication in *Uranographia* and its companion.
- **1800:** conventional date given by Allen and many modern historians, plausibly the design year.
- **1798:** appears in a later chronological table but lacks an identified Bode source.

**Conclusion:** “devised around 1800 and first securely published in 1801” is best supported.

### 2. Was it created from Cetus?

Some French and Spanish summaries say it was formed from southern Cetus.

Bode’s own text says he placed it east of Sculptor and altered the Sculptor’s Workshop. Modern cross-identifications put its principal stars in Sculptor and Fornax.

**Conclusion:** “south of Cetus” describes location; “cut from Cetus” is poorly supported.

### 3. Did Bode borrow from Sculptor alone or from Sculptor and Fornax?

Bode explicitly mentions changing Sculptor. Modern reconstruction also includes ν and μ Fornacis.

**Conclusion:** the image primarily displaced eastern Sculptor, while its broader nineteenth-century tract or selected stars also encroached on Fornax.

### 4. Ramsden or van Marum?

- Ramsden-type identification fits the single rotating plate and compact form.
- Van Marum’s famous Haarlem generator provides cultural context but was a massive double-disk machine.

**Conclusion:** “Ramsden-type friction machine” is strongest; “van Marum’s generator” is unproved.

### 5. Did it depict a dynamo, battery, or motor?

No. Dynamos and practical electric motors are later electromagnetic devices. Bode’s picture shows a frictional electrostatic machine.

Calling it an “electric generator” is acceptable only if “electrostatic” is added.

### 6. Did it commemorate Volta’s pile?

The dates coincide, but the apparatus differs and Bode did not name Volta.

**Conclusion:** attractive modern association; no direct evidence.

### 7. Was it popular?

Sources disagree in tone:

- some modern summaries call it “somewhat popular” because it appeared in several nineteenth-century charts;
- contemporary reception shows it never achieved universal professional acceptance;
- by the late nineteenth century it was generally omitted.

Both can be true: it enjoyed **moderate cartographic circulation** through Bode, Jamieson, *Urania’s Mirror*, Hoffmann, and Argelander, but never became deeply rooted or indispensable.

### 8. “Abolished in 1922” versus “abolished in 1930”

Neither date represents a resolution saying “Machina Electrica is abolished.”

- 1922 excluded it from the canonical list;
- 1930 published the boundaries that allocated every coordinate to accepted constellations.

The two dates describe different stages.

### 9. Abbreviation Mac or MaE

Both occur in modern retrospective sources. Neither is IAU-sanctioned. Using **Mac** can also invite confusion with no official constellation but resembles ordinary three-letter practice; **MaE** is more visibly antiquarian.

### 10. Brightest star and the alpha designation

ν Fornacis is brighter than Bode’s α Machinae Electricae, ε Sculptoris. Statements that α was necessarily brightest impose a rule Bayer and later cartographers did not consistently follow.

### 11. Proper names

Modern websites sometimes imply that every prominent constellation star should possess an Arabic name. The recovered principal stars of Machina Electrica have no securely established traditional proper names. Greek letters, HD/HIP numbers, and modern constellation assignments are catalog designations, not ancient names.

### 12. Astrological antiquity

Robson’s interpretation is sometimes repeated without its date and may appear “traditional.” It is not ancient. No pre-1801 astrology could mention a constellation that did not yet exist.

### 13. Hoaxes

The dedicated searches found no famous historical hoax centered on Machina Electrica. The recurrent problems are instead:

- anachronistic pictures of dynamos or motors;
- claims that Franklin “discovered electricity” and was directly honored;
- overconfident identification with van Marum’s machine;
- treating 1922 as an explicit abolition decree;
- invented ancient or occult pedigrees;
- confusion between its position south of Cetus and star membership in Cetus.

## Open questions

1. **Exact star roster.** A definitive concordance should transcribe every star under Bode’s “Machina Electrica” catalog heading, including his 1800 coordinates and magnitudes, then precess them and cross-match against Gaia/HIP/HD identifiers.

2. **The beta and later Greek letters.** Several modern cross-identifications are secure, but ν and μ Fornacis should be checked directly against the engraved Greek letters and catalog sequence before assigning Bode designations without qualification.

3. **NGC 613 on Plate XVII.** Its position strongly suggests inclusion in the old tract. A high-resolution plate/catalog examination is needed to establish whether Bode plotted Herschel’s 1798 discovery and under which heading.

4. **Bode’s artistic source.** No documentary source has yet been located showing whether Berger copied a named Ramsden instrument, a German instrument-maker’s catalog, a textbook engraving, or a generalized laboratory machine.

5. **First use in 1800.** The conventional date needs a dated Bode manuscript, proof sheet, installment wrapper, announcement, or journal notice to distinguish conception from publication.

6. **French companion wording.** Bode’s parallel French paragraph should be transcribed exactly from the 1801 companion to determine whether it nuances the German “Erfindung der Electrizität.”

7. **Atlas survival after 1850.** A census of every European and American atlas could quantify rather than merely characterize its decline.

8. **Non-Western star identifications.** ν For’s Chinese identity is secure, but a star-by-star comparison with Arabic indigenous asterisms, the *Almagest*, al-Sufi manuscripts, Persian zijes, and southern Chinese additions remains to be completed.

9. **The alleged on-chain Bode collection.** A URL or blockchain contract is required before the “830 stars in 109 groups” claim can be assessed.
