# Lupus: Research Dossier

## Status and evidentiary key

**Status: kept.** Lupus was included among the 88 constellations adopted by the International Astronomical Union at Rome in 1922. Its abbreviation is **Lup**, its Latin genitive **Lupi**, and its official boundaries are those prepared by Eugène Delporte and published for the IAU in 1930. Nothing was “abolished” or redistributed wholesale: every point within those boundaries remains Lupus. The present constellation covers about **333.7 square degrees**, ranking forty-sixth in area. [IAU constellation history and table](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [Delporte, 1930](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ).

Since this dossier must distinguish kinds of evidence, the following labels are used:

- **Documented fact**: directly stated by, depicted in, or securely derived from an identified source.
- **Scholarly reconstruction**: a modern historical or astronomical inference, not an ancient statement.
- **Tradition**: a historically transmitted identification or interpretation whose truth is not independently demonstrable.
- **Disputed**: competing readings or attributions exist.
- **Unverified claim**: repeated in secondary literature but not traced here to adequate primary evidence.
- **Myth**: an ancient or historically transmitted sacred or literary narrative.
- **Legend**: a narrative transmitted as history or folklore without demonstrable historical confirmation.
- **Modern invention**: a post-classical association absent from the ancient constellation tradition.

A major finding should be stated at once:

> **The ancient constellation was not a wolf and had no independent Lycaon catasterism.** Aratus, Hipparchus and Ptolemy called it simply the **Beast**, Greek **Θηρίον, Thērion**. It was held by Centaurus toward Ara. Its specification as a wolf is medieval or Renaissance; its equation with King Lycaon is later still.

A second finding updates a formerly correct commonplace:

> **Lupus now does have a formally named bright star.** On 12 September 2024 the IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Uridim** for α Lupi. Older books saying that Lupus has no named bright star predate that decision. “Men” or “Yang Mun” for α Lupi is an erroneous nineteenth-century transfer from the Chinese South Gate, which belongs to α and ε Centauri. [IAU announcement](https://iau.org/Iau/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx).

A third correction concerns the figure-specific lead about Schiller:

> **Julius Schiller did not replace Lupus with Benjamin.** Plate XLV of his *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* (Augsburg, 1627) replaces the Centaurus–Lupus complex with **the patriarch Jacob/Israel**. Benjamin was an exegetical wolf-association promoted by Petrus Caesius and repeated by R. H. Allen, not Schiller’s actual plate title.

## Basic identification

### Modern position

Lupus lies in the southern celestial hemisphere, chiefly between Scorpius on the west and Centaurus on the east. Its present neighbors are:

- **Libra** to the north;
- **Scorpius** and **Norma** to the west and southwest;
- **Circinus** to the south;
- **Centaurus** to the east;
- **Hydra**, touching at a single northeastern corner.

Its central naked-eye pattern is an irregular assembly of blue-white stars rather than an unmistakable outline. Modern popular line figures differ because the IAU defines Lupus as an **area**, not as one sanctioned connect-the-dots pattern.

Latin *lupus*, “wolf,” is cognate with Greek **λύκος, lykos**, Germanic *wolf*, Sanskrit *vṛka-* and other Indo-European wolf words, though the exact prehistoric sound history is reconstructed rather than documented.

### Bode’s labels and plate

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode included Lupus in:

1. *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln* (Berlin/Stralsund, 1782), his smaller atlas based partly on the reduced French Flamsteed atlas; and
2. *Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio viginti tabulis aeneis incisa* (Berlin, 1801).

In the 1801 *Uranographia*, Lupus appears on **plate XV**, with Sagittarius, Corona Australis, Scorpius, Tubus Astronomicus, Ara, and Norma et Regula. The plate uses the Latin label **Lupus**; Bode’s German explanatory tradition calls it **der Wolf**. The animal is shown as the traditional limp quadruped associated with the Centaur rather than as an autonomous running wolf. An index of the atlas confirms “Ara, Norma et Regula, Lupus, XV.” [Bode atlas index](https://nzdr.ru/data/media/biblio/edu/astro/%21%D0%90%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%BC%D0%B8%D1%8F/1/John%20Chiravalle%2C%20Pattern%20Asterisms%20-%20Springer%20%282006%29%28ISBN%29%28PDF%20%28scan%29%29%20English.pdf); [complete ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148).

Bode’s accompanying *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* catalogued positions for **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae and clusters** over the whole sky. Published descriptions of the atlas also report the inclusion of roughly 2,500 Herschel nebulae. The figure “830 stars in 109 groups” supplied in the brief could not be reconciled with Bode’s printed title, atlas, or accompanying catalogue; it may describe a modern collection or indexing chain rather than Bode’s total astronomical content. It should not be quoted as Bode’s own count without the collection record from which it came.

## Stars and objects

### What stars make the figure?

There is no unique modern official line figure. Historically:

- **Ptolemy** catalogued **19 stars** under *Thērion* in *Almagest* VII–VIII. His anatomical descriptions distribute them through the beast’s head, foreparts, body, feet and tail.
- **Bayer**, in *Uranometria* (1603), depicted the received animal and assigned letters in the broader southern-star tradition.
- **Lacaille** in 1756 applied a much fuller sequence: Greek letters α through ψ and Latin letters, including duplicated letters for close or grouped stars.
- **Bode** added lower-case **d**.
- **Benjamin Gould**, working from Córdoba for the *Uranometria Argentina* (1877/1879), added **ω**, **i**, and **k** and rationalized the southern nomenclature.
- The modern outline most often uses some selection from **α, β, γ, δ, ε, ζ, η, θ, ι, κ, λ, μ, π, ρ, σ** and sometimes **ω Lupi**.

There are about 127 stars within the Delporte area brighter than magnitude 6.5, but that is a boundary count, not a traditional figure.

Most of the brightest stars are members or likely members of the **Scorpius–Centaurus OB association**, especially its **Upper Centaurus–Lupus** subgroup, roughly 15–20 million years old. This physical association is real but much larger than the drawn constellation.

#### The “Men” problem

**Documented error.** R. H. Allen assigned α Lupi the Chinese name **Yang Mun/Men**, “South Gate.” Traditional Chinese **Nánmén** actually consists of α and ε Centauri. α Lupi is **Qíguān shí**, the tenth star of the Imperial Guards. Allen’s mistake propagated through star-name books, planetarium software, astrology pages and online catalogues.

#### Uridim

**Modern official name based on scholarly reconstruction.** The IAU’s 2024 **Uridim** deliberately connects α Lupi with the reconstructed Mesopotamian predecessor UR.IDIM. It does not prove that Babylonian observers singled out α Lupi individually by that name. It is a modern proper-name assignment derived from an ancient constellation name.

### Bayer and Flamsteed nomenclature

Lupus has a rich Bayer-style sequence but few familiar Flamsteed numbers. Flamsteed could not properly observe far-southern Lupus from Greenwich, and the numerical “Flamsteed” designations in the region stem from later publication and southern extensions rather than a clean original numbered sequence comparable to Ursa Major or Taurus.

The historically encountered set includes Greek α–ω, with duplicated κ, ν, ξ, τ, φ and ψ components in older catalogues, and lower-case Latin letters including **a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, k**. Several duplications were later dropped or reassigned. Modern catalogues retain Bayer-style identifiers even where Delporte’s borders make their historical logic obscure.

### Internal and overlapping asterisms

**Western:** No internationally standardized asterism within Lupus rivals the Big Dipper. Amateur charts sometimes call its brighter stars a wolf’s head or torso; these are modern line conventions.

**Chinese traditional sky:**

- **Qíguān 騎官, Imperial Guards**: γ, δ, β, λ, ε, μ, π, ο and α Lupi, plus κ Centauri in common reconstructions.
- **Chēqí 車騎, Chariots and Cavalry**: chiefly ζ, ρ and σ Lupi.
- **Qízhèn Jiāngjūn 騎陣將軍, General of the Mounted Battle Array**: associated with κ¹ Lupi.
- **Zhù 柱, Pillars**: includes ι and τ¹/τ² Lupi.
- **Dùnwán 頓頑**, commonly glossed “Trials” or “the obstinate/criminals”: includes φ¹/φ² and 1 Lupi.
- **Zhènchē 陣車, Battle Chariots**: includes 2 Lupi.

These belong to the eastern Azure Dragon quadrant and span several Chinese lunar mansions; they do not form a Chinese wolf.

**Arabic Bedouin sky:** the most plausible indigenous grouping is **al-Shamārīkh**, the crowded branches or clusters of a date palm, extending across Centaurus–Lupus. A Smithsonian historical study states that Lupus is one of the regions for which no distinct Bedouin animal tradition is known. Thus claims that pre-Islamic Arabs independently called the same stars a leopard, lioness or wolf should be treated cautiously: most such names are scientific translations or late lists.

### Deep-sky objects

Lupus has **no Messier object**.

Important objects within the modern boundaries include:

- **NGC 5824**: a remote, massive globular cluster, historically discovered by James Dunlop and later catalogued by John Herschel.
- **NGC 5986**: a bright globular cluster, discovered by Dunlop in 1826.
- **NGC 5927**: a metal-rich globular cluster near the Galactic plane.
- **NGC 5749**: an open cluster.
- **NGC 5822**: a large open cluster, also associated with Dunlop’s southern observations.
- **NGC 5882**: a bright planetary nebula.
- **IC 4406**, the **Retina Nebula**: a bipolar planetary nebula whose cylindrical or toroidal structure appears rectangular from Earth.
- **NGC 5643**: a barred/intermediate spiral Seyfert galaxy about 40 million light-years away; host of SN 2013aa and SN 2017cbv.
- The **Lupus I–IV molecular-cloud complex**: dark clouds and low-mass star-forming regions, containing T Tauri stars, disks and protostars.
- **Lupus 3**, near HR 5999/6000: one of the best-studied nearby low-mass star-forming clouds.
- The **SN 1006 remnant**, catalogued as **G327.6+14.6** or PKS 1459−41: the shell left by the historical supernova.

### Variables and eruptive stars

- **α Lupi**: β Cephei variable, period about 0.296 day.
- **δ Lupi**: β Cephei variable.
- **γ Lupi**: multiple system with ellipsoidal or related variability.
- **ε Lupi**: a rare magnetic early-B multiple system.
- **σ Lupi**: magnetic early-B star.
- **RU Lupi**: classical T Tauri star with strong accretion and irregular variability.
- **EX Lupi**: prototype of the **EXor** class of young eruptive stars; major recorded outbursts include 1955–56 and 2008. Its name gave the class “EXors.”
- Numerous “dipper” variables in the Lupus clouds show temporary fading caused by circumstellar dust.

### Novae and supernovae

#### SN 1006

**Documented historical transient; physical interpretation reconstructed.** A “guest star” appeared in spring 1006 in the region now assigned to Lupus. Its remnant identifies it as a Type Ia supernova at roughly 7,000–7,500 light-years. Reconstructed peak magnitude is around **−7 to −7.5**, probably making it the brightest stellar event securely recorded in history.

Records include:

- **China:** the *Song Shi* places the guest star south of the lunar mansion Di and records its visibility and auspicious interpretation.
- **Egypt:** the physician and astronomer **ʿAlī ibn Riḍwān**, writing in a commentary on Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*, described a large, intensely bright object, roughly three times Venus in apparent size and bright enough to illuminate the ground.
- **Iraq:** reports survive through Ibn al-Jawzī and later compilers.
- **Yemen:** al-Yamānī and Ibn al-Daybaʿ preserve reports probably dependent on an earlier local source.
- **Iran/Central Asia:** Ibn Sīnā appears to describe a long-lasting, spark-emitting new star in the *Kitāb al-Shifāʾ*; its identification with SN 1006 is persuasive but remains scholarly.
- **Japan:** later chronicles preserve observation of the guest star.
- **St Gall, Switzerland:** the *Annales Sangallenses maiores* report a *nova stella* seen for three months “in the innermost limits of the south.”
- **Benevento/Italy:** monastic chronicle material is plausibly related.

Claims that it cast sharp shadows or could be read by are sometimes reasonable extrapolations but are not uniformly stated by the witnesses. Claims that a Hohokam petroglyph in Arizona depicts SN 1006 are **disputed and presently unsupported**: the archaeological dating and interpretation are not secure.

The radio remnant was linked to the historical event by Douglas Milne and Frank Gardner in 1965; optical filaments were subsequently identified and studied.

#### Extragalactic supernovae

NGC 5643 hosted:

- **SN 2013aa**, Type Ia, discovered by Stuart Parker on 13 February 2013;
- **SN 2017cbv**, Type Ia, discovered by the DLT40 survey on 10 March 2017.

#### Classical novae

- **V407 Lupi / Nova Lupi 2016 / ASASSN-16kt**: discovered 24 September 2016; a very fast nova, peaking near naked-eye magnitude 5.6. The first electronic notice contained badly wrong coordinates, soon corrected—an instructive modern catalogue controversy.
- **V462 Lupi / Nova Lupi 2025 / AT 2025nlr**: discovered by ASAS-SN on 12 June 2025; spectroscopically confirmed as a classical nova and brightened to approximately magnitude 5.5–5.7.

### Meteor showers

Older catalogues and popular guides usually state that Lupus has **no established major meteor shower**.

That requires a 2026 qualification. A weak source named the **ξ²-Lupids**, code **XLU #1100**, was identified in CAMS and Global Meteor Network data and reported from 2024–26 observations, active approximately 27 January–7 February. The investigators described it as meeting criteria for nomination to established status and suggested a relationship to comet C/1979 Y1 (Bradfield) and the July Pegasids. It is therefore a **newly confirmed minor source**, not a traditional conspicuous annual shower.

## Chronology of attestation

### Mesopotamia, second to first millennia BCE

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Cuneiform sources attest **MUL UR.IDIM**, the Mad or Rabid Dog. Lexical material gives *ur-idim* as “mad dog”; the mythical *uridimmu* could be a canine or leonine human hybrid. It is associated in Mesopotamian astral schemes with nearby figures including the Bison-man and solar or divine powers.

Modern researchers have proposed that UR.IDIM, the Bison-man and a neighboring incense-burner or altar supplied a structural predecessor for the Greek Centaurus–Beast–Ara tableau. The juxtaposition is suggestive, but the evidence does not demonstrate direct one-to-one continuity of every star. “Babylonians called Lupus a wolf” is too simple: the name means a mad canine, while the iconographic creature could be composite and lion-like.

No comparably secure Egyptian constellation occupying the identical stars has been recovered. Egyptian decanal astronomy used different groupings; the Dendera zodiac’s Greco-Egyptian imagery is too late to establish an indigenous pharaonic Lupus.

### Eudoxan and Aratean Greek sky, fourth–third centuries BCE

Eudoxus’ lost *Phaenomena* underlies Aratus’ poem, but direct Eudoxan wording for the beast does not survive.

Around 270 BCE, **Aratus, *Phaenomena* 436–442**, gives the earliest secure literary description:

> The Centaur stretches his right hand toward the rounded Altar, while tightly grasping another sign, “the Beast, for so men of old have named it.”

**Documented fact.** The phrase “men of old” shows that the generic beast-name predated Aratus. There is no species and no catasterism. The beast is compositionally subordinate to Centaurus.

Aratus also refers to it in his rising-and-setting passages: the beast rises or sets with parts of Centaurus. That practical treatment confirms it as a recognized sign, even though it remains visually joined to the Centaur.

### Eratosthenic tradition, late third or second century BCE

The surviving *Catasterismi* are epitomized and textually complicated. In the Centaurus chapter, the figure is identified with **Chiron**, whose piety is demonstrated by carrying an animal toward Ara for sacrifice. A variant says he carries a **wineskin** in order to pour a libation; some iconographic traditions combine skin and animal.

**Mythic tradition, not a Lupus catasterism.** The story explains Centaurus, not the independent origin of the beast. Neither full recension nor Epitome says Zeus transformed Lycaon and placed him here.

### Hipparchus, second century BCE

Hipparchus’ commentary on Aratus recognizes **Thērion** as a separate astronomical heading or sign. Older histories consequently say Hipparchus “separated” it from Centaurus. That should not be misunderstood as a formal boundary act: he distinguished it catalogically while retaining the visual relation.

### Roman Republic and early Empire

**Cicero**, in his Latin *Aratea*, rendered the animal as a **great quadruped**, *quadrupes vasta*. Surviving fragments do not assign it a personal myth.

**Germanicus**, *Aratea* c. 414–422 and 670–671, calls it **fera**, “wild beast,” and says Centaurus bears either forest prey or an offering to appease the gods at the nearby altar. His Centaur is pious Chiron.

**Vitruvius, *De architectura* 9.5**, uses **Bestia** in his astronomical arrangement.

**Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.38 and 3.37–38**, discusses the Centaur and its stars. The object is the **hostia**, sacrificial victim, held near Ara. Hyginus’ myths concern the Centaur’s identity and piety; the victim is not named Lycaon.

**Manilius, *Astronomica*** treats the southern figures astrologically but supplies no independent Lupus biography or Lycaon catasterism.

### Ptolemy, Alexandria, c. 150 CE

**Documented fact.** Ptolemy’s *Almagest* VII–VIII lists **Θηρίον** as one of the 48 constellation headings and assigns it 19 stars. Ptolemy still does not call it a wolf.

In *Tetrabiblos* 1.9, Ptolemy says the bright stars of the Beast have the nature of **Saturn and, to a lesser degree, Mars**. This is the constellation’s principal ancient astrological doctrine.

### Farnese Atlas, Roman copy of a Hellenistic prototype, second century CE

The celestial globe borne by the marble Atlas in Naples contains the Centaurus–beast configuration among its reliefs. Its exact dependency and astronomical epoch are disputed. Bradley Schaefer argued for a Hipparchan source; other historians reject the precision of that deduction. What is safe is that the statue is a Roman imperial object reproducing Hellenistic constellation iconography, not a portrait of a Renaissance wolf.

### Late antiquity

The scholia to Aratus and Germanicus elaborate the Centaur’s offering but do not securely furnish a Lycaon identification. No relevant catasterism occurs in Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus or Pausanias. Those authors do preserve wolf and Lycaon material, discussed below, but do not place Lycaon in Lupus.

### Al-Ṣūfī, Shiraz, 964

**Documented fact.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita* retains Ptolemy’s nineteen-star figure as **Ṣūrat al-Sabʿ**, “the Figure of the Wild/Predatory Beast.” He gives two mirror drawings, one as on a globe and one as seen in the sky.

Manuscripts vary zoologically:

- Many eastern Islamic copies make it a lion-like predator.
- Bodleian Marsh 144, copied by al-Ṣūfī’s son in 1009/10, depicts a canine or wolf-like animal held by its hind leg.
- A 1275–76 Islamic globe in the British Museum presents a lion-like beast.
- Al-ʿUrḍī’s 1288 globe also gives a lion.
- Maghribi-related Vatican and Paris al-Ṣūfī manuscripts are unusual in showing a definite wolf.

This variation follows from the semantic breadth of *sabʿ*: a dangerous beast, often lion, not a taxonomic wolf.

### Medieval Latin and Hebrew transmission, eleventh–fifteenth centuries

The Aratea manuscript tradition transmitted **fera**, **bestia** and sacrificial imagery. The 10th-century Boulogne Aratea includes the Centaur–beast image where an earlier St Gall witness reportedly lacks it.

**Abraham bar Ḥiyya**, early twelfth century, labels the received figure with a Hebrew term for wolf. This is one stage in the narrowing of the generic predator into a wolf.

Gerard of Cremona’s twelfth-century Latin translation of the *Almagest* and later Alfonsine material carried the Arabic–Ptolemaic constellation into Western astronomy. By the thirteenth to fourteenth centuries **Lupus** appears beside or in place of *Bestia* and *Fera*.

A Catalonian Hebrew *Almagest* translation of about 1361, now University of Pennsylvania LJS 57, depicts a spotted quadruped. A German manuscript of Michael Scot’s *Liber introductorius*, c. 1450, shows a wolf transfixed lengthwise by the Centaur’s spear.

### Renaissance print culture

**Johannes Honter**, sixteenth century, labels the animal **Fera** and shows Centaurus spearing it.

**Albrecht Dürer’s southern celestial hemisphere** (Nuremberg, 1515), organized by Johannes Stabius with stars placed by Conrad Heinfogel, includes the received Centaur and beast. Dürer’s woodcut is the first printed European star map of the entire southern hemisphere.

**Johannes Bayer, *Uranometria* (Augsburg, 1603)** depicts a clear wolf-like quadruped impaled or carried by Centaurus. Bayer records a lexicon of names including *Fera*, *Bestia*, *Hostiola* and *Lupus*. His plate and nomenclature decisively stabilized the wolf in learned European atlases.

**Modern historical reconstruction.** The precise reason *Lupus* won is uncertain. It may be:

- the natural narrowing of “wild beast” to a familiar European predator;
- a medieval Latin reading already established before Bayer;
- interaction with Arabic predator terms;
- or some combination.

Allen’s claim that the wolf arose from mistranslating Arabic **al-Fahd**, “leopard,” is possible but inadequately demonstrated.

### Schiller, 1627

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, published in Augsburg with assistance from Bayer and engravings by Lucas Kilian, replaced pagan constellations with biblical figures.

**Correction:** plate XLV is **Sancti Israelis sive Jacobi Patriarchae**, the holy patriarch Israel or Jacob, occupying the Centaurus–Lupus area. The common claim that Schiller made Lupus “Benjamin” appears to derive from conflation with Caesius’s Genesis 49:27 interpretation.

### Hevelius, 1690

Johannes Hevelius retained **Lupus** in the posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* (Gdańsk, 1690). His plates are commonly drawn as celestial globes viewed from outside, reversing left and right relative to sky-view atlases. The animal remains attached to Centaurus.

### Flamsteed, 1725/1729

John Flamsteed could not adequately observe Lupus from Greenwich. His *Historia Coelestis Britannica* and posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* preserved the traditional figure through inherited southern data but did not provide a straightforward native Flamsteed numbering sequence. The constellation’s later “Flamsteed numbers” therefore require catalogue-by-catalogue checking.

### Lacaille, 1751–56

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope. His 1756 map and catalogue greatly expanded and regularized the lettering in Lupus, assigning 29 stars Greek and Latin letters.

Lacaille also inserted **Norma**, originally *Norma et Regula*, between Lupus, Scorpius, Ara and Centaurus. This narrowed the visual and cartographic connection between Lupus and Ara. Some stars conceptually or pictorially belonging to the old beast–altar zone were henceforth represented inside Norma.

### Bode, 1782 and 1801

Bode adopted the received wolf, Lacaille’s Norma, and the enriched stellar catalogues. In 1801 Lupus is not one of Bode’s invented constellations; it is explicitly an ancient Ptolemaic figure. He added catalogue detail and the letter **d**, but did not invent the wolf myth.

Bode’s *Uranographia* is often called the last monumental atlas to combine dense scientific plotting with full figurative engraving. Its twenty very large copper plates show more than 17,000 stars and extensive Herschel nebular material.

### Jamieson, 1822; *Urania’s Mirror*, 1824/25; Burritt, 1830s

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* plate 27 includes Hydra, Corvus, Noctua, Centaurus and Lupus. Jamieson used fewer, naked-eye stars and more freely composed pictures than Bode.

*Urania’s Mirror*, first issued in November 1824 and usually encountered in the 1825 edition, places Lupus on card 32 with Centaurus, Hydra and numerous neighboring figures. Punched holes represented stars when the card was held to light.

Elijah H. Burritt’s *Geography of the Heavens* popularized an explicitly Lycaonian interpretation in American education. Some editions assert that Lupus “according to fable” is Lycaon, even dating the supposed king fantastically thousands of years into the past. This is a key channel by which a late constellation myth entered popular literature.

### Argelander and Heis, nineteenth century

F. W. A. Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) includes Lupus as a named region and supplies chart boundaries but abandons elaborate figural illustration. Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* (1872) continues the scientific trend toward star patterns and boundaries rather than mythological art.

### Gould, 1877–79

Benjamin A. Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina*, based on observations from Córdoba, systematized southern constellations. He added ω, i and k Lupi where he believed Lacaille’s lettering incomplete and supplied the model on which Delporte relied for southern boundaries.

### IAU, 1922–1930

At Rome in 1922, the IAU adopted Lupus among 88 official constellations and assigned **Lup**. It did not separately vote that the mythological figure was a wolf; it standardized a Latin astronomical name.

Delporte’s mandate was to give every sky position a unique home, use right-ascension and declination arcs at equinox **B1875.0**, preserve established usage, and especially avoid moving already named variables into new constellations. His 1930 boundary polygon formalized Lupus.

Because pre-modern figures overlapped and lacked borders, it is misleading to ask exactly which constellation “owned” each fringe star before 1930. The largest historical changes were:

- the gradual conceptual separation of the Beast from **Centaurus**;
- Lacaille’s insertion of **Norma**, interrupting the old Beast-to-Ara scene;
- separation from **Scorpius/Libra** on the northwestern side;
- separation from **Circinus**, another Lacaille instrument, on the south;
- Gould/Delporte’s allocation of every fringe star to one area.

### 2024–26

- 12 September 2024: IAU approval of **Uridim** for α Lupi.
- June 2025: naked-eye **Nova Lupi 2025**, now V462 Lupi.
- 2026: observational confirmation reported for the weak **ξ²-Lupid** meteor source.

## Classical myths and legends

## The ancient constellation story

### The Beast held by the Centaur

**Mythic astronomical tradition; first secure attestation Aratus, c. 270 BCE.** The Centaur reaches toward Ara while gripping the unnamed Beast. Aratus does not say that a sacrifice actually occurs, only that the two figures are posed together and that earlier observers called the object the Beast.

### Chiron’s offering

**Mythographic development; Eratosthenic tradition, probably Hellenistic.** In *Catasterismi* 40, Centaurus is Chiron. He carries a victim toward Ara as a demonstration of piety. The gods honor his righteousness by placing him among the stars. The Beast remains the offering, not a separately catasterized mortal.

### The wineskin variant

**Documented variant.** The Eratosthenic tradition also describes what Chiron carries as a wineskin from which he will pour a libation. It may reflect ambiguity in an earlier image: a limp animal skin and a dead quadruped can look similar on a globe. Some later illustrations give both a vessel or skin and an animal.

### Forest prey

**Roman poetic variant.** Germanicus says the Centaur carries either prey taken from the woods or a sacred offering to placate the gods. This is interpretation of the pose, not a transformation myth.

### Relation to Ara

**Traditional iconographic reading.** The Centaur–Beast–Altar complex visually forms a sacrificial procession. Yet Lacaille’s eighteenth-century Norma now occupies much of the apparent gap between Lupus and Ara, obscuring what ancient mapmakers understood as a continuous scene.

## Lycaon: a late constellation identification

No ancient source consulted says Lycaon was placed among these stars. The stories below are genuine classical Lycaon myths, but their attachment to Lupus is a **post-classical modern invention**.

### Lycaon as culture founder

**Myth; attested in Pausanias, second century CE, reflecting earlier Arcadian traditions.** In *Description of Greece* 8.2–4, Lycaon, son of Pelasgus, founds Lycosura, institutes the Lycaean games and establishes worship of Zeus Lycaeus. Pausanias contrasts early pious sacrifices with Lycaon’s human offering.

### The infant sacrifice and wolf transformation

**Myth; Pausanias 8.2.3–6.** Lycaon sacrifices a human infant on the altar of Zeus Lycaeus and pours its blood on the altar. He is immediately changed into a wolf. Pausanias says the Arcadian story is ancient but expresses skepticism toward later embellishments.

This is the narrative most naturally joined to a constellation called “Wolf,” but no ancient catasterism makes that move.

### The recurring Lykaion werewolf

**Legend reported by Pausanias 6.8.2 and 8.2.6.** At the sacrifice to Zeus Lycaeus, a man who tastes human entrails becomes a wolf; if he abstains from human flesh for nine years he becomes human again. The boxer Damarchus was said to have undergone this transformation. Pausanias accepts Damarchus’s Olympic victory but doubts the werewolf tale.

### Zeus tests Lycaon

**Myth; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1.163–252, published c. 8 CE.** Jupiter descends in disguise after hearing of human wickedness. Lycaon mocks pious prayers, plans to murder his divine guest, and serves flesh cut from a Molossian hostage. Jupiter destroys the house and transforms Lycaon into a wolf whose gray hair, fierce expression and bloodlust mirror his former self. This outrage helps prompt Jupiter’s decision to send the flood.

Ovid never says the wolf becomes the constellation Lupus.

### Lycaon’s sons and the mixed flesh

**Mythographic version; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Library* 3.8.1, probably first or second century CE.** Lycaon has fifty sons. Zeus visits disguised as a laborer to test their impiety. At Maenalus’s instigation they mix a murdered local boy’s entrails with the sacrificial meat. Zeus overturns the table at Trapezus and kills Lycaon and most sons with thunderbolts; Earth saves Nyctimus. The flood of Deucalion follows in some accounts.

Unlike Ovid and Pausanias, this version emphasizes the sons and does not make Lycaon’s wolf form its central ending.

### Hyginus’s synthesis

**Mythographic version; *Fabulae* 176.** Jupiter visits Lycaon, also fathers Arcas upon Callisto, and is tested by Lycaon’s sons with human flesh. Jupiter overturns the table, kills the sons with lightning and changes the father into *lupi figuram*, the form of a wolf. The narrative connects Lycaon with the Callisto–Arcas genealogy but not with Lupus.

### Callisto and Arcas

**Related myth, not Lupus.** Lycaon is father or grandfather within the Callisto tradition. Callisto is transformed into a bear and, with Arcas, becomes Ursa Major and either Ursa Minor or Boötes in different catasterisms: Hesiodic *Astronomy* fragment 3, Eratosthenes *Catasterismi* 1, Hyginus *Astronomica* 2.1, Ovid *Metamorphoses* 2 and *Fasti* 2. This is the classical stellar destiny associated with Lycaon’s family. It reinforces the absence of an ancient Lycaon–Lupus catasterism.

### Nonnus, Manilius and Ovidian afterlife

Nonnus uses lupine metamorphosis and Arcadian material but supplies no secure Lupus catasterism. Manilius gives astrological properties to the constellations without naming Lycaon as this one. Later mythographers and schoolbooks fused Ovid’s memorable werewolf with the Renaissance constellation name.

### Date of the constellation identification

The precise first author to say “Lupus is Lycaon” has not been securely identified. Allen in 1899 says vaguely that “mythologists thought it” so. Burritt’s nineteenth-century schoolbook is a demonstrable popular transmitter. The identification should therefore be labeled:

- ancient Lycaon narrative: **documented myth**;
- ancient Lycaon catasterism as Lupus: **absent**;
- equation of Lycaon with Lupus: **early-modern or modern invention**, first exact source still open.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Mesopotamian

UR.IDIM is the strongest proposed antecedent. The word is securely lexical; the star-for-star mapping is reconstructed. Representations of an *uridimmu* as a human-headed canine or lion-being complicate simplistic translations such as “the Babylonian wolf.”

A further claim, found in older star-name literature, makes α Lupi **Kakkab Su-gub Gud-Elim**, “Star of the Left Hand of the Horned Bull.” This has not been confirmed from a securely edited cuneiform star list and should remain **unverified**.

### Egyptian

No indigenous Egyptian Lupus equivalent has been securely traced. The southern stars could participate in decanal or late zodiacal schemes, but equating any Dendera animal directly with modern Lupus would be retrospective.

### Arabic and Persian

Al-Ṣūfī’s scientific figure is **al-Sabʿ**. Its imagery ranges from lion to wolf. His catalogue also notes indigenous Arabic names when available, but there appears to have been no independent Bedouin “Wolf” matching all nineteen Ptolemaic stars.

**al-Shamārīkh**, date-palm fruit branches, probably described the rich spray of stars across Centaurus and Lupus. **al-Fahd** and **al-Asadah**, “the leopard” and “the lioness,” occur in late lists and early-modern citations. Their exact antiquity and stellar scope are disputed.

### Chinese

Traditional China did not have one constellation corresponding to Lupus. The stars were divided among military and administrative asterisms, especially **Qíguān** and **Chēqí**, across the mansions Jiǎo, Kàng and Dī.

The Song observers of SN 1006 located it relative to **Dī**, the Root mansion, not “in Lupus.” Saying the Chinese saw a supernova “in Lupus” is a correct modern coordinate translation, not their terminology.

### Indian

The Indian nakṣatras are sectors along the Moon’s path. Lupus lies well south of the ecliptic, so it is not itself a nakṣatra. Its right-ascension region lies beneath portions of the zodiacal mansion sequence associated with Libra–Scorpius, but no classical Sanskrit Lupus-like wolf constellation in precisely these stars was found.

Modern Indian-language names translating “Wolf” are adaptations of the IAU constellation.

### Hebrew and biblical mapping

The Hebrew Bible contains no demonstrable map of modern Lupus.

Genesis 49:27 says **Benjamin is a wolf that tears/ravins**. Early-modern Christian Hebraists, including Petrus Caesius, applied that verse to Lupus. This is theological reinterpretation, not recovered Israelite uranography.

Schiller’s plate represents **Jacob/Israel**, whose body or attribute absorbs the classical constellation area. Calling Schiller’s figure Benjamin is a secondary error.

### Christianized sky

Schiller’s project was a full replacement system:

- pagan zodiac → Twelve Apostles;
- northern figures → New Testament subjects;
- southern figures → Old Testament subjects.

His Lupus/Centaurus plate is Jacob or Israel. The scheme had little practical adoption because astronomers depended on the conventional names for communication.

### Kabbalistic, Hermetic and alchemical readings

No securely pre-modern Kabbalistic text was found that maps Lupus’s exact stars to a sefirah, Hebrew letter, tribe or angel. Claims that Lupus “is Benjamin” are biblical-zodiacal correspondences, not automatically Kabbalah.

Likewise, no canonical alchemical emblem for Lupus exists. Later occultists can read the Centaur, victim and altar as sacrifice, animal nature subdued by reason, or transformation of matter; these are **Hermetic interpretations**, not ancient astronomical doctrine.

### Astrology

Ptolemy’s direct statement is the foundation: the bright stars of the Beast are like **Saturn mixed with Mars**. Later astrologers interpreted this as violence, aggression, sacrifice, misfortune, tenacity or predation.

Names such as **Thusia** for γ Lupi and **Hilasmus** for δ Lupi appear in modern fixed-star astrology. Greek *thysia* means sacrifice and *hilasmos* propitiation or expiation, but no ancient star catalogue consulted names those individual stars so. They are best treated as **modern learned inventions**.

Lupus is not zodiacal. The Moon and planets do not normally traverse its central stars. Arabic manāzil and Indian nakṣatras can share the same longitude but their defining stars remain near the ecliptic; therefore no lunar mansion “crosses Lupus” in the ordinary stellar sense.

### Behenian stars

The canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars do **not** include a Lupus star. Lists that add α Lupi or “Men” are later expansions.

## Boundary history

### Ptolemy

Ptolemy defined a list and figure, not a polygon. The Beast’s nineteen stars were anatomically catalogued, while neighboring “unformed” stars could remain under Centaurus, Scorpius or other headings.

### Bayer

Bayer supplied a pictorial extent and stellar letters but no legally exclusive boundaries. The Beast could overlap the Centaur’s grasp and the surrounding blank sky.

### Hevelius

Hevelius preserved overlapping art. His external-globe orientation makes comparison with sky-view maps visually reversed.

### Lacaille

Lacaille’s **Norma et Regula** took sky between Lupus and Ara/Scorpius. **Circinus** took additional southern space. The ancient three-figure sacrificial tableau was thus divided by modern instruments.

### Bode

Bode retained these newer constellations while showing the older wolf. His plate XV makes visible the historical collision: the animal survives, but Norma and the astronomical telescope occupy territory within the ancient dramatic composition.

### Gould and Delporte

Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* supplied practical southern limits and revised letters. Delporte followed established northern and southern atlas practice, especially Gould, while preserving named variables. His boundaries run along B1875 meridians and parallels; precession makes them appear slightly tilted on J2000 charts.

The chief neighbors receiving stars formerly drawn as part of, under, or around the Beast were **Centaurus, Norma, Circinus, Scorpius and Libra**. Exact “loss” lists differ depending on which atlas’s artwork, catalogue headings or Bayer letters are treated as ownership.

## Iconography

### Ancient globes

- **Kugel globe**, approximately first century BCE: a small ancient celestial globe sometimes cited for the Centaurus–beast group; details and date remain debated.
- **Farnese Atlas**, Roman second century CE, Naples: oldest monumental surviving celestial globe, with Centaurus and the beast in relief. The supposed Hipparchan date of its underlying star catalogue is disputed.
- **Mainz globe**, Roman imperial period: preserves related constellation imagery in miniature.

No securely identified ancient coin was found whose reverse can be demonstrated to depict the astronomical Lupus rather than an ordinary wolf, Roman she-wolf or sacrificial animal.

### Aratea manuscripts

Illustrated Aratea manuscripts from the ninth through fifteenth centuries show the beast as a small quadruped, skin or victim held by Centaurus. Manuscript variation is substantial. The Leiden and Boulogne traditions are important witnesses, but individual images should not be assigned a species without examining the folio.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts and globes

- **Bodleian Marsh 144**, 1009/10: canine-like beast held by the hind leg.
- **Islamic globe**, 1275–76, British Museum: lion-like predator.
- **Al-ʿUrḍī globe**, 1288: lion.
- **Vatican and Paris Maghribi al-Ṣūfī manuscripts**, medieval: distinctive wolf representations.
- **Risālat al-Ṣūfī**, probably c. 1225 despite a disputed 1159 inscription: Centaurus in Turkic dress with the beast.

The paired drawings—sky view and globe view—regularly reverse the figure.

### European print atlases

- **Dürer/Stabius/Heinfogel, Nuremberg, 1515:** printed southern hemisphere; received beast near Centaurus.
- **Honter, sixteenth century:** *Fera*, speared by Centaurus.
- **Bayer, Augsburg, 1603:** increasingly definite wolf, Bayer letters superimposed.
- **Schiller/Kilian, Augsburg, 1627:** Jacob/Israel replaces the pagan grouping.
- **Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690:** baroque wolf/beast, globe orientation.
- **Flamsteed, London, 1729:** inherited southern figure within a scientific grid.
- **Lacaille, Paris/Cape tradition, 1756:** more schematic treatment, dense new lettering.
- **Bode, Berlin, 1782:** wolf in the smaller *Vorstellung der Gestirne*.
- **Bode, Berlin, 1801, plate XV:** monumental engraved Lupus amid Scorpius, Ara and new instruments.
- **Jamieson, London, 1822, plate 27:** pictorial wolf with Centaurus and Hydra.
- ***Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/25, card 32:** hand-colored pierced card containing Lupus, Centaurus, Hydra and nine other figures.
- **Burritt, United States, 1830s onward:** school-atlas wolf and Lycaon narrative.
- **Argelander, Bonn, 1843:** boundaries and stars, no elaborate animal.
- **Heis, Cologne, 1872:** scientific star atlas continuing the retreat of figural art.

No specific medieval cathedral portal, ceiling or calendar cycle was found where the figure can be securely identified as astronomical Lupus. Wolves in sacred art are too common to sustain identification without a neighboring Centaur, Ara, star labels or a documented program.

## People, events and works

### Astronomers and observers

- **Aratus** preserved the earliest secure literary name.
- **Hipparchus** treated the Beast as a distinct astronomical sign.
- **Ptolemy** catalogued its nineteen stars and astrological quality.
- **Al-Ṣūfī** transmitted the figure and documented Islamic-era imagery.
- **Bayer** stabilized stellar lettering and the Renaissance wolf image.
- **Lacaille** measured the southern region and introduced neighboring Norma and Circinus.
- **Bode** made its most imposing Enlightenment engraving.
- **Gould** regularized its southern nomenclature.
- **Delporte** fixed its permanent scientific area.
- **ʿAlī ibn Riḍwān**, Chinese court astronomers, Japanese chroniclers and European monks documented SN 1006.
- **James Dunlop** discovered or catalogued several of its southern clusters.
- **Milne and Gardner** associated the SN 1006 radio shell with the historical event.
- **Bernard Pagel and collaborators** identified α Lupi’s β Cephei variability in 1956.
- **Stuart Parker** discovered SN 2013aa.
- **DLT40** discovered SN 2017cbv.
- **ASAS-SN** found the 2016 and 2025 Lupus novae.

### Navigation

Lupus is not a first-rank navigational constellation. Its brightest star is only magnitude 2.3, and no Lupus star belongs to the traditional set of principal nautical navigation stars. Mariners in southern waters could use the conspicuous neighboring α and β Centauri or Crux far more readily.

General claims that ancient explorers “steered by Lupus” were not traceable to voyage logs or navigation manuals and should remain unverified.

### Scientific names and works

- **Upper Centaurus–Lupus** is one of the three major subgroups of the Scorpius–Centaurus association.
- The **Lupus molecular clouds** are a major laboratory for low-mass star formation and protoplanetary disks.
- **EXors**, eruptive pre-main-sequence stars, take their class name from EX Lupi.
- Numerous observatory images, poems and artworks use SN 1006 or the wolf as subject, but these are generally inspired by the modern constellation rather than continuous classical tradition.

No historically prominent ship, spacecraft or building could be verified as having been named specifically for the constellation rather than the Latin animal, the medical disease lupus, a personal name, or the fictional werewolf tradition. Such items require documentary naming statements before inclusion.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was it anciently a wolf?

**Resolution:** no. Greek *Thērion* and Latin *Fera/Bestia* are generic. A wolf appears gradually in medieval and Renaissance transmission.

### 2. Was it originally part of Centaurus?

**Nuanced resolution:** visually and mythically yes; catalogically it became distinct by Hipparchus and certainly Ptolemy. Saying Ptolemy “split it off” is inaccurate because it was already separately named before him.

### 3. Was it Lycaon?

**No ancient evidence found.** Ovid, Hyginus, Pausanias and Pseudo-Apollodorus narrate Lycaon’s transformation but never catasterize him as Lupus. Burritt and later popular authors spread the equation.

### 4. Was it Babylonian UR.IDIM?

**Plausible scholarly reconstruction, not proof.** Position, neighboring figures and the predator theme support continuity. The creature’s species and exact star membership remain uncertain.

### 5. Is Uridim an ancient name for α Lupi?

**No.** It is a 2024 IAU proper name derived from an ancient constellation name. There is no evidence that a Babylonian catalogue called α Lupi alone Uridim.

### 6. Are Men and Yang Mun names of α Lupi?

**No; propagated error.** The Chinese South Gate is α and ε Centauri. α Lupi belongs to Qíguān. “Men” is therefore not an authentic Chinese proper name for α Lupi.

### 7. Is Kekouan a proper name for β Lupi?

Historically, **Qíguān** names an asterism, not β alone. The individualized form arose through Western star-name compilation. Any current IAU status must be separated from its original Chinese function.

### 8. Did an Arabic mistranslation create the wolf?

**Disputed.** Allen says Latin *Lupus* arose through erroneous translation of **al-Fahd**, leopard or panther. But medieval wolf imagery and terminology do not form a sufficiently clear chain to prove this. Semantic narrowing from *sabʿ* or *fera* is equally possible.

### 9. Did Suidas call it Knēkias?

Allen reports Greek **Κνηκίας**, a wolf word, from Suidas. Even if lexically authentic, it does not by itself prove that Byzantine astronomers used it as the constellation’s formal name. The claim needs verification in the relevant Suda lemma.

### 10. Schiller’s Benjamin

**Correction:** Schiller’s actual plate title is Jacob/Israel. Benjamin comes from Genesis 49:27 and Caesius’s exegetical mapping. Later summaries conflated the two.

### 11. “No named bright star”

Correct before September 2024, obsolete now. α Lupi is officially **Uridim**.

### 12. “No meteor shower”

Correct for established historical major showers, but incomplete after the 2026 ξ²-Lupid report. Its formal IAU-MDC status should continue to be monitored.

### 13. SN 1006 brightness

“Brightest recorded stellar event” is a strong scholarly conclusion, not a directly measured magnitude. Estimates depend on historical comparisons, extinction, distance and Type Ia light-curve reconstruction. Assertions such as “sixteen times Venus,” daylight visibility for months or definite shadow-casting should not all be treated as direct testimony.

### 14. North American petroglyph

The proposed Hohokam representation of SN 1006 is **disputed**. Similarity to a scorpion and star is insufficient without secure dating, cultural context and evidence that the marks belong together.

### 15. Nova Lupi 2016 coordinates

The discovery telegram initially published coordinates wrong by many degrees. Corrected coordinates established that ASASSN-16kt was V407 Lupi. The error did not create a false star name but temporarily complicated follow-up.

### 16. Ptolemaic star identifications

Most of Ptolemy’s nineteen stars can be matched with modern bright stars, but the faint tail and limb entries contain uncertainty, and at least one location described by al-Ṣūfī lacked a visible star. Precession, copying errors, coordinate corruption and subjective anatomy account for competing identifications.

### 17. Figures versus boundaries

Modern disagreement over “what Lupus really looks like” usually confuses three things:

1. the historical animal drawing;
2. an optional modern line pattern;
3. the official Delporte area.

Only the third has IAU authority.

## Open questions

1. What is the earliest securely datable Latin manuscript that uses **Lupus** rather than *Fera* or *Bestia* as the principal heading?
2. Can Allen’s proposed **al-Fahd → Lupus** mistranslation be demonstrated through a specific Arabic-Latin manuscript chain?
3. Which Suda lemma underlies the reported astronomical use of **Knēkias**, and is it actually constellation nomenclature?
4. Who first explicitly identified the constellation with **Lycaon**? Burritt is a clear popularizer but probably not the inventor.
5. How precisely should UR.IDIM be projected onto modern stars? Published reconstructions agree on the general region more than on individual membership.
6. What was the exact indigenous range of **al-Shamārīkh** across Centaurus and Lupus?
7. Is the reported “830 stars in 109 groups” a collection-specific metadata statement? It is not Bode’s printed total of 17,240 objects.
8. Will the ξ²-Lupids receive permanent established status from the IAU Meteor Data Center?
9. Several online sources now give β Lupi as **Kekouan**. Its current formal catalogue status should be checked directly against the continually updated IAU Catalog of Star Names rather than inferred from secondary tables.
10. A full atlas-to-atlas star-ownership concordance—Ptolemy, al-Ṣūfī, Bayer, Lacaille, Bode, Gould and Delporte—would require tabulating coordinates from the original catalogues, not merely comparing the pictures.
