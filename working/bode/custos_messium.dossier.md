# Custos Messium: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

> **Status: abolished / not retained as a constellation.** Custos Messium was omitted from the standardized list adopted at the International Astronomical Union’s first General Assembly at Rome in 1922. Because it was already disappearing from professional use, “abolished” is convenient shorthand, but the IAU did not pass a resolution individually condemning or deleting it. The 1922 resolution adopted Latin names and three-letter abbreviations for the “88 principal constellations”; Custos Messium simply was not among them. Eugène Delporte’s boundaries, approved in 1928 and published in 1930, subsequently assigned its former sky principally to **Cassiopeia**, with portions commonly reconstructed in **Camelopardalis** and, depending on how Lalande’s or Bode’s figure is outlined, **Cepheus**. [IAU 1922 resolution](https://iauarchive.eso.org/static/resolutions/IAU1922_French.pdf), [IAU history of the constellations](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### Identity and names

| Form | Meaning and status |
|---|---|
| **Custos Messium** | Lalande’s scholarly Latin: “Keeper/Guardian of the Harvests.” *Custos* is a guardian, watcher, keeper, or custodian; *messium* is the genitive plural of *messis*, “harvest/crop,” hence “of the harvests.” |
| **Messier / Le Messier** | Lalande’s French title. In eighteenth-century French a *messier* was an officer or rural watchman charged with guarding crops. The *Encyclopédie* explains that a *sergent blavier* was “la même chose que messier ou sergent messilier, *messium custos*.” Thus the astronomical name is a deliberate pun on Charles Messier’s surname, not merely a later interpretation. [Encyclopédie, “Sergent”](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/L%E2%80%99Encyclop%C3%A9die/1re_%C3%A9dition/SERGENT) |
| **Erndte Hüter / Erndtehüter** | Bode-era German, in historical spelling: “harvest keeper.” Modern German would normally be *Erntehüter* or *Erntebehüter*. Allen’s compact *Erndtehüter* is not standard modern spelling. |
| **Harvest-Keeper / Guardian of the Harvests** | Normal English translations. |
| **Vineyard Keeper** | Thomas Young’s altered English name on his 1807 northern planisphere. This is not a translation of *messis* and should be treated as Young’s adaptation. |
| **the Shepherd** | Reported by E. Otis Kendall in 1845. No independent early attestation has been found; probably a confusion produced by the crook or staff in the image. |
| **Mietitore** | Italian, literally “reaper,” reported by Richard Hinckley Allen; a freer rendering than “guardian.” |
| **Custode/Guardiano delle Messi** | Modern Italian translations. |
| **Guardián de la Cosecha / Guardián de las Mieses** | Modern Spanish translations, not demonstrated eighteenth-century astronomical names. |
| **Oogstbewaarder** | Dutch “harvest keeper,” found in twentieth-century Dutch reference works. |
| **Хранитель Урожая** | Modern Russian “Keeper of the Harvest.” A reported mistranslation *Страж Мессии*, “Guardian of the Messiah,” is a modern confusion of *Messium* with “Messiah,” not a historical name. |
| **Custos Messius** | Occasional modern misspelling. The historically attested form is *Custos Messium*. |
| **Custodis Messium** | Regular Latin genitive, useful in phrases such as “a star of Custos Messium,” but never an IAU genitive. |
| **CsM, Cus** | Retrospective abbreviations used in modern obsolete-constellation lists and astrology. Neither is official. |

**Documented fact:** Lalande’s pun is stated in the 1776 review of his globe pamphlet: *“On appelle Messier, en François, celui qui est préposé à la garde des moissons…”*—“In French, one calls *Messier* the person appointed to guard the harvests.” This is the decisive primary evidence.

**No IAU abbreviation or official genitive exists.** Because the IAU did not retain Custos Messium, it has neither an official three-letter abbreviation nor an official genitive. Modern lists giving **CsM**, **Cus**, *Custodis Messium*, or *Custodis Messoris* are retrospective conveniences. *Custodis Messoris* actually means “of the guardian/reaper” and does not reproduce Lalande’s title.

### Position and figure

Lalande placed the figure among faint northern stars between Cassiopeia, Cepheus, and Camelopardalis. Bode placed it between Cassiopeia and Rangifer/Tarandus, the Reindeer. In Bode’s mature depiction the keeper is a rustic male figure near or on the northern end of the Giraffe, carrying agricultural or pastoral implements. In the Jamieson–*Urania’s Mirror* tradition he has both a crooked staff and a sickle.

The figure is circumpolar from much of Europe. It is not a zodiacal constellation and never possessed universally accepted borders.

### Present allocation of the stars

The core stars most consistently associated with Custos Messium now belong to:

- **Cassiopeia:** 23, 40, 42, 47, 48, 49, 50, and 54 Cassiopeiae; ψ and ω Cassiopeiae are included by some reconstructions.
- **Camelopardalis:** γ Camelopardalis and the stars now called BE, BK, and sometimes CS Camelopardalis.
- **Cepheus:** descriptions regularly say that the figure “embraced part of Cepheus,” although modern overlays of Bode’s actual drawing place most identifiable figure-stars in Cassiopeia and Camelopardalis. Claims about individual Cepheus members require an explicitly stated historical outline.

This uncertainty is intrinsic. Before Delporte, a constellation could mean a picture, a star list, or an approximate district; Lalande’s globe, Bode’s chart, and later atlas artists did not draw identical polygons.

Bode placed letter labels on stars of the constellation, extending through approximately **A–r** in the published chart/catalogue tradition. These are Bode’s constellation-local reference letters, not ancient names and not securely interchangeable with the Bayer letters of Cassiopeia or Camelopardalis. Modern web tables that call them “Bayer designations of Custos Messium” overstate their status.

### Proper names and their derivations

- **Custos**, for BE Camelopardalis, is Latin “keeper.” **Documented modern naming:** adopted by the IAU Working Group on Star Names on 25 December 2025 as a commemoration of Custos Messium. It does not restore the constellation. [IAU 2026 list, item 10](https://www.iau.org/IAU/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx)

- **Gang**, for 50 Cassiopeiae, represents Chinese 杠 (*Gàng*), “shaft,” “pole,” or “support,” specifically the support of the imperial canopy. **Documented modern naming based on an older Chinese asterism:** adopted on 25 December 2025. [IAU 2026 list, item 13](https://www.iau.org/IAU/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx)

- **Shaowei**, for γ Camelopardalis, is derived from a traditional Chinese official-title asterism. It too was standardized in 2025.

- **Rangifer**, for 49 Cassiopeiae, is modern scholarly Latin for “reindeer,” preserving Le Monnier’s neighboring obsolete constellation. Its assignment warns against treating the Bode figures as mutually exclusive territorial regions.

No securely documented medieval Arabic, Greek, or Latin proper name has been found for 50 Cassiopeiae itself. Its faintness and far-northern position left it outside the bright named-star tradition from which most familiar Arabic-derived star names emerged.

### Asterisms within the same stars

There is no enduring western naked-eye asterism called the Harvest Keeper independent of the constellation picture. The principal non-western structures are Chinese:

- **Gàng 杠, the Canopy Support/Shaft:** later Chinese charts include 49 and 50 Cassiopeiae and neighboring stars in this support beneath or beside the imperial canopy.
- **Huágài 華蓋, the Emperor’s Canopy:** includes 40 Cassiopeiae and other stars in northern Cassiopeia.
- **Zǐwēi Zuǒyuán 紫微左垣, Left Wall of the Purple Forbidden Enclosure:** includes 23 Cassiopeiae.
- **Wǔdì Nèizuò 五帝內座, Interior Seats of the Five Emperors:** includes 47 Cassiopeiae in one modern identification.

The detailed identification of Chinese stars changed between historical maps. Research summarized in the All Skies Encyclopaedia indicates that early forms of Huagai and Gang lay more toward Cepheus, with the Cassiopeia–Camelopardalis form becoming standard from the Tang period onward. [Gang historical summary](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Gang), [Cassiopeia identifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassiopeia_in_Chinese_astronomy)

### Deep-sky objects

**Documented negative finding:** no nebula, open cluster, globular cluster, or galaxy has a historically established name derived from **Custos Messium**, and no Messier object lay securely inside the compact figure as drawn by Bode. The irony is notable: a constellation honoring the author of the Messier Catalogue contains no Messier object.

Bode’s enormous companion catalogue included nebulae and clusters throughout his atlas, but that does not establish a “Custos Messium object” unless the object was catalogued under the constellation’s heading.

#### NGC 771: an instructive catalogue error

50 Cassiopeiae itself appears as **NGC 771**. This is not a galaxy or true nebula. John Herschel recorded the star as nebulous, and Dreyer transmitted the observation into the *New General Catalogue*. Modern identification shows that the entry is the star 50 Cassiopeiae. Thus the brightest Custos star also illustrates exactly the kind of star/nebula classification problem that complicated eighteenth- and nineteenth-century catalogues.

#### Reported multiple star

Kendall’s 1845 *Uranography and Atlas* says Custos Messium contained a “remarkable quintuple star” near the then-current position RA 58°, declination +61°52′, with two sixth-magnitude and three ninth-magnitude components. Because coordinates were epoch-dependent and Kendall supplies no durable modern identifier in that passage, a unique modern identification should not be asserted without a fresh astrometric reduction. [Kendall scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/ec/Uranography._%26_Atlas_%28IA_uranographyatlas00kend%29.pdf)

### Variables, novae, supernovae

- **BE Camelopardalis/Custos:** irregular Lc variable; the most important variable securely attached to the historical figure.
- **SU Cassiopeiae:** short-period classical Cepheid within broad modern overlays.
- **RZ Cassiopeiae:** Algol-type eclipsing binary within broad overlays.
- Other variable designations in northern Cassiopeia and Camelopardalis depend on how one converts an artistic figure into a polygon.

**No securely documented historical nova or supernova was described as occurring “in Custos Messium.”** Transients in this region were reported under Cassiopeia, Cepheus, Camelopardalis, or coordinate positions once the Harvest Keeper had faded from use.

The famous supernova remnant Cassiopeia A lies much farther south in Cassiopeia than the core of Custos Messium. It should not be appropriated to the obsolete figure merely because both are now in Cassiopeia.

### Meteor showers

No recognized meteor shower bears the name **Custodids**, **Messiids**, or **Custos Messiids**. The often-repeated remark that a major shower preserves an abolished constellation refers to the **Quadrantids**, named after Lalande’s *Quadrans Muralis*, not Custos Messium.

### Boundary history

- **Ptolemy, second century:** Custos did not exist. Its stars were faint northern stars outside or around the formal figures of Cassiopeia and Cepheus—what later cataloguers called *informes* or “unformed” stars.
- **Bayer, 1603:** no Custos. Bayer’s *Uranometria* retained ancient Cassiopeia and Cepheus; Camelopardalis was not yet an established Bayer constellation.
- **Plancius, 1612/1613:** Camelopardalis entered printed celestial cartography, absorbing much of the previously unfigured northern space.
- **Schiller, 1627:** his Christian atlas reassigned the ancient figures but preceded Custos by nearly 150 years.
- **Hevelius, 1690:** mapped Camelopardalis and the surrounding circumpolar field but not Custos Messium.
- **Corbinianus Thomas, 1730:** showed the later Custos stars as unformed or associated with the vicinity of Camelopardalis.
- **Le Monnier, 1740s–1770s:** placed the Reindeer, *Rangifer/Tarandus*, across part of the same northern field. The two figures later overlapped.
- **Lalande, 1775:** gathered unnamed/unformed stars into *Messier/Custos Messium*.
- **Bode, 1782:** incorporated the figure in *Vorstellung der Gestirne*.
- **Fortin’s revised Flamsteed tradition:** Rangifer appeared in the French *Atlas céleste* of 1776; later states, especially the 1795 revision influenced by Lalande, included Custos Messium. It is misleading to credit John Flamsteed personally: he died in 1719.
- **Bode, 1801:** gave the figure its best-known elaborate form in *Uranographia*, Plate III, adjoining Rangifer, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, and Camelopardalis.
- **Delporte, 1928–1930:** imposed non-overlapping boundaries along right ascension and declination at equinox 1875.0. Most of the visually reconstructed keeper fell inside Cassiopeia; peripheral figure-stars fell in Camelopardalis and possibly Cepheus. [Delporte bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1774: no Harvest Keeper

**Documented negative finding:** no Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Indian, or medieval Latin constellation corresponding to Custos Messium has been traced. It is not an ancient figure revived under a new title.

Ancient and medieval cultures did map portions of the same sky, especially through Cassiopeia, Cepheus, Chinese circumpolar officialdom, and Arabic Bedouin camel imagery. Those are parallel traditions, not antecedent “Harvest Keepers.”

### 1774: the cometary occasion

The comet now designated **C/1774 P1 (Montaigne)** was discovered by Jacques Laibats-Montaigne, not Messier. It first appeared near the north celestial pole between Cassiopeia and Cepheus. Messier followed it telescopically for 41 days, from 18 August to 25 October according to his 1811 account, tracing it through Lacerta, Andromeda, Pegasus, and toward Aquarius.

**Documented fact:** Lalande repeatedly visited Messier’s observatory to view the comet while a new celestial globe was being engraved.

**Date distinction:** Messier’s retrospective 1811 title says Lalande created the constellation “en 1774.” This refers to the conception and cometary occasion. The globe on which it first appeared was published in **1775**.

### 1775: Lalande’s globe

Lalande prepared a celestial globe one French foot in diameter, engraved and sold by **Jean Lattré**, paired with a terrestrial globe by Rigobert Bonne. It included the new figure under the name **Messier / Custos Messium**. Lalande did not warn Messier beforehand, according to Messier’s later notice.

Lalande’s own *Bibliographie astronomique* later summarized the globe as containing all then-known stars, Lacaille’s new southern constellations, and “celle que j’avais introduite sous le nom de Messier.” [Stevenson’s globe history, quoting Lalande](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/39867/pg39867-images.html)

### November 1776: first surviving printed rationale

A review of *Explication des nouveaux globes céleste et terrestre* in the *Journal des Sçavans* gives the essential contemporary explanation:

> *“A l’occasion de la Comète de 1774… M. de la Lande a cru devoir placer, dans son globe céleste, une nouvelle constellation sous le nom de Messier, Custos Messium.”*

It continues that a French *messier* guards harvests or the “treasures of the earth”; Charles Messier had for twenty years been similarly appointed to guard the sky and discover comets. Lalande gathered the scattered, unformed stars between Cassiopeia, Cepheus, and the Giraffe—between the rulers of an agricultural people and an animal destructive to crops.

This passage proves:

1. the cometary occasion;
2. the pun;
3. the honor to Messier;
4. the declared surrounding-constellation symbolism;
5. Lalande’s description of the stars as *sparsiles ou informes*.

### 1782: Bode’s first adoption

Johann Elert Bode included the new figure, in German as **Erndte Hüter**, in *Vorstellung der Gestirne*. This was its crucial entry into German celestial cartography.

Bode did not invent it. Claims that Bode was its creator arise because his images are much more accessible and influential than Lalande’s globe.

### 1795: Fortin’s revised French Flamsteed atlas

A later state of Jean Fortin’s reduced French adaptation of Flamsteed included Custos Messium. Attribution should be made to the French editors, particularly Lalande’s influence, rather than to Flamsteed. The nearby Reindeer was already engraved in the 1776 edition, according to Messier.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode published *Ioannis Elerti Bode Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio…* at Berlin, twenty large copperplates accompanied by his *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, cataloguing 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. [ETH complete facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [companion catalogue](https://books.google.com/books/about/Allgemeine_Beschreibung_und_Nachweisung.html?id=NUlRAAAAcAAJ)

Custos Messium appears on **Plate III** beside Rangifer. Bode’s text says:

> *“Ist von Herrn de la Lande zu Ehren des Herrn Messier der bey seinen unermüdeten Durchmusterungen des Firmaments, in 30 Jahren fast alle erschienene Cometen entdekte, zwischen der Cassiopeja und dem Rennthier ans Firmament gebracht.”*

Translation: “It was placed in the firmament by M. de Lalande in honor of M. Messier, who in his indefatigable surveys of the heavens discovered nearly all the comets that appeared over thirty years; it lies between Cassiopeia and the Reindeer.”

**Documented fact:** Bode explicitly credited Lalande and explained the honor to Messier.

**Iconographic fact:** Bode shows a rustic guardian with cap, staff/crook, and harvested vegetation. The familiar sickle becomes especially conspicuous in later British derivatives.

### 1805: Bode’s revised popular atlas

The revised *Vorstellung der Gestirne* retained Custos and made the figure more widely available in smaller-format astronomy.

### 1807: Thomas Young’s *Vineyard Keeper*

Thomas Young labelled the figure **Vineyard Keeper** on Plate XXXVI, figure 517, of *A Course of Lectures on Natural Philosophy and the Mechanical Arts*. This is the earliest located English renaming. “Vineyard” was Young’s invention; Lalande’s *moissons* means harvests/crops generally.

### 1811: Messier’s own notice

Messier published *Notice sur une nouvelle constellation créée dans le ciel par M. de Lalande, en 1774, sous le nom de Messier; Custos Messium* in the *Journal de physique*, volume 72.

This is indispensable first-person evidence. Messier stated that Lalande had inserted it on the Lattré globe without telling him and tied it directly to the 1774 comet. He also observed that Lalande’s figure crossed Le Monnier’s Reindeer. [Reproduction containing the 1811 text](https://s6c1eff70f1848319.jimcontent.com/download/version/1447615545/module/11989619027/name/RELAZ%20REST%20GLOBI%20SEM%20PD%202015.pdf)

### 1822: Alexander Jamieson

Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* retained Custos Messium. His composition strongly shaped the subsequent popular image: a farmer or rural guardian stationed by Camelopardalis and Rangifer, holding a crook and sickle.

### 1824/1825: *Urania’s Mirror*

Card 2 of *Urania’s Mirror; or, a View of the Heavens* depicts **Camelopardalis, Tarandus and Custos Messium**. The cards were first advertised in November 1824; surviving plates and catalogue records are often dated 1825. They were engraved by Sidney Hall, derived largely from Jamieson, and are now attributed to the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam rather than the anonymous “lady” advertised at publication. [Library of Congress/Wikimedia image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sidney_Hall_-_Urania%27s_Mirror_-_Camelopardalis,_Tarandus_and_Custos_Messium.jpg)

This image—keeper on or immediately above the Giraffe, reindeer almost colliding with him—is now the most reproduced colored representation.

### 1829: Russian reception

Kornelius Reissig’s Russian celestial atlas, based on Bode’s cartographic tradition and printed dramatically in gold on dark grounds, retained obsolete figures including Custos Messium. [Rare-book description](https://crouchrarebooks.com/browse/the-first-russian-celestial-atlas-printed-in-gold/)

### 1845: Kendall

E. Otis Kendall still called Custos a “new constellation,” gave “the Shepherd” as an alternative, and described its alleged quintuple star. This demonstrates popular survival in American instructional astronomy but not strong professional acceptance.

### 1870s–1890s: decline

Chambers in 1877 still noted that Lalande had placed Messier’s name in the heavens near Tarandus. Other late-century textbooks omitted it. Richard Hinckley Allen wrote in 1899 that Custos “has now passed out of the recognition of astronomers.” [Allen, pp. 191–92](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Custos_Messium%2A.html)

Argelander’s and Heis’s observational atlases helped establish a scientific style centered on measured stars rather than decorative figures. No evidence was found that either treated Custos as an important independent constellation; their omission is part of its practical disappearance.

### 1913: lexicographical afterlife

The *Century Dictionary and Cyclopedia* described it as a 1775 proposal embracing parts of Cepheus, Cassiopeia, and Camelopardalis and “stealing” a fourth-magnitude star from each of the last two. By then it was a historical entry rather than a working chart figure.

### 1922–1930: standardization

At Rome in 1922 the IAU adopted the standardized principal constellations and abbreviations. Custos was omitted. At Cambridge in 1925 Delporte presented a boundary system; it was approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930.

**Correct formulation:** Custos was not a thriving constellation abruptly erased at Rome. It had largely vanished from professional astronomy before the IAU existed. The IAU codification made that disappearance permanent.

### 2025–2026: partial nominal restoration

On 25 December 2025 the IAU named BE Camelopardalis **Custos**, while 50 Cassiopeiae became **Gang** and 49 Cassiopeiae **Rangifer**. The names were announced publicly in February 2026. This restored the words Custos and Rangifer as **proper names of individual stars**, not as constellations. [IAU announcement](https://www.iau.org/IAU/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx)

## Invention, meaning, and reception

### What Lalande intended

**Documented fact:** the figure honored Charles Messier for his long vigilance in searching for comets.

**Documented pun:** terrestrial *messier*, guardian of crops; celestial Messier, guardian of the sky.

**Documented spatial conceit:** Lalande’s review placed him between an agricultural royal couple—Cepheus and Cassiopeia—and an animal said to damage crops, Camelopardalis.

**Documented comet connection:** the 1774 comet first appeared in this otherwise unnamed field.

### Messier and the “harvest” of comets

Charles Messier’s catalogue of nebulae and star clusters was designed to help comet searchers distinguish stationary diffuse objects from moving comets. The first list, presented to the Académie in 1771 and printed in 1774, contained 45 objects; later lifetime editions reached 103, with seven additional objects attached by later historical reconstruction. [Messier publication bibliography](https://messier.lamost.org/seds/seds.org/messier/en/xtra/history/m-pub.html), [University of Oklahoma exhibit](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/catalog-nebulae-and-star-clusters.html)

Lalande’s surviving explanation praises Messier primarily as a tireless observer and discoverer of comets. Describing the catalogue itself as the “harvest” that Custos guards is a fitting later metaphor but not the exact wording of the 1776 rationale.

### The “Great Comet of 1774”

**Contested/popular label:** some modern retellings call C/1774 P1 the “Great Comet of 1774.” The primary notice simply calls it the comet that appeared near the north pole. The comet was important as the occasion for the constellation, but “Great Comet” is not securely established here as its contemporary standard title.

### French astronomers naming one another

Lalande’s honor to Messier belongs to a late-eighteenth-century culture of proposing celestial monuments to people, patrons, instruments, inventions, and national achievements. Relevant figures included Poczobut’s Taurus Poniatovii, Hell’s proposed Herschel telescope and George’s Harp, Bode’s Honores Friderici, and Lalande’s later Quadrans Muralis.

The suggested reciprocal pattern “Lalande’s Felis, Messier’s Custos” needs correction:

- **Custos Messium was Lalande’s constellation for Messier.**
- **Felis was also Lalande’s own proposal**, inspired by his fondness for cats, not a constellation Messier made for Lalande.
- Messier did not name Custos; he was its surprised honoree.

## Myths and legends

### Classical mythology

**Documented negative finding:** Custos Messium has no Greek or Roman myth, catasterism, divine placement story, or ancient mythographic identity. It appears in none of the relevant passages of Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi*, the *Epitome*, Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*, Aratus or the scholia, Germanicus, Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, or Pausanias.

Accordingly, no responsible dossier can supply “every classical myth” for Custos itself. Those authors discuss the surrounding ancient figures Cassiopeia and Cepheus, but transferring their Ethiopian royal mythology to Lalande’s farmer would create a false pedigree.

### Lalande’s agricultural narrative

**Modern invention, 1775–76:** the keeper guards the harvest from the crop-damaging Giraffe while standing between the royal rulers of an agricultural people. This is an emblematic explanation, not a classical myth.

The earliest surviving carrier is the 1776 *Journal des Sçavans* review of Lalande’s explanatory pamphlet.

### Charles Messier as celestial watchman

**Modern honorific narrative:** Messier had watched over the sky for twenty years as a rural *messier* watches crops. Lalande is the first documented author; Bode spread the idea in German and Messier retold the origin in 1811.

### Joseph Cornell’s guardian myth

Twentieth-century American artist **Joseph Cornell** collected and reused nineteenth-century celestial imagery, including Bode’s Custos. In works and notes concerning Marilyn Monroe he transformed the forgotten keeper into a protective celestial companion for the actress. Scholar Kirsten Hoving describes this explicitly as Cornell creating “a myth where none existed”: a child seen in the subway became an incarnation of Custos, imagined as Monroe’s guardian.

This is a genuine modern artistic mythology, but it belongs to Cornell, not Lalande or ancient astronomy. [Hoving, “Joseph Cornell’s Cosmos”](https://www.cultureandcosmos.org/pdfs/24/24_Hoving_Joseph_Cornell.pdf)

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian and Egyptian

No Babylonian or Egyptian constellation matching Lalande’s compact northern farmer has been identified. Assertions that Custos has a direct Mesopotamian harvest-god antecedent are unsupported.

The region’s high northern declination and the faintness of its defining stars complicate attempts to match it with ancient Near Eastern figures. The absence of a secure match should not be filled with a generic “harvest” deity.

### Greek and Roman

The stars lay around the Ptolemaic figures **Kassiepeia** and **Kepheus** or among their unformed stars. They did not constitute a separate classical person, tool, or animal.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita* of 964 retained Ptolemy’s 48 constellations. Cassiopeia was **Ṣūrat Dhāt al-Kursī**, “the figure of the Lady of the Chair/Throne.” [Al-Ṣūfī constellation list](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_constellations.htm)

The famous Bedouin asterism **al-kaff al-khaḍīb**, “the henna-dyed hand,” corresponds to the bright W of Cassiopeia, not to the faint northern Custos core. In another Bedouin conception the Cassiopeia–Andromeda region formed part of a large she-camel, and al-Ṣūfī noted the Dyed Hand at the camel’s hump. [Smithsonian study of Islamic celestial globes](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/6147a263-47ab-4fe2-96fb-ef754e80fcc8/content)

Thus:

- there is rich Arabic lore nearby;
- none of it is an Arabic “Harvest Keeper”;
- no specific al-Ṣūfī name for 50 Cassiopeiae has been securely traced.

### Persian

Al-Ṣūfī worked at Shiraz and was Persian, but his astronomical book is in Arabic and combines the Greek-Ptolemaic system with indigenous Arabian lore. Later Persian manuscripts sometimes show Cassiopeia as a crowned woman and sometimes superimpose camel imagery. These belong to Cassiopeia’s manuscript tradition, not Custos.

No independent Persian name for the Lalande group has been found.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No early Hebrew constellation equivalent to Custos Messium has been documented. Biblical or rabbinic identifications of Cassiopeia, Cepheus, or the far-northern stars are speculative and inconsistent.

No primary Kabbalistic text was located that maps this exact faint star group to a sefirah, angel, tribe, or biblical person. Modern esoteric charts that assign it such meanings are retrospective constructions.

### Indian

The region lies far north of the ecliptic and outside the normal chain of the 27 or 28 **nakṣatras**. No Vedic or classical Indian lunar mansion crosses Custos Messium.

The IAU’s adoption of Indian-derived names elsewhere in 2025 does not imply an Indian tradition for Custos.

### Chinese

Chinese astronomy provides the strongest genuine alternative organization:

- the stars belong to the **Purple Forbidden Enclosure**, the circumpolar celestial bureaucracy;
- 50 Cassiopeiae is in **Gang**, the support of the emperor’s canopy;
- 40 Cassiopeiae is associated with **Huagai**, the canopy;
- 23 Cassiopeiae lies in the Left Wall;
- 47 Cassiopeiae belongs to the Interior Seats of the Five Emperors in a standard identification.

These mappings are older than Lalande but historically mutable. They are parallel sky cultures, not sources for his design.

### Julius Schiller’s Christian sky, 1627

Schiller lived too early to replace Custos itself. The same field was distributed among his Christianized substitutes for older constellations:

- **Cassiopeia → Sanctæ Mariæ Magdalenæ**, Saint Mary Magdalene.
- **Cepheus → Sancti Stephani Protomartyris**, Saint Stephen the Protomartyr.
- Camelopardalis did not supply a direct “Harvest Keeper” analogue in the relevant figure.

Schiller’s atlas was published at Augsburg by Andreas Aperger, with contributions associated with Johann Bayer and engravings by Lucas Kilian. [ETH Astrorara presentation](https://astrorara.library.ethz.ch/en/coelum-stellatum), [constellation concordance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coelum_Stellatum_Christianum)

### Astrology

Because Custos was invented in 1775, it appears in neither Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* nor the classical fixed-star doctrines. None of its defining stars is one of the fifteen medieval **Behenian fixed stars**. No Arabic lunar mansion or Indian nakṣatra crosses it.

Vivian E. Robson’s *Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology* (1923) states that Custos supposedly gives a simple, kind, retiring, pleasant, honest nature and an interest in rural pursuits. This is **modern astrological invention**, first traced here to Robson, not an ancient doctrine. Later astrology websites repeat it without older evidence. [Modern transcription citing Robson](https://astrologyking.com/custos-messium-constellation/)

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No eighteenth-century alchemical or Hermetic interpretation specifically attached to Custos Messium has been located. Generic modern readings of the sickle as Saturn, harvest as spiritual reaping, or keeper as an initiatory guardian are symbolically possible but unverified as historical tradition.

## Iconography

### Pre-Custos background

- **Farnese Atlas, Roman, usually dated second century CE:** contains classical constellation figures but no Custos. It is relevant only as evidence for the ancient Cassiopeia/Cepheus sky.
- **Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, from the eleventh century onward:** show the Lady of the Chair and occasionally associated camel imagery, never a harvest keeper. A well-known Cassiopeia manuscript image is available through [Utrecht’s al-Ṣūfī project](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_manuscripts.htm).
- **Dürer’s 1515 northern star map:** depicts the classical circumpolar constellations but no Custos.
- **Bayer, Augsburg, 1603:** no Custos.
- **Schiller, Augsburg, 1627:** Mary Magdalene replaces Cassiopeia; no Custos.
- **Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690:** no Custos; the area remains with Camelopardalis and unformed stars.
- **Corbinianus Thomas, 1730:** later Custos stars appear near the Giraffe without Lalande’s figure.

### Lalande’s globe, Paris, 1775

This is the first attested image. It was a one-foot celestial globe engraved by Lattré. Surviving examples of the first state are rare, and Stevenson reported difficulty locating a matched 1775 pair; a related celestial globe dated 1779 survives at Palermo.

### Bode, Berlin and Stralsund, 1782

*Vorstellung der Gestirne*, Plate 2, incorporates **Erndte Hüter**. This is the first major atlas reception.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

*Uranographia*, Plate III, supplies the canonical scholarly engraving. The keeper is a rustic watchman positioned amid Camelopardalis, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, and Rangifer. The atlas consists of twenty monumental copperplates, approximately 64–66 cm in format, and was published by Bode himself with financial support acknowledged to Friedrich von Hahn. [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [NYPL copy](https://old-digitalcollections.nypl.org/collections/joannis-elerti-bode-uranographia-sive-astrorum-descriptio-viginti-tabulis-aeneis)

### Thomas Young, London, 1807

The northern planisphere labels the figure **Vineyard Keeper**. It is reduced to a schematic rather than Bode’s fully modeled figure.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson dramatizes the farmer with implements and places him tightly against the Giraffe and Reindeer. His atlas directly shaped *Urania’s Mirror*.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/25

Plate 2 is the most familiar color image: a rustic keeper, sickle and crook in hand, perched on or above Camelopardalis as Tarandus approaches. Pinholes at the stars allowed the card to be held before light. [High-resolution plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sidney_Hall_-_Urania%27s_Mirror_-_Camelopardalis,_Tarandus_and_Custos_Messium.jpg)

### Russian atlas, St Petersburg, 1829

Reissig’s gold-on-black adaptation of the Bode/Fortin tradition carried the obsolete figure into Russian celestial publishing.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

**Negative finding:** no securely documented ancient coin, cathedral portal, medieval calendar, or historic astronomical ceiling depicts Custos Messium, since the figure did not exist before 1775. Modern decorative reproductions of Bode or *Urania’s Mirror* should not be described as independent iconographic traditions.

## People, events, and works

### Joseph-Jérôme Lefrançois de Lalande

Inventor of the figure; editor, teacher, ephemeris-maker, and one of the most influential French astronomical communicators of his generation. Custos demonstrates both his affection for wordplay and his willingness to use the sky as a space of professional commemoration.

### Charles Messier

The honoree. Messier’s persistent comet searches and his practical catalogue of diffuse stationary objects made him an ideal “guardian of the sky.” Lalande’s constellation is one of the most extravagant honors he received while alive.

Messier is also commemorated by the Messier catalogue, lunar craters Messier and Messier A, and asteroid **(7359) Messier**—not 7539, a number sometimes printed in secondary accounts.

### Jacques Laibats-Montaigne

Discoverer of C/1774 P1. His role matters because popular summaries often imply that Messier discovered the comet commemorated by the figure. Messier observed it intensively but did not discover it.

### Pierre Charles Le Monnier

Creator or promoter of neighboring **Rangifer/Tarandus**, traditionally connected with his northern expedition to Lapland. Messier reported that Lalande’s new figure crossed Le Monnier’s Reindeer.

### Johann Elert Bode

The great popularizer, not the inventor. His 1782 and 1801 charts gave Custos its durable visual identity. His 1801 catalogue contained 17,240 listed stars and other objects, far more than the figure itself.

### Thomas Young

Renamed it Vineyard Keeper in 1807. This did not establish an independent English astronomical tradition.

### Alexander Jamieson, Sidney Hall, Richard Rouse Bloxam

Together—atlas designer, engraver, and now-identified designer/compiler of *Urania’s Mirror*—they produced the best-known British iconography.

### Joseph Cornell

Rescued Custos from antiquarian obscurity for modern art. His Cornell-box cosmology associated the guardian with protection, memory, lost constellations, and Marilyn Monroe. This is the most substantial twentieth-century cultural afterlife located.

### Navigation, ships, and missions

No explorer is known to have steered by “Custos Messium” as a named navigation figure. Its stars are faint, and mariners used Polaris, the Bears, Cassiopeia, and brighter named stars.

No historical ship or spacecraft mission named **Custos Messium** was located. Modern companies, artworks, and online projects using the name are namesakes, not continuations of an astronomical institution.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was it invented in 1774, 1775, 1776, or 1779?

- **1774:** conception and cometary occasion; Messier’s 1811 title.
- **1775:** publication of Lalande’s Lattré globe; best date for introduction.
- **1776:** publication of the surviving review explaining it.
- **1779:** date of a related surviving Lalande celestial globe and the erroneous date repeated by some French web sources.

**Conclusion:** “conceived in 1774, introduced on the published globe of 1775, explained in print in 1776” reconciles the evidence.

### 2. Did Lalande or Bode invent it?

**Documented:** Lalande. Bode explicitly credits him. Bode’s prominence results from the survival and reproduction of *Uranographia*.

### 3. Did Messier discover the 1774 comet?

No. Montaigne discovered it; Messier observed it extensively. Claims that Lalande rewarded Messier for discovering this particular comet are erroneous.

### 4. Did the figure commemorate Messier’s catalogue or his comets?

The first rationale chiefly praises his vigilance and comet discovery. The catalogue had just appeared in print with 45 entries and belongs to the context, but the primary text does not say that Custos represents the catalogue itself.

### 5. “Great Comet of 1774”

A modern amplifying label. The primary accounts consulted call it simply the comet of 1774 or the comet that appeared near the northern pole.

### 6. The Phoenician “Wheat Field”

Allen stated that the Phoenicians imagined a large Wheat Field in this region. Later writers used this to construct an elaborate ancient agricultural pedigree.

**Disputed/unverified:** no Phoenician primary text, inscription, or cuneiform star list was supplied by Allen, and no independent ancient attestation was located. Lalande’s own agricultural symbolism is documented and sufficient; the Phoenician claim should remain unverified.

### 7. A northern counterpart to Polophylax

Modern writers have proposed that the circumpolar Harvest Keeper answered the southern **Polophylax**, “Guardian of the Pole.”

**Scholarly speculation:** no statement by Lalande has been found linking the two. Similarity of “guardian” imagery and circumpolar placement is not evidence of intention.

### 8. Lalande avoided placing a living man directly in the sky

A plausible interpretation of the pun, but the 1776 review does not say Lalande was restrained by such a rule. He openly says the name will preserve Messier’s memory. Treat this as reconstruction, not documented motive.

### 9. Lalande invented constellations while drunk

This anecdote appears in modern secondary writing, sometimes attributed to Philip Pugh and sometimes generalized from stories about Lalande’s correspondence.

**Unverified as applied to Custos:** no contemporary witness or Lalande document was located showing that Custos Messium was proposed under intoxication. It should not be used as an explanation of the 1775 figure.

### 10. “Shepherd”

Kendall’s 1845 alternative is isolated. The crook invites the interpretation, but Lalande’s *messier* was a crop guardian, not a shepherd. The name is a nineteenth-century conflation.

### 11. Farmer standing beside, or on, Camelopardalis?

Both descriptions reflect atlas variants. Bode situates the figure close to the Giraffe; Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror* make the keeper appear perched on its back. Artistic overlap is not a precise boundary statement.

### 12. Sickle or crook?

Bode’s figure is commonly described with a staff or crook; Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror* clearly emphasize both crook and sickle. Claims that the sickle was intrinsic to Lalande’s first globe require inspection of a securely dated original globe.

### 13. Which star was brightest: 40 or 50 Cassiopeiae?

Modern astrometry makes **50 Cassiopeiae**, magnitude about 3.95, the brightest star in the usual Bode outline. Some modern Messier literature calls 40 Cassiopeiae the only “noticeable” or principal star, probably following historical descriptions of its membership rather than comparing modern magnitudes. The two statements should not be conflated.

### 14. Did Delporte deliberately draw Cassiopeia around Custos?

John Barentine states that Delporte’s Cassiopeia boundary was carefully drawn to take in almost all the former stars. This is plausible from the overlay, but no Delporte memorandum explicitly saying “to preserve Custos” was located. The claim should be treated as cartographic inference.

### 15. Was it “abolished by the IAU in 1922”?

Substantively yes, procedurally imprecise. The IAU adopted a standard list that excluded it. Custos was already obsolete and was not named in a special abolition resolution.

### 16. Abbreviation and genitive confusion

- **CsM/Cus:** unofficial.
- **Custodis Messium:** grammatically defensible retrospective genitive.
- **Custodis Messoris:** later reformulation, not Lalande’s title.
- **Custos Messius:** error or modernization, not the standard historical form.

### 17. “Messiah’s Guardian”

A modern mistranslation or joke generated by resemblance between *Messium* and “Messiah.” Latin *messium* derives from *messis*, harvest; it has no relation to *Messias*.

### 18. NGC 771 as a nebula

NGC 771 is 50 Cassiopeiae misreported as nebulous. Calling it a genuine Custos nebula perpetuates Herschel’s observational/catalogue error.

### 19. Restoration in 2025

The IAU proper name **Custos** does not reinstate the constellation or its borders. It names one star, BE Camelopardalis. Likewise **Rangifer** names 49 Cassiopeiae and **Gang** names 50 Cassiopeiae.

## Open questions

1. **The exact 1775 star list.** A complete, star-by-star transcription directly from a securely dated first-state Lalande–Lattré globe remains desirable.

2. **The first globe’s iconography.** Did the original keeper carry a sickle, crook, grain sheaf, or some different combination? Later Bode and British images may have elaborated the attributes.

3. **Bode’s letter concordance.** A critical table matching every Bode letter in Custos Messium to Gaia/HIP/HD identifiers would resolve conflicting online lists.

4. **The “Phoenician wheat field.”** Allen’s ultimate source has not been traced. Until a Phoenician, Greco-Roman, or early scholarly citation is produced, it should remain unverified.

5. **Kendall’s quintuple star.** His epoch-dependent coordinates need reduction and comparison with modern multiple-star catalogues.

6. **The Delporte-intention claim.** The modern boundary overlay supports Cassiopeia’s absorption of most figure-stars, but archival evidence for a deliberate effort to preserve the group within Cassiopeia has not been found.

7. **Early vernacular reception.** Italian *Mietitore*, German *Erndtehüter*, English “Shepherd,” and Young’s “Vineyard Keeper” need fuller bibliographical histories distinguishing first printed use from Allen’s later compilation.

8. **Fortin states.** Surviving copies of the 1776, amended 1778, and 1795 French Flamsteed atlas should be collated plate by plate to determine precisely when Custos first entered each impression.

9. **The “830 stars in 109 groups” description.** This figure could not be reconciled with Bode’s 1801 companion catalogue, whose title explicitly advertises 17,240 stars and related objects. It may refer to a particular collection, index, digital chain, or subset rather than *Uranographia* as a whole.
