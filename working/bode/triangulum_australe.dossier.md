# Triangulum Australe: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier uses the following labels:

- **Documented fact** — directly attested in a contemporary chart, catalogue, institutional record, or modern scientific catalogue.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an identification or historical sequence argued from surviving evidence but not stated by the original source.
- **Tradition** — a culturally transmitted interpretation reported by a source, without implying scientific or historical equivalence.
- **Disputed** — competing identifications or attributions remain plausible.
- **Legend/myth** — a sacred, mythographic, or narrative interpretation.
- **Modern invention** — a recent name or symbolism retrospectively attached to the stars.
- **Unverified claim** — repeated in secondary literature but not securely traceable to an appropriate source.

## Basic identification

### Status

**Documented fact:** Triangulum Australe is one of the 88 constellations retained by the International Astronomical Union. It was not abolished. The IAU standardized its name and abbreviation in the constellation reforms initiated at Rome in 1922; Eugène Delporte supplied its exact boundaries, adopted by the IAU and published in 1930.

- Official Latin nominative: **Triangulum Australe**
- Official genitive: **Trianguli Australis**
- IAU abbreviation: **TrA**
- English: **the Southern Triangle**
- Modern Spanish: **Triángulo Austral**
- French: **Triangle austral**
- German: **Südliches Dreieck**
- Italian: **Triangolo Australe**
- Portuguese: **Triângulo Austral**
- Dutch historical form: **Den Zuyder Trianghel** or **Den Zuyden Trianghel**
- Modern Chinese: **南三角座**, *Nán Sānjiǎo Zuò*, “Southern Triangle Constellation”

The IAU’s own constellation list gives *Triangulum Australe*, TrA, “the Southern Triangle,” and the genitive *Trianguli Australis*. Its present boundary chart confirms the neighboring constellations Ara, Norma, Circinus, and Apus. [IAU constellation list and charts](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### Etymology

**Documented fact:** Latin *triangulum* means “triangle”; *australe* is the neuter singular of *australis*, “southern.” The full name therefore means “Southern Triangle,” distinguishing it from ancient northern Triangulum.

*Auster* was the Latin south wind and, by extension, “the south.” “Austral” in European vernaculars descends from this Latin adjective.

### Position, size, and visibility

**Documented fact:** The modern constellation occupies about **110 square degrees**, ranking approximately 83rd in area. Its Delporte polygon has eighteen boundary segments and lies roughly between declinations −60° and −71°. It is circumpolar through much of the Southern Hemisphere and is wholly invisible from most of Europe.

Its extreme southerly position explains several historical absences:

- it is absent from Mesopotamian constellation systems;
- absent from pharaonic Egyptian astronomical ceilings and diagonal-star lists;
- absent from the classical Greek sphere and Ptolemy;
- absent from indigenous Arabic and Persian adaptations of Ptolemy;
- absent from Jewish and medieval Latin star catalogues derived from Greco-Arabic authorities;
- absent from traditional Chinese uranography until the Jesuit introduction of European southern stars.

Those absences are not merely gaps in storytelling: most of the figure lies too far south to have been observed from Babylon, Egypt, Greece, Rome, medieval Persia, northern Arabia, China, or Europe.

### Shape and principal stars

The conspicuous triangle is formed by:

- **α Trianguli Australis — Atria**
- **β Trianguli Australis**
- **γ Trianguli Australis**

Some modern line diagrams insert ε TrA along the β–γ side, but the historically defining figure is the bright three-star triangle. A recent Royal Astronomical Society of Canada asterism handbook describes the standard plotted outline as α–β–ε–γ, while also recognizing the older three-star figure. This is a difference in modern stick-figure convention, not a boundary dispute.

### Modern boundaries

**Documented fact:** Delporte’s boundaries follow right ascension and declination lines for equator and equinox **B1875.0**, the same reference framework used by Benjamin Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina*. Precession makes them look slightly tilted on modern J2000 maps, but the legal astronomical boundary remains the precessed form of the B1875 polygon. [Delporte, *Délimitation scientifique des constellations*](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

Present neighbors:

- Ara to the north
- Norma to the northwest
- Circinus to the west
- Apus to the south and east

### “Where its stars went”

Not applicable: Triangulum Australe survived the IAU reform. Stars inside Delporte’s polygon remain in TrA regardless of older artistic figures. Conversely, stars outside the polygon cannot now be assigned to it merely because an early engraver placed them inside a triangle or attached line.

## Names through time

### Babylonian and Egyptian

**Finding: no antecedent established.**

No Babylonian constellation or Egyptian decan can be securely identified with α, β, and γ TrA. These stars culminate too far south for useful observation from Babylon and northern Egypt.

Claims that a three-branched Egyptian sacred tree, cynocephalus, or hieroglyph was “formed by the Southern Triangle” occur in speculative nineteenth-century comparative mythology, notably in discussions connecting Chinese characters, Egyptian motifs, and Firmicus. They are **not** supported by Egyptian astronomical texts and should be classified as **antiquarian speculation**, not an Egyptian name for TrA.

### Greek

**Finding: none.**

There is no ancient Greek name because there was no Greek constellation here. Triangulum Australe must not be confused with the northern **Τρίγωνον**, *Trigōnon*, Ptolemy’s Triangulum.

### Latin

Historical Latin forms include:

- **Triangulus Antarcticus** — Plancius’s 1589 globe
- **Triangulum Australe** — Bayer, 1603, and the permanent standard
- **Triangulum Austrinum** — occasional early-modern variant
- **Triangulum Antarcticum** — grammatical normalization sometimes used by later writers
- **Triangulum Australe seu Libella** — Bode, 1801, “Southern Triangle or Level”

The 1589 *antarcticus* means “opposite the Bear,” hence southern or south-polar; *australe* means southern.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

**Finding: no indigenous Arabic or al-Ṣūfī name.**

Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābitah* (“Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars,” c. 964) reproduces and revises the Ptolemaic constellations. Its Triangle is northern Triangulum, not Triangulum Australe.

The forms sometimes attached to the southern figure in early-modern European compilations—such as **Almutabet algenubi**—appear to be Neo-Latin attempts to manufacture or transfer an Arabic-sounding “southern triangle” name. Richard Hinckley Allen reported Caesius’s form but supplied no medieval Arabic observation establishing an asterism in these stars. It is therefore **documented as an early-modern European label but unverified as indigenous Arabic astronomy**. [Allen, *Star Names*, p. 417](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Triangulum_Australe%2A.html)

No Bedouin asterism securely composed of α, β, and γ TrA was located. This is unsurprising because the group is very low or invisible from much of Arabia.

Modern Arabic **المثلث الجنوبي**, *al-muthallath al-janūbī*, is a translation of the international constellation name, not an ancient Bedouin survival.

### Persian

**Finding: no premodern Persian name located.**

Persian astronomers worked substantially through the Ptolemaic-Arabic catalogue tradition; TrA lay outside that inherited sphere. Modern Persian **مثلث جنوبی**, *mosallas-e jonubi*, is a translation, “southern triangle.”

### Hebrew and Jewish traditions

**Finding: no ancient or medieval Hebrew asterism securely attached to these stars.**

Biblical *mazzaroth*, rabbinic astronomy, medieval Hebrew translations of Arabic astronomical works, and later Kabbalistic zodiacal correspondences concern the zodiac and northern inherited constellations. No primary Hebrew source was found mapping Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—or a Kabbalistic triad—to α, β, and γ TrA before Christian European star allegory.

Modern Hebrew **המשולש הדרומי**, *ha-Meshulash ha-Dromi*, is a translation of the modern constellation name.

### Dutch

Frederick de Houtman’s 1603 catalogue calls it **Den Zuyder Trianghel**, “the Southern Triangle.” His book says that its southern-star positions had been improved and augmented on Sumatra. The catalogue is the oldest surviving printed coordinate catalogue of the twelve Dutch southern constellations. [Utrecht University copy of de Houtman, 1603](https://dbc.library.uu.nl/handle/20.500.14918/33112)

### Spanish and Portuguese

- Spanish: **Triángulo Austral**
- Portuguese: **Triângulo Austral**

These are translations of the Latin. No independent Iberian constellation-name predating the Dutch figure was recovered, although Portuguese and Italian navigators certainly observed southern stars before the Dutch surveys.

### Bode’s labels

On plate XX of the 1801 *Uranographia*, Bode labels the figure:

> **TRIANGUL. AUSTRALE seu LIBELLA**

“Southern Triangle, or Level.”

**Documented fact:** *Libella* means a small balance, level, or leveling instrument. Bode followed Lacaille’s visual reinterpretation of the constellation as a surveyor’s level. It was not the same instrument as neighboring **Norma** and **Regula**, which Bode plotted separately.

Bode’s German-language explanatory tradition uses **das südliche Dreieck** and the level-equivalent **Wasserwaage** or **Setzwaage**, depending on edition or paraphrase.

The user-supplied phrase “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be reconciled with Bode’s 1801 catalogue. The surviving title and catalogue explicitly claim **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters**. Bode’s atlas contains twenty large engraved plates and more than one hundred constellation figures, including obsolete inventions. “830 in 109 groups” may belong to a collection catalogue, teaching set, or a different Bode publication, but it is **unverified for the 1801 Uranographia**. [Bode’s accompanying catalogue](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

The customary catalogue set has ten surviving Bayer-style Greek designations: α through κ, omitting a currently used λ and with the complications around η.

### Atria

**Documented fact:** The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Atria** for α TrA in 2016. It is not Arabic, Greek, Aboriginal, or ancient Latin. It is a modern contraction of **A**lpha **Tri**anguli **A**ustralis.

The name is traceable at least to the *American Nautical Almanac for 1952*. Its creation belongs to a twentieth-century navigational practice of supplying short names to bright navigation stars. [Atria historical entry](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Atria); [SIMBAD α TrA](https://simbad.u-strasbg.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=Alpha+Trianguli+Australis)

**Contested/popular error:** Online etymologies sometimes associate *Atria* with Latin *atrium*, the entrance court of a Roman house, or with the plural anatomical *atria* of the heart. These are wordplay, not the star-name’s derivation.

### “Betria” and “Gatria”

**Modern invention/unofficial usage:** “Betria” for β TrA and “Gatria” for γ TrA are occasionally formed by analogy with Atria. Neither is an IAU-approved traditional proper name. Their appearance in modern astrology or hobbyist lists must not be read backward into navigation history.

### Bayer letters

Despite being called “Bayer designations,” the Greek letters in TrA were **not assigned by Bayer in 1603**. Bayer plotted the new southern figures but did not letter their individual stars. Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille assigned Greek letters during his Cape survey, published preliminarily in 1756 and posthumously in *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* (1763).

Lacaille assigned letters through λ, with two stars sharing η. Later catalogue revision retained η¹ while the other is generally identified by an HD or HR number; λ fell out of regular use because of its faintness or later catalogue rationalization. This is a standard example of “Bayer designation” meaning the naming system, not necessarily Bayer’s personal act. [Lacaille catalogue and maps](https://pbarbier.com/catalogs/lacaille/lacaille.html)

### Flamsteed numbers

**Documented fact:** Triangulum Australe has no standard Flamsteed-number sequence. Flamsteed’s numbered system was based on stars observable and catalogued from England, and the far-southern group was beyond effective observation. Statements such as “1 Trianguli Australis” should therefore be checked for confusion with northern Triangulum or another catalogue.

### Navigational use

The three bright stars were called the **seamen’s Triangle Stars** or **navigators’ triangle** in later English literature. Allen records this usage but does not establish its first nautical manual.

Atria itself is one of the 57 standard navigational stars in modern nautical practice. The triangle is a conspicuous orientation marker, but it is not as direct or canonical a south-pole finder as Crux plus α and β Centauri. Claims that “Triangulum Australe points exactly to the south celestial pole” are overstated: it assists orientation in the southern sky but does not define a single standard pole-line comparable to the long axis of Crux.

## Internal asterisms

1. **The Southern Triangle proper** — α, β, γ TrA.
2. **Four-point plotted outline** — α, β, ε, γ in some modern charts.
3. **The Three Patriarchs** — Christian reinterpretation of the three bright stars; discussed below.
4. **Seamen’s or Navigators’ Triangle** — nautical descriptive name, date uncertain.
5. **The surveyor’s level** — Lacaille and Bode’s eighteenth-century instrumental reinterpretation.

## Deep-sky objects

There are **no Messier objects** in TrA. M33, the famous “Triangulum Galaxy,” lies in northern **Triangulum**, not Triangulum Australe.

Principal NGC and IC objects inside the Delporte boundaries include:

|---|---|---|
| NGC 6025 | Open cluster | The constellation’s showpiece cluster, integrated magnitude about 5.1 |
| NGC 5979 | Planetary nebula | Recorded by John Herschel at the Cape, 1835 |
| NGC 5844 | Planetary nebula | Faint, extended planetary |
| NGC 5938 | Spiral galaxy | Faint southern galaxy |
| NGC 6183 | Galaxy | Faint galaxy |
| IC 4571 | Galaxy | Faint |
| IC 4584 | Galaxy | Faint |
| IC 4585 | Galaxy | Faint |
| IC 4595 | Galaxy | Faint |

The revised NGC/IC material lists NGC 6025, NGC 5844, NGC 5979 and the faint galaxies separately. [Revised NGC/IC list for TrA](https://deepskycorner.ch/catalogs.en.php?cat=NGCIC&const=TrA)

Additional notable objects include:

- **Henize 2-138**, a compact planetary nebula catalogued in Karl Henize’s southern emission-object work.
- **ESO 137-001**, the “Jellyfish Galaxy,” whose spectacular gas tail is produced by ram-pressure stripping in the Norma Cluster. It lies near the modern TrA–Norma region and is assigned to TrA by current coordinate boundaries.
- The **Great Attractor** direction straddles Norma and Triangulum Australe. It is a large-scale gravitational region, not a single object named after the constellation.

No nebula, cluster, or galaxy generally bears “Triangulum Australe” as an official proper name comparable to the Triangulum Galaxy. Catalogued objects use TrA adjectivally only when positional nomenclature calls for it.

## Variable stars, novae, and eruptive objects

- **R TrA** — classical Cepheid, approximately magnitude 6.4–6.9, period about 3.389 days.
- **S TrA** — classical Cepheid, approximately 6.1–6.8, period about 6.323 days.
- **U TrA** — classical Cepheid, approximately 7.5–8.3, period about 2.568 days.
- **RT TrA** — unusual carbon-rich Cepheid, roughly magnitude 9.2–10.0, period about 1.95 days.
- **X TrA** — red carbon variable, mean naked-eye-borderline magnitude, with long periods near 385 and 455 days.
- **EK TrA** — SU UMa-type dwarf nova, noticed in 1978 and published as a new dwarf nova in 1980.
- **FL TrA** — SU UMa-type dwarf nova; superhumps were measured during the 2006 outburst. [Photometric study of FL TrA](https://arxiv.org/abs/0804.3126)
- **NR TrA / Nova Trianguli Australis 2008** — slow nova reaching approximately magnitude 8.4 in April 2008.

No securely documented historical naked-eye supernova is known in TrA. No recognized Galactic supernova remnant in the constellation has supplied a classical historical sighting.

## Meteor showers

**Documented negative finding:** No established annual meteor shower is officially associated with Triangulum Australe in standard modern lists. “Triangulum” meteor names elsewhere normally refer to northern Triangulum. Apparent isolated radiants reported near TrA should not be promoted to established showers without an International Astronomical Union Meteor Data Center designation.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1500: no Western constellation

**Documented fact:** TrA is absent from the Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, medieval Latin, and traditional Chinese canonical spheres.

Ptolemy’s *Almagest* catalogued stars of Centaurus and Ara farther north, but not the three-star Southern Triangle as a constellation. Precession does not bring α, β, and γ TrA far enough north to make a convincing classical identification.

### 1501–1504: Amerigo Vespucci’s southern stars

In *Mundus Novus*, circulated in print around 1503–1504, Amerigo Vespucci described unfamiliar southern stars and said that he had made fuller records in a small book then held by King Manuel I of Portugal.

One surviving passage describes three stars arranged in a triangle. Its identification is **disputed**:

- it may be α, β, γ TrA;
- it may be a group in Apus;
- another triangular diagram in the tradition may fit Chamaeleon better.

The text does not name “Triangulum Australe,” supply unambiguous coordinates, or inaugurate a continuous cartographic tradition. Thus:

- “Vespucci saw a southern triangle” — **documented**;
- “Vespucci described these exact three stars” — **scholarly reconstruction/disputed**;
- “Vespucci invented Triangulum Australe” — **unsupported overstatement**.

The surviving English translation preserves Vespucci’s statement that he had carefully noted many southern stars in a separate book. [Markham edition of Vespucci’s letters](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/36924/36924-h/36924-h.htm)

### 1516: Andrea Corsali

Corsali’s published letter and southern-sky diagram document Portuguese-route knowledge of southern stars. His famous secure contribution is an early printed representation of the Southern Cross; he did not clearly delineate or name Triangulum Australe. [State Library of New South Wales, Corsali letter](https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/collection-items/lettera-di-andrea-corsali-allo-illustrissimo-signore-duco-iuliano-de-medici)

### Portuguese observations before Plancius

**Documented general fact:** Portuguese and Italian mariners had observed southern skies for navigation before the Dutch voyages.

**Open/disputed:** No surviving Portuguese chart or catalogue has been demonstrated to name α–β–γ TrA as a formal constellation before Plancius. Priority assertions based simply on Portuguese presence in southern latitudes confuse seeing stars with defining a constellation.

### 1589: Plancius and van Langren

**Documented fact:** Petrus Plancius and Jacob Floris van Langren’s 32.5-cm celestial globe includes **Triangulus Antarcticus**. It is inaccurately positioned south of Argo Navis and effectively inverted relative to the modern triangle.

This is the **earliest surviving cartographic attestation of a named southern triangle**.

It predates Keyser’s 1595 departure. It therefore cannot have been based on Keyser’s voyage observations.

### 1595–1597: Keyser and the first Dutch voyage

Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser, trained or instructed in astronomical observation in Plancius’s circle, sailed on the first Dutch expedition to the East Indies. Frederick de Houtman also participated.

Keyser observed southern stars with navigational instruments, probably including an astrolabe or cross-staff. He died at Bantam in September 1596. His observations returned to the Netherlands with the surviving expedition in 1597.

### 1597/1598: Plancius’s corrected globe

Plancius’s globe produced with Jodocus Hondius in late 1597 or 1598 contains the twelve familiar new southern constellations in reasonably accurate positions, including the corrected Southern Triangle.

This resolves the apparent “1589 versus 1598” contradiction:

- **1589** — first named depiction, inaccurate and pre-Keyser;
- **1597/98** — first accurate surviving depiction based on expeditionary measurement.

### 1598–1602: Frederick de Houtman’s second observations

During his second eastern voyage and captivity on Sumatra, de Houtman revised and expanded the southern-star catalogue.

### 1603: de Houtman and Bayer

Two foundational publications appeared in 1603:

1. De Houtman printed his catalogue as an appendix to his Malay and Malagasy dictionary. It lists **Den Zuyder Trianghel** and gives stellar positions.
2. Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* presented the twelve southern constellations to a wide European readership on plate 49 and used **Triangulum Australe**.

Bayer credited “Petrus Theodorus,” conventionally understood as a Latinized form of Pieter Dircksz Keyser. Bayer did not assign the present Greek letters to the TrA stars. [Digitized Bayer plate 49](https://dbc.library.uu.nl/handle/20.500.14918/33114)

### Early seventeenth century: attribution controversy

Contemporaries and later historians variously credited:

- Keyser as observer and originator;
- de Houtman as observer/cataloguer;
- Plancius as designer and publisher;
- Bayer as introducer.

The most defensible reconstruction is collaborative:

- Plancius had already imagined an inaccurate Antarctic triangle in 1589;
- Keyser supplied systematic voyage observations in 1595–96;
- de Houtman participated and later revised/published them;
- Plancius formed or stabilized the constellation scheme on globes;
- Bayer disseminated its Latin form in an atlas.

A modern study emphasizes that attribution disputes began early and that star-chart authorship should not be collapsed into a single “discoverer.” [Verbunt and van Gent, early southern catalogues](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2011/06/aa16795-11.pdf)

### 1624–1627: Bartsch and Schiller

Jacob Bartsch helped circulate the Plancian southern constellations. He did not invent TrA.

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* (Augsburg, 1627) replaced pagan constellation imagery with biblical subjects. The southern constellations were collectively mapped into biblical genealogical symbolism. For Triangulum Australe, later catalogues describe the three bright stars as associated with the **three patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob**, while Schiller’s chart labels the relevant southern grouping through the Hebrew-letter/genealogical scheme **Signum Tau**.

A frequent error says that Schiller depicted a single saint here. Unlike his replacement of northern Triangulum with the papal tiara of St Peter, TrA belongs to the southern patriarchal/genealogical program. The exact one-to-one assignment “Atria = Abraham” is more clearly stated by later writers than by Schiller’s surviving plate labels.

### 1649–1750: normal atlas reception

The figure appears as a simple triangle on:

- Plancius-derived globe gores, including a 1649 example with the misprint **Trangulum Austr.**
- Edmond Halley’s *Catalogus Stellarum Australium* (1679)
- Hevelius’s *Prodromus Astronomiae* and *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* (published 1690)
- Carel Allard’s southern planisphere (1709)
- John Senex’s planispheres (1721/22)
- John Flamsteed’s *Atlas Coelestis* (1729), generally as **Triangulum**
- Richard Cushee’s pocket globe (1731)
- Doppelmayr’s *Atlas Coelestis* (1742)
- Tobias Conrad Lotter’s planisphere (c. 1750)
- John Bevis’s unpublished or posthumously issued *Uranographia Britannica*

Hevelius’s northern *Triangulum Majus* and *Triangulum Minus* should not be confused with southern Triangulum Australe.

### 1751–1756: Lacaille

At the Cape of Good Hope, Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille accurately observed the southern sky and assigned Greek letters to TrA’s stars.

On his 1756 planisphere he renamed or reinterpreted it:

> **le Triangle Austral ou le Niveau**

“The Southern Triangle or the Level.”

The plumb line and triangle made it a surveyor’s spirit or plumb level. Lacaille coordinated it iconographically with:

- Norma/Regula, the square and rule;
- Circinus, the compass;
- TrA/Level, the leveling instrument.

The statement that Lacaille “replaced Triangulum Australe with Norma” is false. Both constellations occur side by side; he only superimposed a tool interpretation on TrA.

### 1763: *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*

Lacaille’s posthumous catalogue established the lettered stars and much improved southern positions.

### 1782: Bode’s smaller atlas

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln* was based substantially on the Flamsteed/Fortin atlas tradition, enlarged and adapted for German readers. It included the accepted southern constellations and prepared the visual vocabulary he expanded in 1801. [Heidelberg copy and description](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/)

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode published the most elaborate culmination of the pictorial star-atlas tradition:

- twenty large copperplate maps;
- more than one hundred ancient, modern, and proposed constellation figures;
- an accompanying German/French catalogue claiming 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters.

Triangulum Australe appears on plate XX as a geometrical level with plumb line, labeled **Triangul. Australe seu Libella**. Norma and Regula remain distinct above it, disproving later claims that Bode’s *Libella* was simply another name for Norma.

Bode’s text is descriptive rather than mythographic: because the figure is modern, he gives position, composition, names, and catalogue data rather than a classical catasterism. [Digital *Uranographia*](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [digital accompanying text](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

### Nineteenth-century atlases

Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* (1822) was designed primarily for constellations visible from Britain and did not give TrA a dedicated pictorial plate comparable to northern constellations.

Likewise, *Urania’s Mirror* (1824/25), derived from Jamieson, has no separate Triangulum Australe card. Card 5’s **Triangula** refers to the northern triangles associated with Andromeda and Gloria Frederici, not TrA. Claims that an *Urania’s Mirror* triangle card depicts Triangulum Australe are therefore **misidentifications**. [Royal Astronomical Society, *Urania’s Mirror*](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror)

Elijah Burritt’s northern-audience *Geography of the Heavens* similarly gives limited attention to the far south. Argelander’s and Heis’s scientific atlases suppressed elaborate figure art in favor of stars and coordinate grids; their observational latitude also limited independent southern coverage.

### 1877–1879: Gould and the *Uranometria Argentina*

Benjamin Apthorp Gould charted the southern sky systematically from Córdoba. His catalogue and atlas regularized many southern letter designations and drew explicit constellation boundaries. Gould counted **46 stars down to approximately seventh magnitude** in TrA, according to Allen.

These provisional boundaries influenced Delporte but were not identical to the final IAU polygon.

### 1889: Brazilian national flag

The flag adopted with the Brazilian Republic depicts the sky over Rio de Janeiro associated with 15 November 1889, conventionally viewed as though from outside the celestial sphere.

The three principal TrA stars represent Brazil’s three southern states:

- α TrA/Atria — Rio Grande do Sul
- β TrA — Santa Catarina
- γ TrA — Paraná

This is official modern civic symbolism, not indigenous or ancient myth. [Brazilian National Observatory guide](https://www.gov.br/observatorio/pt-br/assuntos/areas-de-atuacao/divulgacao-e-popularizacao-da-ciencia/revistas/pdf/bandeiras.pdf)

### 1922: IAU retention

At its Rome General Assembly, the IAU adopted the standard list and abbreviations. Triangulum Australe was retained as **TrA**, distinct from northern Triangulum, **Tri**.

### 1928–1930: Delporte boundaries

The IAU commissioned Eugène Delporte to complete rational boundaries along B1875 right-ascension and declination lines. His *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* appeared in 1930.

No wholesale transfer of TrA’s three principal stars occurred. The new limits clarified the crowded interface with Ara, Norma, Circinus, and Apus and fixed ownership of faint stars and variable-star designations.

### 2016: Atria standardized

The IAU Working Group on Star Names officially approved **Atria** for α TrA. This standardized a twentieth-century navigational contraction rather than reviving an ancient name.

## Classical myths: negative dossier

### Why there is no Greek or Roman catasterism

Triangulum Australe is modern and was not visible as a complete group from the principal centers of classical Mediterranean astronomy. Therefore there are no authentic myths or catasterisms for it in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome
- Hyginus’s *De Astronomia/Poetica Astronomica*
- Hyginus’s *Fabulae*
- Aratus’s *Phaenomena*
- the scholia to Aratus
- Germanicus’s *Aratea* or its scholia
- Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* or *Fasti*
- Manilius’s *Astronomica*
- Nonnus’s *Dionysiaca*
- Pseudo-Apollodorus’s *Bibliotheca*
- Pausanias’s *Description of Greece*
- the Vatican Mythographers

No god, hero, beast, or object in those works is Triangulum Australe.

### Confusion with northern Triangulum

Stories of:

- Sicily or the triangular island Trinacria;
- the Nile delta;
- Demeter or Ceres;
- the triangular Greek letter delta;
- Hermes placing a triangle in the sky;

belong to **northern Triangulum** in post-classical discussions of its catasterism. Moving them to Triangulum Australe is a modern category error.

Consequently, an “exhaustive classical myth” section for TrA consists of this negative finding. Supplying northern-Triangle myths here would fabricate antiquity for a constellation created in early-modern Europe.

## Invention and reception

### What did the constellation honor?

Unlike most of the Dutch twelve, Triangulum Australe did not represent an exotic animal encountered or reported during East Indian voyages. It was a geometric recognition figure.

It did not originally honor:

- a king;
- a patron;
- a saint;
- a ship;
- a machine;
- a specific voyage.

Lacaille later turned it into a surveyor’s level, bringing it into his Enlightenment ensemble of scientific instruments. Bode preserved that secondary instrumental meaning.

### Division of credit

The phrase “one of the twelve constellations of Keyser and de Houtman” is conventional but incomplete.

A source-sensitive allocation is:

- **Plancius:** first surviving named depiction, 1589; later organizer and globe publisher.
- **Keyser:** principal observer on the 1595–97 voyage; credited as Petrus Theodorus by Bayer.
- **Frederick de Houtman:** participant, reviser, and first printer of the coordinate catalogue.
- **Bayer:** first major printed atlas dissemination under the enduring Latin name.
- **Lacaille:** accurate Cape remeasurement, surviving Greek letters, and the level iconography.
- **Bode:** monumental synthesis, explicitly *Triangulum Australe seu Libella*.
- **IAU/Delporte:** official retention, abbreviation, and exact modern territory.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Chinese

### Before European contact

**Documented negative:** α, β, and γ TrA were not part of the traditional Three Enclosures and Twenty-Eight Mansions. They lay beyond the effective southern limit of the classical Chinese sky.

### Jesuit-Ming/Qing incorporation

During the late Ming astronomical reform, Xu Guangqi and Jesuit collaborators introduced additional far-southern asterisms from European charts. In the revised Chinese system TrA became:

- **三角形**, *Sānjiǎoxíng*, “Triangle”
- within the **近南極星區**, *Jìnnánjíxīngōu*, “Near-South-Pole star region”

Star assignments:

- γ TrA — 三角形一, “Triangle, first star”
- β TrA — 三角形二
- α TrA — 三角形三
- δ TrA — 三角形增一, “first added star”
- ε TrA — 三角形增二

This is a Chinese-language incorporation of a European constellation, not an independent ancient Chinese triangle. [Chinese astronomy mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangulum_Australe_in_Chinese_astronomy); for the wider Jesuit reform, [Elman, *On Their Own Terms*](https://www.princeton.edu/~elman/documents/Benjamin%20A%20%20Elman%20On%20Their%20Own%20Terms%20-%20Science%20in%20China%201550-1900%20%282005%29.pdf)

Allen’s “San Kiō Hung” appears to be an obsolete Wade-Giles-like transcription or corruption of a Chinese expression for the triangular figure. It is not the standard modern romanization and should not be treated as an ancient name.

## Indian

**Finding: no secure classical Indian identification.**

The stars lie far south of the ecliptic and outside the nakṣatra belt. No Vedic nakṣatra is formed from them. Indian Ocean navigators may have seen the triangle, but no Sanskrit asterism securely matching α–β–γ TrA was located in the consulted sources.

## Aboriginal Australian: Wardaman

**Tradition:** Bill Yidumduma Harney and Hugh Cairns report that in Wardaman sky knowledge, stars in the TrA region form part of the tail of **Gorrondolmi**, the Rainbow Serpent, extending across the southern sky toward Scorpius. Its seasonal position is connected with lightning and the approach of the wet season.

This is not simply “the Wardaman name for Triangulum Australe.” It is a larger Indigenous sky figure crossing several Western constellation territories. The mapping is reported from a named modern tradition-bearer and must not be generalized to all Aboriginal Australian cultures.

## Christianized sky

### Caesius

Philippus Caesius associated the three bright stars with the **Three Patriarchs: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob**. Later summaries specify Atria as Abraham, but the exact stellar allocation requires caution unless checked against the relevant Caesius edition.

This is **early-modern Christian allegory**, not Hebrew antiquity.

### Julius Schiller

Schiller’s 1627 *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* attempted a systematic biblical replacement of pagan celestial imagery. TrA entered the southern genealogical scheme, conventionally summarized as the Three Patriarchs or **Signum Tau**.

Confusion frequently arises with Schiller’s northern Triangulum, transformed into the **Mitra Pontificalis S. Petri**, the papal tiara of St Peter. That image does not replace Triangulum Australe.

## Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No premodern Kabbalistic text was found mapping the *sefirot*, patriarchs, Hebrew letters, or divine names specifically onto α–β–γ TrA. Schiller’s Hebrew-letter framework was a Christian uranographic construction.

Modern occult diagrams that call the stars Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob derive from Caesius/Schiller or later constellation lore and are not evidence of Jewish tradition.

## Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No medieval alchemical or Hermetic source was found assigning TrA a metal, planet, talisman, or operation. Its absence from the classical and Arabic spheres precludes it from the medieval fixed-star magical corpus.

Nineteenth-century attempts to connect it with:

- a mystical three-branched tree;
- a solar cynocephalus;
- Chinese character origins;
- ancient Egyptian triads;

are speculative comparative symbolism, not historical star lore.

## Astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* assigns planetary natures to stars and regions of the traditional constellations but cannot mention TrA, which was not part of the Ptolemaic sphere.

The fifteen **Behenian fixed stars** do not include Atria, β TrA, or γ TrA.

Neither the Arabic 28 lunar mansions nor the Indian 27/28 nakṣatras passes through these stars: lunar mansions follow the Moon’s ecliptic path, whereas TrA is near declination −60° to −70°.

Modern tropical fixed-star astrology sometimes assigns effects to Atria, “Betria,” or the constellation according to zodiacal longitude. These are **modern astrological inventions**, primarily derived from twentieth-century handbooks and websites, not continuous ancient doctrine. The use of “Betria” is especially diagnostic of modern analogy rather than inherited nomenclature.

## Boundary history and neighboring constellations

### Ptolemy

No TrA territory existed. Ptolemy’s southern limit left these stars uncatalogued as a constellation. It is therefore misleading to say TrA “took stars from Centaurus” in classical antiquity; its principal stars were not securely listed as parts of Ptolemy’s Centaur.

### Plancius 1589

The erroneous *Triangulus Antarcticus* was placed south of Argo Navis. Its drawn location cannot be interpreted as a precise modern ownership boundary.

### Plancius 1597/98 and Bayer 1603

The triangle was relocated to the present region between Centaurus/Ara and the newly created Apus/Chamaeleon complex. Early charts used figures, not legally exhaustive borders, so peripheral stars could appear ambiguously attached.

### Hevelius

Hevelius preserved it as a small three-star triangle. His atlas did not create modern borders.

### Lacaille

Lacaille’s new constellations **Norma** and **Circinus** narrowed and organized the previously informal sky immediately northwest and west of TrA. His instrument ensemble created the enduring neighborhood: level, square/rule, and compasses.

The claim that he abolished TrA in favor of Norma is contradicted by his own maps.

### Bode

Bode drew relatively explicit constellation regions and retained both TrA/Libella and the neighboring Lacaille instruments. Artistic plumb lines extend visually, but they are not equivalent to modern boundaries.

### Gould

Gould’s Córdoba survey regularized southern star ownership and supplied boundary precedents. His assignments influenced Delporte.

### Delporte

Delporte fixed the final polygon. The boundaries resolved ambiguous faint-star territory against Ara, Norma, Circinus, and Apus. The three defining stars remained together.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas

**Negative finding:** The Farnese Atlas, a Roman sculpture preserving a classical celestial globe, cannot depict TrA because the constellation did not exist in the classical sphere.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

**Negative finding:** Illustrated manuscripts of al-Ṣūfī depict northern Triangulum but not Triangulum Australe. Images labeled merely “Triangulum” in al-Ṣūfī collections should not be used as TrA iconography.

### Dürer, 1515

Dürer’s northern and southern celestial maps reproduce the classical/Ptolemaic sphere. They do not contain TrA.

### Plancius–van Langren globe, Amsterdam, 1589

Earliest surviving image: a simple, incorrectly placed and inverted **Triangulus Antarcticus**.

### Plancius–Hondius globe, Amsterdam, 1597/98

Corrected triangle using the Dutch voyage observations; part of the twelve-constellation southern program.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Plate 49 of *Uranometria* shows the southern constellations. TrA appears as an unornamented geometrical triangle, with α Centauri nearby. No mythic person inhabits it. [Bayer plate](https://dbc.library.uu.nl/handle/20.500.14918/33114)

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

The pagan/geometric sky is absorbed into Schiller’s Christianized biblical scheme. The iconography is genealogical and symbolic rather than observational.

### Unidentified seventeenth-century maps: Ceneus and Siren

A puzzling pair of anonymous Leiden maps replaced Chamaeleon, Musca, and TrA with **Ceneus** and **Siren**. These maps are not evidence that TrA was generally abolished; they appear to be an isolated experimental recasting. Their author and date remain uncertain. [Van Gent, “A Pair of Puzzling Star Maps”](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/celestia/puzzlingstarmaps_main.htm)

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

A simple triangle marked *Triangulum Australe* or abbreviated forms. Unlike Hevelius’s elaborate mythological figures, it remained geometric.

### Lacaille, Paris/Cape observations, 1756

Depicted as **le Niveau**, a surveyor’s level with a suspended plumb line, coordinated with Norma and Circinus.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Plate XX gives the most elaborate canonical tool-image: an outlined triangle/level, plumb line and bob, with the caption **Triangul. Australe seu Libella**. A digitized historical comparison is available through the Tartu-derived atlas presentation. [Bode historical image](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/triangulumaustrale.htm)

### Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

No dedicated southern-Triangle plate. The “Triangula” on *Urania’s Mirror* card 5 is northern and must not be cited as TrA.

### Scientific atlases after 1850

In Argelander, Heis, Gould, and later atlases, pictorial imagery increasingly yields to coordinate grids, star symbols, abbreviated names, and eventually geometric boundaries.

### Coins, cathedral portals, and ceilings

No securely identified ancient coin, classical ceiling, or medieval cathedral portal depicts Triangulum Australe as such. Triangular symbols on such objects cannot be identified with this modern far-southern constellation without labels or a matching star map.

The Brazilian flag is its best-established monumental civic representation.

No major premodern poem, canonical painting, musical work, building, ship, or spacecraft mission was found whose historically documented namesake is specifically Triangulum Australe rather than northern Triangulum or the generic geometric triangle.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was it invented in 1589 or 1598?

**Resolution:** both dates describe different milestones.

- 1589: Plancius’s inaccurate *Triangulus Antarcticus*.
- 1597/98: corrected Plancius/Hondius form based on Dutch observations.
- 1603: first major atlas publication and first printed surviving coordinate catalogue.

Calling 1598 the “first appearance” ignores the 1589 globe; calling 1589 the fully modern constellation ignores its serious positional error.

### 2. Did Vespucci discover it?

**Disputed:** Vespucci described triangular southern groups, but the surviving words and diagrams do not uniquely identify α–β–γ TrA. The candidate may instead be in Apus or Chamaeleon.

The first secure named representation remains Plancius’s 1589 globe.

### 3. Keyser, de Houtman, Plancius, or Bayer?

No single verb captures all four roles. “Discovered by Bayer” is false in an observational sense; “invented by Keyser” neglects the 1589 globe; “invented by de Houtman” neglects Keyser and Plancius; “invented solely by Plancius” neglects the observational catalogue underlying the accurate form.

### 4. Was Plancius’s 1589 triangle the same constellation?

**Disputed but conventionally accepted as the precursor:** it bore the appropriate conceptual name but was incorrectly located. Some specialists describe it as “another” southern triangle rather than the same accurately identified stellar group. It is safest to call it the first named precursor.

### 5. Did Lacaille replace it with Norma?

No. Lacaille depicted TrA as a level and Norma as a separate square/rule. Bode’s plate preserves both.

### 6. Is Libella another name for Norma?

No in Bode’s usage. His label explicitly reads *Triangulum Australe seu Libella*. Norma and Regula are separate nearby instruments.

### 7. Is Atria ancient?

No. It is a twentieth-century contraction, attested in navigational literature and standardized by the IAU in 2016.

### 8. Does Atria mean “heart chamber”?

No historically. Its identity with Latin/anatomical *atria* is accidental wordplay.

### 9. Are Betria and Gatria traditional?

No. They are unofficial modern analogical formations.

### 10. Are the letters really Bayer’s?

Not personally. Lacaille assigned them. “Bayer designation” names the system.

### 11. Does *Urania’s Mirror* depict it?

No dedicated card does. “Triangula” on card 5 refers to northern figures.

### 12. Does al-Ṣūfī depict it?

No. The frequently reproduced al-Ṣūfī “Triangle” is northern Triangulum.

### 13. Did ancient Arabs call it *Almutabet algenubi*?

The form is documented in early-modern European constellation lore but not securely traced to an indigenous Arabic catalogue. Treat it as a Neo-Arabic or corrupted European label unless a manuscript witness is produced.

### 14. Is it one of the ancient Chinese asterisms?

No. Chinese **三角形** belongs to the late-Ming/Jesuit extension of the southern sky.

### 15. Is it an ancient Egyptian tree or cynocephalus?

No credible Egyptological evidence supports this. The claim belongs to speculative nineteenth-century comparative symbolism.

### 16. Does it have classical mythology?

No. Mythic accounts transferred from northern Triangulum are errors.

### 17. Does it point to the south celestial pole?

Only loosely as a recognizable southern orientation figure. Standard precise naked-eye procedures use Crux and the Centauri pointers. Describing TrA as a perfect pole pointer exaggerates its navigational function.

### 18. “Triangulum Australis” versus “Triangulum Australe”

The official nominative is **Triangulum Australe**, because *triangulum* is neuter. **Trianguli Australis** is the genitive. “Triangulum Australis” mixes cases and is a frequent catalogue or web error, though it appeared in some older reference tables.

### 19. The “830 stars in 109 groups” claim

This could not be verified for Bode’s 1801 atlas or catalogue. The primary title claims 17,240 entries. It may describe a particular collection, index, educational chain, or abridged atlas, but should not be presented as a statistic of the *Uranographia* without a separate catalogue citation.

## Open questions

1. Which exact stars did Vespucci intend in each triangular passage and diagram? Modern identifications remain dependent on imperfect textual transmission and approximate geometry.

2. Did Plancius derive his 1589 Antarctic triangle from an identifiable Portuguese chart, a report by Vespucci or another navigator, or independent geometrical invention? No conclusive intermediate document survives.

3. How much of the 1595–97 catalogue was personally observed by Keyser, and how much by Frederick de Houtman? The surviving printed catalogue reflects later revision.

4. Who selected the figures and names of all twelve Dutch southern constellations—Keyser, de Houtman, Plancius, or collaborative discussion aboard ship and in Amsterdam? The evidence assigns roles but not every act of naming.

5. What is the earliest primary nautical manual explicitly calling α, β, and γ the seamen’s or navigators’ Triangle Stars? Allen records the usage, but a prior manual remains to be identified.

6. Can Caesius’s earliest printed wording and exact star-to-patriarch allocation be established from a fully digitized edition? The collective identification is well attested; the assignment of Atria specifically to Abraham is less securely traced.

7. What is the precise source and intended meaning of Caesius’s *Almutabet algenubi*? Its Arabic morphology and transmission deserve manuscript-level investigation.

8. Who first coined **Atria**? It is attested in the 1952 *American Nautical Almanac*, but an earlier hydrographic or air-navigation list may exist.

9. What source underlies the “830 stars in 109 groups” description associated in the brief with a “Bode collection on chain”? It does not match Bode’s own 1801 catalogue title.

10. The anonymous seventeenth-century Leiden maps replacing TrA with Ceneus and Siren remain unattributed.
