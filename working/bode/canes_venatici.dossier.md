# Canes Venatici: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact** — directly attested by a historical text, chart, catalogue, institutional record, or modern scientific publication.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion argued from comparison of sources, languages, or reconstructed star positions.
- **Tradition** — a report repeated in historical or popular literature but not securely documented at its alleged date.
- **Disputed claim** — incompatible accounts survive, or the evidence does not establish the usual attribution.
- **Unverified claim** — encountered in secondary literature but not traced to a contemporary source.
- **Myth/legend** — narrative symbolism rather than historical or astronomical fact.
- **Modern invention** — a name, image, or interpretation created after antiquity.

## Basic identification

### Present status

**Kept by the International Astronomical Union.** Canes Venatici is one of the 88 official constellations. It was included in the IAU’s Rome list of 1922 and received exact boundaries in Eugène Delporte’s *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* of 1930.

- Official Latin nominative: **Canes Venatici**
- Meaning: **Hunting Dogs**
- Genitive: **Canum Venaticorum**
- Abbreviation: **CVn**
- Area: approximately **465 square degrees**, 38th in size
- Neighbors under the 1930 boundaries: **Ursa Major, Boötes, and Coma Berenices**
- Principal naked-eye line in many modern charts: **α CVn–β CVn**
- Brightest star: **α² Canum Venaticorum, Cor Caroli**, approximately magnitude 2.9

The IAU adopted the constellation names and three-letter abbreviations at its first General Assembly in Rome in May 1922. Contrary to a common shorthand, the exact boundaries were not adopted there: Delporte’s boundary work followed and was published in 1930. The lines were drawn along hour circles and parallels for epoch B1875.0, with instructions to avoid displacing established variables and important lettered or numbered stars where practicable. [IAU constellation history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), [original 1922-list discussion](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html), [Delporte boundary data](https://cdsarc.cds.unistra.fr/viz-bin/ReadMe/VI/49?format=html&tex=true).

### Grammar and etymology

**Documented linguistic fact.** Latin *canēs* is the nominative plural of *canis*, “dog”; *vēnāticī* is the masculine nominative plural of *vēnāticus*, “pertaining to hunting,” from *vēnārī*, “to hunt.” The genitive plural is consequently *Canum Venaticorum*, “of the Hunting Dogs.”

The name is descriptive, not the inherited proper name of a Greek mythical animal.

### Names in later languages

These are overwhelmingly translations of the post-Renaissance Latin constellation, not independent ancient constellations.

- German: **die Jagdhunde**
- French: **les Chiens de chasse**; historically also *Lévriers*
- Italian: **i Cani da Caccia**; also *Levrieri*
- Spanish: **los Perros de Caza** or **los Lebreles**
- Portuguese: **Cães de Caça**
- Modern Greek: **Κύνες Θηρευτικοί**
- Arabic: **السلوقيان** (*al-salūqiyyān*, “the two salukis/greyhounds”)
- Persian: **تازی‌ها** (*tāzī-hā*, “greyhounds”) or **سگ‌های شکاری**, “hunting dogs”
- Modern Hebrew: **כלבי ציד** (*kalvei ṣayid*, “hunting dogs”)
- Chinese: **獵犬座 / 猎犬座** (*Lièquǎnzuò*, “Hunting-Dog Constellation”)
- Polish: **Psy Gończe**
- Czech: **Honicí psi**

A useful multilingual comparison is maintained by [In-The-Sky](https://in-the-sky.org/data/constellations_translations.php). These forms should not be projected backward into Babylonian, Pharaonic Egyptian, classical Greek, Arabic-Ptolemaic, Persian, or rabbinic astronomy.

### Bode’s labels

On plate VII of Johann Elert Bode’s 1801 *Uranographia*, the Latin figure is **Canes Venatici**, while the companion German description uses **Jagdhunde**. Bode retained the individual labels **Asterion** and **Chara**, and depicted the crowned **Cor Caroli** at Chara’s collar or leash.

Bode’s atlas consists of twenty large copperplates and was accompanied by the *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, whose catalogue title advertises 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. The atlas is available in full from [ETH Zürich](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); the companion catalogue is described and digitized by the [Bibliothèque numérique patrimoniale](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036).

The figure-specific lead “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be reconciled with Bode’s published contents. **Unverified claim:** it may describe a modern collection or indexing subset rather than the *Uranographia* itself. Bode’s documented catalogue total is 17,240 entries, while the plates contain more than one hundred constellation figures and asterisms. It should not be quoted as the size of Bode’s complete stellar catalogue without a collection-level citation.

## Stars and objects

## The figure as drawn

### Hevelius’s dogs

**Documented fact.** Hevelius drew two coursing hounds on a common or paired leash held by Boötes:

- **Asterion**, the northern dog
- **Chara**, the southern dog

Hevelius’s catalogue contains **23 entries** under Canes Venatici. A modern positional analysis confirms the 23-star total. [Hevelius catalogue study](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2010/08/aa14003-10.pdf), [electronic reconstruction](https://www.pbarbier.com/hevelius/hevelius.html).

The two dogs were large pictorial figures containing many faint stars. They were not originally equivalent simply to β CVn and α CVn. In Hevelius’s image, the brighter stars now called α and β CVn both belong to the southern dog, Chara; Asterion is a diffuse northern dog made chiefly from faint stars.

The familiar modern equation “Asterion = β CVn, Chara = α CVn” is a **modern simplification**, especially associated with Antonín Bečvář’s twentieth-century atlases. It is not Hevelius’s original anatomy. [Historical discussion](https://www.astronomy.com/science/do-you-know-about-asterion/).

### Asterion

*Asterion* derives from Greek **ἀστέριον**, “little star,” a diminutive of **ἀστήρ**, “star”; it can also suggest “starry.” Hevelius used it as the name of the northern dog, not as a securely inherited ancient name for β CVn.

### Chara

*Chara* comes from Greek **χαρά**, “joy” or “delight.” Older writers sometimes glossed it through Latin *cārus/cāra*, “dear, beloved,” but that is a secondary association rather than the most direct etymology.

The IAU now recognizes **Chara** as the proper name of **β CVn**. Kunitzsch and Smart describe it as a Renaissance application of Hevelius’s Greek dog-name to the star. [Kunitzsch and Smart, *Dictionary of Modern Star Names*](https://rexresearch1.com/AstronomyTelescopesLibrary/DictionaryModernStarNamesShortGuideKunitzsch.pdf).

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

Canes Venatici did not exist when Bayer published the first edition of the *Uranometria* in 1603. Its “Bayer letters” α and β are therefore later extensions of Bayer’s method, not labels printed by Bayer himself for this constellation.

Flamsteed’s British catalogue numbered **1 through 25 Canum Venaticorum**, in approximate order of right ascension. Three complications matter:

- **1 CVn** lies inside modern Ursa Major.
- **13 CVn** lies inside modern Coma Berenices.
- **22 CVn** is not a real independent star entry: it is conventionally treated as nonexistent or arising from catalogue error.

Thus the sequence 1–25 should not be mistaken for 25 surviving modern CVn stars. [Flamsteed catalogue reconstruction](https://pbarbier.com/flamsteed/flamsteed.html), [CVn star chart](https://pbarbier.com/flamsteed/CVN.pdf).

### Cor Caroli

Latin **Cor Caroli** means “Charles’s Heart.”

The system’s brighter component is α², paradoxically, while the fainter visual component is α¹. In 2016 the IAU Working Group on Star Names assigned **Cor Caroli specifically to α² CVn**, not indiscriminately to both components.

α² CVn is chemically peculiar, with strong silicon, europium, and other abundance patches organized by a powerful magnetic field. Rotation carries these patches in and out of view, producing a roughly 5.47-day light and spectrum cycle. It is the prototype of the **α² Canum Venaticorum variables**.

### Chara

The IAU name **Chara** belongs to β CVn. It is a comparatively nearby G0 V star and a solar analog. The alternative historical name **Asterion** was applied to β CVn by Bečvář and became widespread, but it collapses Hevelius’s entire northern dog into one star.

### La Superba

**Documented with a qualification.** Y CVn is a cool J-type carbon star and semiregular variable. Angelo Secchi described it in 1868 as exceptionally “superba.” Later literature converted the adjective into the proper name **La Superba**, “the magnificent/splendid one.”

The claim that Secchi formally christened the star “La Superba” is somewhat overstated: recent star-name research finds that Secchi used *superba* adjectivally rather than presenting a formal name. The IAU later accepted the established popular name. [Historical WGSN investigation](https://www.sciengine.com/parse/pdf/1440-2807/05BDA1C834174D149406589843FA9281.pdf?attname=05BDA1C834174D149406589843FA9281.pdf), [IAU-name discussion](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/La_Superba).

### Variable-star classes carrying the genitive

Canes Venatici has supplied three important class names:

- **α² CVn variables:** rotating, magnetic, chemically peculiar upper-main-sequence stars whose surface abundance patches modulate brightness and spectral lines.
- **RS CVn binaries:** close binaries, usually with a cool evolved component, showing strong chromospheric/coronal activity and large starspots. The class definition was formalized by Douglas Hall in 1976. [Modern study of prototype RS CVn](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/523/3/4146/7192454).
- **AM CVn stars:** ultracompact, hydrogen-deficient binaries transferring helium-rich material to a white dwarf. AM CVn itself is the prototype.

These classes preserve the Latin genitive even when the physical class has no connection with hunting dogs.

## Deep-sky objects

### The five Messier objects

| Object | NGC | Type | Discovery/history |
|---|---:|---|---|

| **M51** | NGC 5194 + NGC 5195 | Interacting spiral system | Main galaxy discovered by Messier in 1773; companion recognized by Pierre Méchain; spiral structure detected and drawn by Lord Rosse in 1845 |
| **M63** | NGC 5055 | Spiral galaxy, “Sunflower Galaxy” | Discovered by Pierre Méchain on 14 June 1779 |

| **M106** | NGC 4258 | Active spiral galaxy | Discovered by Méchain in 1781; added to the modern Messier list from his records |

M3 contains hundreds of thousands of stars and an unusually large population of variables. NASA currently gives at least 274 known variables. [NASA M3](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/explore-the-night-sky/hubble-messier-catalog/messier-3/).

### M51 and the invention of “spiral nebulae”

**Documented fact.** William Parsons, third Earl of Rosse, using the 72-inch “Leviathan of Parsonstown” at Birr Castle, recognized M51’s spiral form in spring 1845. It was the first nebula recorded as spiral.

The celebrated published image was not simply an instantaneous eyepiece sketch: historical work on Rosse’s observing books shows a process of repeated observation, working drawings, discussion, and stabilization into a publishable image. [Historical reconstruction](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-for-the-history-of-science/article/abs/observation-working-images-and-procedure-the-great-spiral-in-lord-rosses-astronomical-record-books-and-beyond/A7F2AE71F71B7B69A5124368C29D544A), [Rosse’s 1850 observations and plates](https://www.sunflower-astronomy.com/Books/Observations_of_the_Nebulae/Observations_of_the_Nebulae.html).

M51 has hosted several recorded supernovae:

- **SN 1945A**
- **SN 1994I**, Type Ic
- **SN 2005cs**, a subluminous Type II-P event
- **SN 2011dh**, Type IIb

[NASA’s SN 2005cs material](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/hubble/sn-2005cs-in-m51-before-progenitor-stars-explosion/); [radio-supernova survey of M51](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/452/1/32/1749114).

### Other prominent NGC and named objects

Canes Venatici is unusually rich in nearby galaxies. Important objects include:

- **NGC 4214** — irregular or Magellanic-type star-forming galaxy
- **NGC 4244** — edge-on “Silver Needle Galaxy”
- **NGC 4395** — nearby low-surface-brightness spiral with a low-mass active nucleus
- **NGC 4449** — irregular starburst galaxy, Caldwell 21
- **NGC 4490 and NGC 4485** — interacting pair; NGC 4490 is commonly called the **Cocoon Galaxy**
- **NGC 4618 and NGC 4625** — asymmetric, one-armed spiral systems
- **NGC 4631** — edge-on **Whale Galaxy**, Caldwell 32
- **NGC 4656/4657** — distorted **Hockey Stick** or **Crowbar Galaxy**
- **NGC 5005** — bright spiral galaxy, Caldwell 29
- **NGC 5033** — nearby Seyfert spiral
- **NGC 5371** and the **Hickson 68** compact group
- **Canes Venatici I and II dwarf spheroidal galaxies** — very faint Milky Way satellites discovered in Sloan Digital Sky Survey data in 2006–07
- **Canes Venatici I Cloud / M94 Group** and **Canes Venatici II Group / M106 Group** — names used for nearby galaxy aggregations

There are more than five hundred NGC, IC, and Messier catalogue entries inside the Delporte polygon; the objects above are the observationally or historically conspicuous subset. [Catalogue listing](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/canes_venatici-deepsky-objects), [Canes Venatici dwarf discovery](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0604354).

## Novae and supernovae

No securely documented naked-eye Galactic “guest star” or classical nova has been identified as occurring in the modern CVn boundary.

Numerous extragalactic supernovae have been recorded in its many galaxies. M51’s four well-studied events are the most culturally and scientifically prominent; M106 has included **SN 1981K** and **SN 2014bc**, among others. A genuinely exhaustive transient list would be dynamic and belongs in the IAU Transient Name Server rather than a static historical catalogue. [IAU Transient Name Server](https://www.wis-tns.org/index.php/).

## Meteor showers

The **Canum Venaticids**, IAU MDC number **403**, code **CVN**, appear on the Meteor Data Center’s **Working List**, not its list of securely established showers. This distinction is important: MDC registration records a published proposed shower, but does not certify that the stream is physically real or independent. [MDC Working List](https://iaumdc.astro.sk/working), [MDC explanation of statuses](https://iaumeteordatacenter.org/).

The grammatically correct shower form is **Canum Venaticids**, derived from *Canum Venaticorum*. “Canes Venaticids” is a popular but formally irregular form.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before the Common Era: Mesopotamia and Egypt

**Finding: no secure direct antecedent.**

No Babylonian constellation corresponding to two hunting dogs in this exact northern field has been identified in the standard reconstruction of Mesopotamian uranography. The area fell among stars later absorbed into the Greek Great Bear, Boötes, or unfigured northern space. Claims that Canes Venatici itself is Babylonian are anachronistic.

A modern archaeoastronomical proposal associates a broad Egyptian northern figure called **Anu/An(w)**, a falcon-headed harpooner or form of Horus, with a region extending from Lynx toward Canes Venatici and the Great Bear. This is a **modern coordinate reconstruction**, not an Egyptian text naming Cor Caroli or a recognizable CVn constellation. The evidence is too broad to call Anu a direct ancestor of Hevelius’s dogs.

### Second century CE: Ptolemy

**Documented fact with identification caveat.** Canes Venatici is absent from Ptolemy’s 48 constellations. Stars now in the region were treated chiefly as **ἀμόρφωτοι**, “unformed” stars outside the primary figure of Ursa Major, with other nearby stars entangled in the description of Boötes and his implement.

“Unformed” did not mean unknown. It meant that a catalogue star was assigned to the vicinity of a constellation but not to the outlined body of its image.

Modern summaries often say flatly that “Ptolemy put all the CVn stars in Ursa Major.” This is broadly useful but too simple: historical identifications depend on reconstructed positions, precession, manuscript readings, and the changing division between the Bear, Boötes, and surrounding unformed stars.

### c. 964: al-Ṣūfī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita* (*Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars*) reproduced and corrected the Ptolemaic system. He did **not** know Canes Venatici as an independent constellation and consequently supplied no Arabic equivalent of Asterion or Chara.

The relevant stars remained entries belonging to the Ptolemaic Bear/Boötes field or were discussed through indigenous Arabic star lore. Statements that “al-Ṣūfī named the Hunting Dogs” are unsupported.

### Ninth–twelfth centuries: the club, hook, and dogs problem

This is the most disputed historical thread.

One Ptolemaic description of Boötes’s implement used the rare Greek word conventionally reconstructed as **κολλόροβος / κολλόροβον** (*kollorobos/kollorobon*), an implement interpreted as a club, staff, or crook.

In the Arabic transmission it became a phrase such as:

> **العصا ذات الكلاب**  
> *al-ʿaṣā dhāt al-kullāb* — “the staff having a hook.”

In unvocalized Arabic, **كلاب** can be read as *kullāb*, “hook,” or *kilāb*, “dogs.” Gerard of Cremona’s twelfth-century Latin translation rendered the phrase approximately:

> **hastile habens canes** — “the spear-shaft having dogs.”

#### What is secure

- **Documented linguistic fact:** the unpointed Arabic consonantal form permits the hook/dogs ambiguity.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Paul Kunitzsch traced how the rare Greek term was paraphrased in Arabic and misread in Latin.
- **Documented medieval result:** the Latin phrase associating a staff and dogs circulated.

#### What is not secure

The popular story often claims that Ptolemy’s actual stars of Canes Venatici were a Greek “club,” that Hunayn ibn Isḥāq mistranslated them into a hook, and that Gerard then moved those same stars into dogs. This overcompresses several stages:

- the textual description concerned Boötes and his implement;
- the medieval dogs were initially attributes of Boötes;
- the stars later chosen by Hevelius for his dogs were substantially the faint, unformed Ursa Major field;
- a garbled word alone did not create Hevelius’s exact constellation boundaries.

Thus Allen’s vivid “club became dogs” account contains a genuine transmission history but encourages a false one-to-one continuity between Ptolemy’s club stars and Hevelius’s dogs. [Allen’s account](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Canes_Venatici%2A.html), [Kunitzsch-based summary](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/advice/canes-venatici-hunting-dogs-constellation).

### 1515: Dürer’s maps

Albrecht Dürer’s northern planisphere presents the traditional Ptolemaic figures. It contains no independent Canes Venatici. The relevant stars, where plotted, belong to the Bear/Boötes environment. Any modern image labeling dogs on the Dürer map is an overlay, not Dürer’s constellation.

### 1533: Peter Apian

**Documented precursor, not yet the constellation.** Peter Apian’s *Horoscopion generale* depicted Boötes with two dogs. The date is sometimes incorrectly printed as **1553**; 1533 is the usual bibliographical date for the work.

These dogs are important iconographic evidence that the Latin mistranslation had become pictorial. But they remained accessories of Boötes and did not occupy exactly the independent Hevelian figure.

### 1602: Willem Janszoon Blaeu’s celestial globe

A Blaeu globe dated 1602 shows two dogs near Boötes, and its dog bodies make use of the unformed stars later assigned to Canes Venatici. This is a stronger graphic precursor than Apian.

**Contested attribution:** because Blaeu anticipated both the two-dog image and the use of the unformed star field, he deserves some credit in the visual genealogy. Nevertheless, he did not publish the stable catalogue-and-atlas constellation named *Canes Venatici*. Hevelius remains the conventional inventor of the formal constellation. [Discussion and globe image](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/advice/canes-venatici-hunting-dogs-constellation).

### 1603: Bayer

Bayer’s *Uranometria* did not contain Canes Venatici as a constellation. Cor Caroli is plotted as an unlabeled star on the Ursa Major plate. Consequently, α and β CVn are not original 1603 Bayer letters.

### 1627: Julius Schiller

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* predates Hevelius’s constellation by six decades. Therefore there is no Christianized “Canes Venatici” in it.

The relevant field is distributed across Schiller’s Christian replacements for the older Ptolemaic figures, principally the region of Ursa Major and Boötes. Boötes was replaced by **Saint Sylvester**. It is misleading to ask which saint “replaced Canes Venatici,” because the constellation did not yet exist. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/), [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720).

### 1660–1673: Cor Caroli appears

A later tradition states that court physician **Sir Charles Scarborough** remarked that the star shone especially brightly on the eve of Charles II’s Restoration and suggested the name to Edmond Halley.

No secure contemporary 1660 record of that exchange has been located.

The earliest presently traceable cartographic evidence is Francis Lamb’s *Astroscopium* planisphere of **1673**, which labels the star:

> **Cor Caroli Regis Martyris**  
> “Heart of Charles, the Martyred King.”

That wording points clearly to **Charles I**, executed in 1649, not Charles II. The Restoration context may nevertheless explain why Stuart memorial symbolism flourished under Charles II.

### 1687/1690: Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius completed the *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* before his death in 1687. His widow and collaborator **Elisabeth Hevelius** brought the *Prodromus Astronomiae*, catalogue, and atlas to publication at Danzig/Gdańsk in **1690**.

This is the formal introduction of **Canes Venatici, Asterion et Chara** as an independent constellation. Hevelius catalogued 23 stars in it and issued a dedicated plate.

Calling it either a “1687” or “1690” constellation is defensible if the distinction is explained:

- **1687:** date on the atlas’s title/front matter and year of Hevelius’s death/completion
- **1690:** actual posthumous publication

[Full Hevelius scan](https://dspace.ut.ee/items/e0e0d294-bf84-4218-8f7c-0188ed3ff4c0), [plate collection](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Firmamentum_Sobiescianum,_sive_uranographia).

### 1725–1729: Flamsteed and the Halley story

Flamsteed’s *Historia Coelestis Britannica* catalogue appeared in 1725 and the posthumous *Atlas Coelestis* in 1729. Flamsteed accepted Canes Venatici among the Hevelian constellations and numbered its stars.

R. H. Allen later claimed that Halley in 1725 made **Cor Caroli** a separate emblem in honor of Charles II, following Scarborough’s suggestion. The attribution appears ultimately to depend heavily on Bode and later retellings; it is not supported by an identified contemporary Halley act of naming.

The securely dated 1673 Lamb map is earlier and names the “martyred king.” Therefore the strongest current conclusion is:

- **Documented:** Lamb, 1673, Charles I.
- **Tradition:** Scarborough, Restoration night, Charles II.
- **Disputed:** Halley as the original namer in 1725.
- **Possible later reinterpretation:** an existing Charles I emblem was broadened or redirected toward Charles II.

### 1764–1781: Messier and Méchain

- 3 May 1764: Messier discovered M3.
- 1773: Messier discovered M51.
- 1779: Méchain discovered M63.
- 1781: Méchain discovered M94 and M106.

These discoveries transformed the faint constellation into an important deep-sky observing field.

### 1782: Bode’s earlier atlas

Bode included the Hunting Dogs in his *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln*, based partly on the Paris/Flamsteed atlas tradition. This predates his monumental 1801 work and demonstrates that his use of the figure was reception, not invention.

### 1792: Piazzi

Giuseppe Piazzi used the abbreviated Italian form **Cani da Cacc.** in Palermo astronomical material. This is an early documented vernacular catalogue reception, not a distinct Italian invention.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode’s plate VII combines Boötes, Corona Borealis, Canes Venatici, Coma Berenices, and nearby figures. His dogs run beneath the Great Bear, restrained by Boötes. The crowned Stuart heart is fastened at the upper end of Chara’s collar/leash.

Bode’s treatment is historically consequential because it visually fuses two originally separate developments:

1. Hevelius’s paired Hunting Dogs.
2. The English royal emblem Cor Caroli.

The atlas plotted more than 17,000 stars and thousands of nebular objects, while preserving elaborate mythographic figures. [Bode scan](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [plate VII collection record](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?id=IM%2F000415&src=CalmView.Catalog).

### 1822–1825: Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* of 1822 reproduced the two leashed dogs in a compact popular atlas. The anonymous or pseudonymous *Urania’s Mirror* cards, issued in London around 1824–25 with an accompanying text associated with Jehoshaphat Aspin, borrowed heavily from Jamieson’s compositions.

Card 10 combines **Boötes, Canes Venatici, Coma Berenices, and Quadrans Muralis**. The card uses pierced stars intended to glow when held before light. It helped establish the paired greyhounds in nineteenth-century popular imagination.

### Burritt

Elijah H. Burritt’s *Geography of the Heavens* and accompanying atlas, first published in the 1830s and repeatedly revised, accepted the dogs and retold their association with Boötes. Burritt belongs to the American educational reception rather than the constellation’s invention. [Digitized Burritt](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/81/The_geography_of_the_heavens_-_and_class-book_of_astronomy_-_accompanied_by_a_celestial_atlas_%28IA_geographyofheav00burr%29.pdf).

### 1843 and 1872: Argelander and Heis

F. W. A. Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (Berlin, 1843) and Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* (Cologne, 1872) retained Canes Venatici in scientific naked-eye uranography. Their importance lies less in elaborate dog iconography than in stabilizing the constellation set used by observers. [Argelander facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/162910), [historical comparison](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Introduction%2A.html).

### 1845: Rosse’s spiral

At Birr Castle, Lord Rosse identified M51’s spiral structure. This observation made a Hevelian filler constellation central to the nineteenth-century debate over the nature of nebulae.

### 1868: Secchi and Y CVn

Angelo Secchi classified the intensely red Y CVn among his Type IV spectra and called its appearance *superba*. Later observers turned this description into **La Superba**.

### 1899: Allen

Richard Hinckley Allen’s *Star-Names and Their Meanings* consolidated much of the English-language lore: Hevelius’s invention, Asterion and Chara, the Ptolemaic unformed stars, and the Scarborough–Halley–Charles II story.

Allen remains valuable as a witness to nineteenth-century traditions, but his linguistic histories predate Kunitzsch’s detailed Arabic-textual work and should not be treated as final authority.

### 1922–1930: IAU standardization

- **1922:** Canes Venatici and abbreviation **CVn** accepted at Rome.
- **1930:** Delporte assigned its exact polygonal area.
- The official boundaries preserved α and β CVn and most of the established Flamsteed sequence.
- 1 CVn remained geographically in Ursa Major; 13 CVn in Coma Berenices.
- No neighboring obsolete constellation was “absorbed” wholesale by CVn.

### 1950s: Bečvář

Antonín Bečvář’s *Atlas Coeli* popularized the simplified assignment:

- Asterion = β CVn
- Chara = α CVn

This is responsible for much modern confusion. The IAU has retained **Chara** for β CVn, while **Asterion** is not the official IAU proper name of a current star.

### 2016 onward: IAU star-name standardization

The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Cor Caroli** for α² CVn and **Chara** for β CVn. **La Superba** was subsequently accepted for Y CVn.

## Invention and reception

## Who invented it?

The fairest source-driven formulation is:

- **Textual precursor:** the medieval Latin misreading that gave Boötes “dogs.”
- **Early depiction:** Apian, 1533.
- **Strong cartographic precursor:** Blaeu’s 1602 globe, which placed dogs among relevant unformed stars.
- **Formal inventor:** Johannes Hevelius, whose completed 1687/posthumously published 1690 atlas and catalogue named, delimited, illustrated, and catalogued *Canes Venatici*.
- **Publisher and collaborator essential to survival:** Elisabeth Hevelius.
- **Major synthesizer:** Bode, 1782 and 1801.
- **Official perpetuator:** IAU, 1922/1930.

Plancius, Keyser, de Houtman, Lacaille, Lalande, Poczobut, Hell, Lemonnier, and the southern-voyage constellation makers had no demonstrable role in inventing Canes Venatici. Their inclusion in generic constellation templates should not imply involvement where none exists.

The figure did not honor a voyage, scientific instrument, machine, or patron. It represented hunting animals attached to Boötes. The **heart inside it**, however, was royal Stuart commemoration.

## What was invented?

Not merely a new name. Hevelius performed three operations:

1. He detached a set of faint, formerly unformed or loosely assigned stars from the Ursa Major–Boötes field.
2. He organized them into two animal bodies.
3. He supplied the pair with a collective Latin name and individual Greek names.

The result was a large pictorial figure, not the two-star line of modern introductory charts.

## Myths and legends

## Classical Greek and Roman material

### No ancient Canes Venatici myth

**Documented negative finding.** Canes Venatici is absent from:

- Eratosthenes, *Catasterisms* and its Epitome
- Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica*
- Hyginus, *Fabulae*
- Aratus, *Phaenomena*
- Germanicus’s Aratean poem
- the scholia to Aratus and Germanicus
- Ovid, *Fasti* and *Metamorphoses*
- Manilius, *Astronomica*
- Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*
- Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*
- Pausanias, *Description of Greece*

No Greek or Roman author identifies Asterion and Chara as Boötes’s dogs, no classical god places them in the sky, and there is no ancient catasterism for the pair.

Accordingly, there are no competing classical identities, no classical perpetrator of their catasterism, and no independent myths in which these two named hounds act.

### The Great Bear chase

**Modern mythographic extension.** Later atlas writers say that Boötes holds the dogs as they chase Ursa Major around the pole. This makes visual sense in Hevelius’s map but is not a classical dog-catasterism.

Classical literature does contain many identities for Boötes—Arcas, Icarius, a ploughman, ox-driver, bear-guard, or celestial watchman—and several variants for the Great Bear, especially Callisto. Those narratives belong to Boötes and Ursa Major. Attaching Asterion and Chara to them is post-Hevelian synthesis.

### Asterion and Greek mythological namesakes

Greek mythology contains several unrelated figures called **Asterion/Asterius**, including the Cretan king associated with Europa and the Minotaur tradition, and one of Actaeon’s dogs in some later lists. There is no evidence that Hevelius meant either figure.

The dog-name’s transparent meaning, “little star/starry one,” is sufficient. Identifying the northern hound with a named dog of Actaeon is a **modern associative invention** unless a Hevelian source is produced.

### Chara and mythological dogs

No securely ancient dog named Chara is attached to Boötes. The name means joy and functions as a descriptive Renaissance name. Claims that Chara was a dog belonging to Icarius, Arcas, or Callisto are untraced conflations.

## The Cor Caroli legend

The Restoration-night story—that the star suddenly shone more brilliantly as Charles II returned—is a **royalist legend or courtly anecdote**, not evidence of an astronomical outburst. α² CVn is variable, but only by a small fraction of a magnitude; it did not become a nova in May 1660.

The story may have been encouraged by the later discovery that the star really varies, but the variability is far too small to support a conspicuous Restoration omen.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Arabic and Bedouin astronomy

There is no medieval Arabic constellation equivalent to Hevelius’s paired greyhounds.

Al-Ṣūfī retained the Ptolemaic Bear and Boötes framework. Indigenous Bedouin astronomy frequently organized northern stars into groups different from the Greek constellations, especially around the Great Bear, but no securely documented Bedouin asterism matching Hevelius’s entire 23-star figure was found in the consulted sources.

The modern Arabic **السلوقيان** is a translation/adaptation of the European constellation, literally the two Saluki-type hounds. It should not be attributed to al-Ṣūfī.

## Chinese astronomy

The modern CVn area overlaps several traditional Chinese star officials within the Three Enclosures.

### Chángchén — 常陳, Imperial Guards

The principal equivalent is **常陳** (*Chángchén*), usually interpreted as the emperor’s permanent array or imperial guards. The *Jin shu*, *Treatise on Astronomy*, describes seven stars arranged somewhat like the asterism Bi, north of the imperial seat, representing armed palace guards who establish defensive limits.

A frequently used modern identification runs through:

- α CVn
- 10 CVn
- β CVn
- 6 CVn
- 2 CVn
- 67 UMa

Reconstructions sometimes include a seventh component or differ in order because historical Chinese star maps do not supply modern coordinates with uniform precision. Hence exact member lists should be labeled **scholarly reconstructions**.

Chinese star names derived from this reconstruction include:

- α CVn: **常陳一**, First Star of Chángchén
- 10 CVn: **常陳二**
- β CVn: one of the subsequent numbered guard stars

The old English star name “Chang Chen” for α CVn is not a traditional personal proper name in the Western sense; it is a shortened borrowing from the asterism’s Chinese name.

### Sāngōng — 三公, Three Excellencies

Stars or objects in northern CVn have been associated with **三公**, the Three Excellencies, senior ministers of state. Modern identifications include 24 and 21 CVn and a position near M51, but the latter demonstrates the hazard of naïvely equating catalogue objects with ancient naked-eye “stars.” Reconstructions vary.

### Xiàng — 相, Prime Minister

**5 CVn** is commonly identified with **相**, the Prime Minister, with nearby faint stars later treated as additions.

Chinese astronomy thus supplies a genuine independent tradition in this sky area, but it does not contain hunting dogs.

## Indian astronomy

Canes Venatici lies far north of the ecliptic and outside the classical chain of 27 or 28 nakṣatras. No Vedic or siddhāntic constellation matching the dogs was found. Modern Indian-language names for CVn are translations or IAU-era adaptations, not an ancient lunar mansion.

## Hebrew and Jewish traditions

Modern Hebrew **כלבי ציד** is a translation of the Western constellation.

No biblical, rabbinic, or securely medieval Hebrew constellation corresponding to Hevelius’s dogs was found. Attempts to identify it with Job’s *ʿAyish*, *Kesil*, *Kimah*, or Mazzaroth are unsupported; those terms concern other, disputed celestial identifications.

## Kabbalistic, alchemical, and Hermetic readings

Because Canes Venatici was created in the late seventeenth century:

- it has no place in classical Hermetic paranatellonta;
- it is absent from standard medieval astrological constellation doctrine;
- it is not one of the fifteen Behenian fixed stars;
- it does not cross the ecliptic and therefore contains no traditional Western, Arabic, Indian, or Chinese lunar mansion in the ordinary sense.

Later occult writers may freely interpret hunting dogs as loyalty, pursuit, royal power, or guardianship, and Cor Caroli as a solar/royal heart. Such readings are **modern esoteric invention**, not recoverable ancient doctrine.

## Astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* gives planetary natures to stars of the ancient constellations but knows no Canes Venatici. Later astrologers sometimes transfer a **Mercury–Saturn** nature to α or β CVn by analogy with the unformed stars of Ursa Major or Boötes. This is a retrospective allocation.

Claims that “Ptolemy says Canes Venatici causes hunting ability” are anachronistic.

Vivian Robson and subsequent fixed-star literature assign traits such as hunting passion, cleverness, penetration, loyalty, or speculation to the dogs. These are twentieth-century astrological syntheses derived from the figure’s name.

## Boundary history

### Ptolemy

No bounded constellation existed. Relevant stars were primarily unformed stars associated with Ursa Major or descriptions near Boötes.

### Bayer, 1603

No CVn. The field lay chiefly on the Ursa Major plate and in undivided space toward Boötes and Coma Berenices.

### Hevelius, 1687/1690

Hevelius created a pictorial territory for the dogs and catalogued 23 stars. His outlines were artistic rather than legal polygons; animal bodies could overlap neighboring pictorial regions.

### Flamsteed, 1725/1729

Accepted the constellation and supplied the numbered sequence 1–25. Catalogue membership, rather than an exact border, governed attribution.

### Lacaille

Lacaille’s work chiefly reorganized the southern sky and did not materially create or redraw Canes Venatici.

### Bode, 1801

Bode drew explicit constellation boundary lines, but they were atlas-specific and not internationally binding. His boundaries surrounded the paired dogs while accommodating Coma Berenices, Ursa Major, and Boötes. Like other eighteenth-century atlases, they could “gerrymander” around inherited star assignments.

### Argelander and Heis

Their catalogues helped stabilize nineteenth-century observational membership, although no universal legal sky partition yet existed.

### Delporte, 1930

Delporte replaced pictorial membership with a closed polygon defined in B1875 coordinates. In modern J2000 coordinates the precessed edges no longer all appear exactly horizontal and vertical.

Consequences:

- **1 CVn** is within Ursa Major.
- **13 CVn** is within Coma Berenices.
- The traditional CVn sequence keeps those obsolete designations as historical labels.
- CVn gained no major named star from an abolished constellation.
- It borders only Ursa Major, Boötes, and Coma Berenices.
- Its official status concerns an area of sky; the IAU does not prescribe Hevelius’s dog drawing or a mandatory two-star stick figure.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas

**Negative finding.** The Farnese celestial globe predates the constellation by roughly fifteen centuries and cannot depict Canes Venatici. The corresponding surface carries the classical Great Bear/Boötes arrangement and surrounding space. Online pages claiming to show “Canes Venatici on the Farnese globe” are modern coordinate projections, not identification of an ancient dog image.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

No Hunting Dogs figure appears. Manuscripts show the Ptolemaic Great Bear and Boötes, conventionally in paired views—one as seen on a globe and one as seen in the sky.

### Dürer, 1515

No dogs as an independent constellation.

### Apian, 1533

Two dogs accompany Boötes. This is the earliest securely identified printed dog precursor encountered.

### Blaeu, 1602

Two dogs are displayed on the globe among stars later used by Hevelius. This is the most important pre-Hevelian graphic anticipation.

### Bayer, 1603

No independent dogs. Cor Caroli’s star is present but unnamed.

### Schiller, 1627

The field is Christianized only through the older constellations; no posthumous Canes Venatici saint exists.

### Francis Lamb, 1673

The relevant star appears as the crowned or royal **Cor Caroli Regis Martyris**, providing decisive iconographic evidence for Charles I.

### Hevelius, 1690, Gdańsk

Dedicated plate: two lean hunting hounds, Asterion north and Chara south, running toward the Bear while Boötes holds their leads. The map is presented in the external/globe convention and therefore appears reversed relative to a sky-view chart.

### Flamsteed, 1729, London

The dogs are accepted into the great telescopic atlas and receive the scientific catalogue framework behind the Flamsteed numbers.

### Bode, 1801, Berlin

Plate VII provides the most elaborate synthesis: two dogs, leash, Boötes, Great Bear chase, and crowned Cor Caroli attached to Chara’s collar. The plate belongs to the last monumental scientific atlas in which full allegorical bodies coexist with dense modern observational data.

### Jamieson, 1822, London

Smaller, more naturalistic greyhounds adapted for a popular atlas.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, c. 1824–25, London

Card 10 reproduces the figure in a theatrical pierced-card format, together with Boötes, Coma Berenices, and Quadrans Muralis. The artwork is substantially dependent on Jamieson.

### Argelander, 1843, Berlin; Heis, 1872, Cologne

Increasing emphasis on accurate naked-eye magnitudes and chart utility. Heis depicts abbreviated front portions of two hounds; the artistic figure is becoming subordinate to scientific plotting.

### Coins, cathedral portals, ceilings, and calendars

No ancient coin, classical ceiling, or medieval cathedral portal can securely portray Canes Venatici as such. Dog imagery near hunters, bears, or celestial figures is insufficient evidence without identifying stars or inscriptions.

No specific pre-Hevelian calendar image of the constellation was verified. Later decorative ceilings and calendars may reproduce atlas iconography, but no canonical monumental example comparable to the zodiac cycles was located.

## People, events, and works

### Johannes and Elisabeth Hevelius

Johannes supplied the observations, catalogue structure, and figures; Elisabeth completed and published the posthumous work. Calling Johannes the sole operative behind the 1690 publication obscures Elisabeth’s documented editorial and publishing role.

### Charles I and Charles II

The constellation contains one of the last royalist proper-star names to remain in common scientific use. The best early evidence points to Charles I; later tradition connects it to Charles II and the Restoration.

### Francis Lamb

His 1673 chart is central to resolving the Cor Caroli dispute. It predates the alleged Halley naming and uses language unmistakably referring to the martyred Charles I.

### Edmond Halley and Charles Scarborough

Their association with the name survives as tradition. No contemporary record examined establishes Halley as its creator.

### Charles Messier and Pierre Méchain

Their discoveries gave the constellation four major galaxies and one major globular cluster in the Messier catalogue.

### William Herschel

Resolved M3 into stars in 1784 and discovered or catalogued numerous galaxies in the field.

### William Parsons, Earl of Rosse

His 1845 observation of M51 supplied the first recognized spiral form in the heavens.

### Angelo Secchi

His spectroscopy of Y CVn helped establish carbon-star classification and generated the description that became *La Superba*.

### Solon Irving Bailey and M3 variables

Early twentieth-century studies of M3’s numerous variables made the cluster important to the calibration and classification of pulsating stars.

### Modern dwarf-galaxy surveys

Sloan Digital Sky Survey analyses announced Canes Venatici I and II among the ultra-faint satellites that transformed estimates of the Milky Way’s dwarf-galaxy population.

### Navigation and exploration

No major explorer or voyage is securely documented as having steered specifically by Cor Caroli or β CVn. The stars are too faint and too close to the much more useful Great Bear and Arcturus pathways to have become primary traditional navigation markers.

Modern observers use Cor Caroli as a stepping stone from the Big Dipper toward Coma Berenices, M3, and the CVn galaxies. Claims that ancient mariners navigated by “the Hunting Dogs” are anachronistic.

### Literature

The line “Boötes hath unleash’d his fiery hounds,” quoted by Allen from Owen Meredith’s *Clytemnestra*, reflects nineteenth-century poetic reception, not classical tradition.

The constellation appears frequently in modern astronomy poetry and science fiction as a resonant Latin place-name, but no single poem, opera, painting, building, ship, or spacecraft named unmistakably and prominently for the constellation emerged as a major historical tradition in the consulted record.

“CVN” is also the United States Navy hull classification for a nuclear-powered aircraft carrier. Apparent references such as “CVN-65” or “CVN-78” have nothing to do with Canes Venatici.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Did Hevelius invent the dogs?

**Conclusion: formally yes, absolutely from nothing no.**

- Medieval Latin supplied dogs around Boötes.
- Apian drew two dogs in 1533.
- Blaeu used relevant unformed stars in two dogs by 1602.
- Hevelius first stabilized them as the named, catalogued independent constellation.

“Created by Hevelius” is correct in the conventional uranographic sense; “first person ever to draw dogs there” is false.

### 2. Were the stars Ptolemy’s club?

**Overstatement.** The translation history concerns Boötes’s implement, while Hevelius’s dog stars were largely the unformed stars of the Ursa Major region. The club-to-hook-to-dogs story explains the iconographic stimulus, not an exact transfer of one fixed ancient asterism.

### 3. Did Hunayn ibn Isḥāq make the error?

The common chain attributes the Arabic paraphrase to Hunayn and the dog reading to Gerard. Kunitzsch’s work supports the broader textual transmission, but popular accounts often assert personal error and motive more confidently than surviving manuscript evidence permits. The safest statement is that the rare Greek term was paraphrased as a hooked staff in the Arabic tradition and misread as dogs in Latin transmission.

### 4. Charles I or Charles II?

The best early chart evidence favors **Charles I**:

- 1673 Lamb: *Regis Martyris*.

The Charles II case rests on:

- the Restoration date;
- the Scarborough brightness anecdote;
- later attribution to Halley;
- Bode and Allen’s influential retellings.

It is possible that Stuart loyalists allowed the shortened *Cor Caroli* to commemorate both father and son. But “Halley named it for Charles II in 1725” is not securely documented.

### 5. Was Cor Caroli a temporary bright star in 1660?

No. The claim is legend. Its real variability is small and periodic; it cannot explain an obvious nova-like blaze.

### 6. Which dog is which star?

Hevelius:

- Asterion = an extended northern dog.
- Chara = an extended southern dog containing both bright principal stars.

Bečvář and modern simplification:

- β CVn = Asterion
- α CVn = Chara

Current IAU:

- β CVn = Chara
- α² CVn = Cor Caroli
- no official star named Asterion

Popular charts often mix these incompatible systems.

### 7. Did Bayer name α and β CVn?

No. Bayer could not have lettered stars in a constellation that did not yet exist in his 1603 atlas. “Bayer designation” describes the later extension of his letter system.

### 8. Flamsteed’s missing stars

- 1 CVn and 13 CVn survive as historical designations outside modern CVn.
- 22 CVn is spurious/nonexistent as a separate star.
- Therefore lists claiming “25 Flamsteed stars presently in CVn” are wrong.

### 9. Is “Canes Venaticids” the meteor-shower name?

The IAU-style grammatical form is **Canum Venaticids**. The shower remains on the Working List; it should not be described as an established annual shower without qualification.

### 10. Did the IAU “drop constellations in 1930”?

The IAU did not conduct a simple 1930 abolition vote in which Canes Venatici defeated rival candidates. The canonical set was adopted in 1922; Delporte then made the sky exhaustive through formal boundaries. Obsolete figures disappeared because their names were not in the adopted set and their stars fell inside one of the 88 regions.

### 11. Does the IAU recognize the picture?

No. It recognizes the Latin name, abbreviation, and bounded region. It does not officially mandate two dogs, their breeds, their sex, their direction of motion, or a particular stick figure.

### 12. Is La Superba Secchi’s formal coinage?

The historical evidence suggests Secchi used *superba* descriptively. Later observers lexicalized it as **La Superba**. The common attribution is substantially correct but bibliographically imprecise.

### 13. Ancient Egyptian Canes Venatici?

The proposed Anu/An(w) mapping is a broad modern reconstruction. It is not an Egyptian inscription equating a pair of hunting dogs with α and β CVn.

### 14. “Asterion was Actaeon’s dog”

Unverified associative mythology. The name Asterion has multiple Greek mythological bearers, but no source connects Hevelius’s dog to Actaeon.

### Dedicated hoax/error search result

No famous deliberate Canes Venatici hoax was found. The principal misinformation consists of:

- pseudo-ancient myths written for a modern constellation;
- the simplified club-to-dogs story;
- the Charles II/Halley attribution stated as certainty;
- the 1553-for-1533 Apian date;
- calling the Working-List Canum Venaticids established;
- identifying β CVn simultaneously as both Chara and Asterion;
- treating modern Chinese, Arabic, Persian, or Hebrew translations as ancient names;
- claiming a Farnese-Atlas Hunting Dogs image;
- confusing the naval abbreviation CVN with the constellation.

## Open questions

1. **The earliest exact manuscript occurrence of *hastile habens canes*.** A critical comparison of Gerardine Almagest manuscripts would clarify whether the dog reading originated with Gerard, his exemplar, or later copying.

2. **Blaeu’s independence.** More work on the sources for Blaeu’s 1602 globe could establish whether his dog-star allocation came through Apian, an intermediary globe tradition, or an independent reading of Ptolemaic unformed stars.

3. **Hevelius’s written rationale.** The catalogue and plate securely establish the figure, but a dedicated statement by Hevelius explaining why he chose the names Asterion and Chara has not been identified here.

4. **Cor Caroli before Lamb.** Lamb’s 1673 planisphere is the earliest securely traced chart. An earlier manuscript, courtly poem, broadside, globe, or Scarborough correspondence could change the Charles I/Charles II assessment.

5. **The Halley attribution.** Bode’s report needs to be traced to its precise documentary basis. No identified Halley manuscript or publication has yet verified the naming act.

6. **Exact historical membership of Chángchén.** Chinese-star identifications differ among modern reconstructions. Comparison of the Dunhuang chart, Suzhou planisphere, *Kaiyuan zhanjing*, and later official star maps is needed before treating a six- or seven-star modern list as final.

7. **Bedouin asterisms in this exact field.** No securely bounded equivalent was recovered. A focused consultation of al-Ṣūfī’s indigenous-name notices and ethnographic *anwāʾ* literature may identify small component asterisms without yielding a whole-dog counterpart.

8. **Bode’s “830 stars in 109 groups.”** This wording could not be verified as a statement in Bode’s atlas or catalogue and may belong to a modern collection database. Its originating record should be supplied before use.

9. **Full transient census.** The large number of galaxies means that a complete supernova/transient list changes continuously. A dated export from the TNS or Open Supernova Catalog would be needed for a truly exhaustive event appendix.
