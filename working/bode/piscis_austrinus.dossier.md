# Piscis Austrinus: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **Kept.** Piscis Austrinus was retained among the 88 constellations adopted by the International Astronomical Union in 1922. Eugène Delporte gave it exact, rectilinear boundaries for epoch B1875.0 in 1930. Its official abbreviation is **PsA** and its genitive is **Piscis Austrini**. No abolished Bode constellation is involved.
>
> **Principal historical change:** the ancient Southern Fish was larger. Stars on its southern tail were separated as **Grus** in the late sixteenth century, while several western stars later fell inside **Microscopium**. Fomalhaut itself was ambiguously counted by Ptolemy under both Aquarius and the Fish; modern boundaries assign it unequivocally to Piscis Austrinus.
>
> **Bode, 1801:** Bode depicts it as **Piscis Notius**, the Southern Fish, lying beneath Aquarius and receiving the stream from his urn. It is an ancient figure, not one of Bode’s inventions.

## Method and evidentiary labels

The following labels distinguish kinds of evidence:

- **Documented fact:** directly attested by a primary text, catalogue, chart, manuscript, official record, or modern scientific publication.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern conclusion inferred from comparative astronomy, philology, or historical cartography.
- **Tradition:** a historically transmitted identification whose original proof or exact first formulation is incomplete.
- **Disputed:** incompatible identifications or interpretations exist.
- **Legend/myth:** a narrative offered by an ancient or later mythographic source, not an empirical claim.
- **Modern invention:** a recent occult, popular, artistic, or nomenclatural construction.
- **Unverified:** repeated in handbooks or online sources but not traced here to an adequate early source.

A negative conclusion is important: Piscis Austrinus has only a small ancient mythographic dossier of its own. Many modern summaries enlarge it by importing myths properly belonging to zodiacal Pisces, Dagon, Oannes, Aphrodite and Eros, or Atargatis generally. Those connections are treated below but not silently presented as classical catasterisms of the Southern Fish.

### Official identity

| Item | Identification |
|---|---|
| Official name | **Piscis Austrinus** |
| Meaning | “The Southern Fish” |
| Abbreviation | **PsA** |
| Genitive | **Piscis Austrini** |
| Status | One of the 88 IAU constellations; retained in 1922 |
| Ancient status | One of Ptolemy’s 48 constellations |
| Area | About 245 square degrees; 60th in modern area rankings |
| Brightest star | **Fomalhaut**, α Piscis Austrini, visual magnitude about 1.16 |
| Messier objects | None |
| Modern neighbors | Aquarius, Capricornus, Microscopium, Grus, Sculptor |
| Conventional observing season | Northern autumn; southern spring |
| Approximate visibility | Entire figure is best south of the northern mid-latitudes; Fomalhaut is visible much farther north |

The Latin is singular: *piscis*, “fish,” plus *austrinus*, “southern.” **Piscis Australis** is a common variant meaning the same thing. “Pisces Austrinus,” although widespread, is grammatically mixed—plural *Pisces* with singular adjective *Austrinus*—and should not be treated as the official form.

### Bode’s labels

**Documented atlas evidence.** Johann Elert Bode’s *Uranographia* (Berlin, 1801) uses **PISCIS NOTIUS** on the plate: literally “Southern Fish” by way of Greek *notios*. His associated catalogue belongs to the expanded late-Enlightenment sky in which 830 stars were distributed among 109 constellation figures or groups. Piscis Notius is the classical fish, not one of the experimental figures Bode introduced or promoted.

Bode shows:

- the fish horizontally beneath Aquarius;
- its open mouth at Fomalhaut;
- Aquarius’s water ending at, or entering, the mouth;
- Grus already independent to the south;
- Microscopium occupying territory southwest of the ancient fish;
- the nearby experimental **Ballon Aerostatique**, later abolished.

No evidence was found that Bode gave Piscis Austrinus a new German proper name beyond the ordinary *Südlicher Fisch*. The Latin plate heading is the significant label.

### Modern boundaries

The IAU’s 1922 action standardized the list and three-letter abbreviations. Delporte’s 1930 *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* converted the inherited figures into mutually exclusive regions using right ascensions and declinations referred to equinox B1875.0. The resulting Piscis Austrinus is bordered:

- north by Aquarius;
- northwest by Capricornus;
- southwest by Microscopium;
- south by Grus;
- east by Sculptor.

Because precession moves the coordinate grid, the borders no longer look exactly horizontal and vertical when plotted in J2000 coordinates, but their celestial locations remain fixed. [Delporte’s 1930 publication](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ) is the controlling historical source.

## Stars and objects

### The classical fish and its stars

Ptolemy’s *Almagest* VIII lists **12 stars in the figure** and **six “unformed” stars nearby**. The detailed descriptions locate stars in the mouth, head, body, fins and tail. The surviving al-Ṣūfī manuscript tradition unusually omits the six external stars from its tabulation, although they belong to the Ptolemaic catalogue; this is a manuscript-history problem, not evidence that Ptolemy lacked them. A modern study of al-Ṣūfī manuscripts explicitly notes the omission.

The exact identification of every faint Ptolemaic entry with a modern Bayer/Flamsteed star is sometimes uncertain because:

- ancient coordinates contain observational and copying errors;
- proper motion and precession change apparent configuration;
- the figure was redrawn;
- Grus and Microscopium removed portions;
- Bayer, Lacaille and Bode did not use identical letter assignments.

Ptolemy’s catalogue gives Fomalhaut a double conceptual position: it is the bright star at the Fish’s mouth, but it also concludes Aquarius’s water. This became the later catalogue ambiguity between **α PsA** and **79 Aquarii**.

Flamsteed numbered 24 stars under Piscis Austrinus, but modern boundaries place his **1–4 Piscis Austrini in Microscopium**. Fomalhaut’s competing number, **79 Aquarii**, preserves his adherence to Ptolemy’s dual allocation.

### Bayer and later letter changes

Bayer’s *Uranometria* (1603) titled the constellation **Piscis Notius** and assigned Greek letters through approximately μ. His ordering was not a rigorously decreasing magnitude sequence. Lacaille later:

- added ν and π;
- redrew the faint outline;
- changed the use of γ, δ and ε in ways not universally accepted.

Francis Baily judged Lacaille’s ν too faint and omitted it. Gould and later cataloguers generally restored or preferred the Bayer-based identifications. Bode added τ and υ but also introduced letter-placement anomalies: notably δ/ο and γ/ξ duplications reported in later star lists. These are charting/copying issues, not additional physical stars.

### Fomalhaut: name and physical system

**Documented etymology.** *Fomalhaut* descends from Arabic **فم الحوت**, *fam al-ḥūt*, “the mouth of the fish.” With *al-janūbī*, it is “the mouth of the Southern Fish.” It translates the anatomical Ptolemaic description rather than an unrelated indigenous Arabic constellation name. Latin equivalents include:

- *Os Piscis Meridiani*;
- *Os Piscis Meridionalis*;
- *Os Piscis Notii*.

The name accumulated exceptional spelling variation in European manuscripts and printing: *Fomahant, Fumahaud, Fumahaut, Fomahand, Fontabant, Phomaut, Phomelhaut* and others. The IAU Working Group on Star Names standardized **Fomalhaut** for α PsA A in 2016.

**Documented astronomy.** Fomalhaut is a young A-type main-sequence star, approximately 25 light-years away, with a complex dusty planetary system. Its wide companions are TW Piscis Austrini and LP 876-10, making a remarkably extended triple system.

Infrared observations revealed circumstellar dust; Hubble resolved an eccentric outer belt. The belt’s sharply defined and offset appearance encouraged the inference that planets might sculpt it.

#### Fomalhaut b / Dagon

- **2004 and 2006:** Hubble recorded an optical point-like source inside the debris system.
- **2008:** Kalas and colleagues announced it as a directly imaged planetary candidate, **Fomalhaut b**.
- **Disputed, 2008–20:** its lack of a convincing infrared detection, unusual brightness and apparently belt-crossing trajectory resisted an ordinary giant-planet interpretation.
- **2015:** the IAU public NameExoWorlds process assigned it **Dagon**, after the Semitic deity. That name did not validate its physical nature.
- **2020:** reanalysis showed the source expanding and fading. Gáspár and Rieke interpreted it as a dispersing dust cloud from a collision rather than a surviving planet.
- **Present evidentiary position:** Fomalhaut b is not securely established as a planet. The observed source is best treated as a **disputed exoplanet candidate**, plausibly a transient dust cloud. Other, unseen planets may still sculpt the belts.

NASA’s account describes the reversal directly: [“Exoplanet Apparently Disappears”](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/exoplanet-apparently-disappears-in-latest-hubble-observations/). JWST subsequently resolved an inner asteroid-belt analogue, the broad intermediate disk and the outer belt, strengthening the picture of a structurally complex system without reinstating Fomalhaut b as a confirmed planet. [NASA/JWST account](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/webb/webb-looks-for-fomalhauts-asteroid-belt-and-finds-much-more/).

### “The lonely star of autumn”

**Modern observing tradition.** Fomalhaut is called the **“Lonely One,” “Lonely Star of Autumn,”** or equivalent because it shines at first magnitude in a broad northern-autumn region lacking comparably bright nearby stars. This is descriptive modern sky lore, not an ancient proper name. Aratus already noticed the isolation of the Southern Fish from the zodiacal pair, but no evidence shows that he called Fomalhaut “the lonely star.”

### Internal asterisms

The common modern outline runs from Fomalhaut around the fish’s body through ε, τ, θ, ι, μ and β, with γ and δ helping define the head/body region. It has no universally standardized named Western sub-asterism comparable to the Big Dipper.

Chinese astronomy divided the same stars among several unrelated asterisms; see below.

### Deep-sky objects

There are **no Messier objects** in Piscis Austrinus. Most catalogued deep-sky objects are faint galaxies.

Important examples include:

The apparent grouping NGC 7173/7174/7176 with nearby NGC 7172 is especially notable visually and dynamically. Online “complete” lists commonly count roughly 55 NGC/IC objects inside the border, but completeness depends on catalogue version and treatment of duplicate entries.

### Variables, transients, novae and supernovae

- **R Piscis Austrini:** Mira-type long-period variable, period about 297–98 days, reaching roughly magnitude 8 at favorable maxima.
- **S Piscis Austrini:** long-period variable.
- **TW Piscis Austrini:** BY Draconis-type spotted, rotating variable; Fomalhaut B.
- **ZZ Piscis Austrini:** contact eclipsing binary studied as a possible luminous-red-nova progenitor. “Progenitor” is a theoretical evolutionary possibility, not a prediction that an eruption is imminent.
- **Fomalhaut:** sometimes catalogued as suspected variable; any visual amplitude is slight.
- **SN 2009ip:** discovered in NGC 7259. Initially classified as a luminous blue variable eruption or supernova impostor, it underwent major outbursts in 2012. Whether its terminal 2012 event was a genuine core-collapse supernova remains disputed in the literature.
- No securely documented historical naked-eye nova or supernova is known to have been recorded specifically as occurring in the classical Fish.

### Meteor showers

The **Piscis Austrinids**, IAU shower number **183**, abbreviation **PAU**, are an **established** meteor shower in the IAU Meteor Data Center. Activity occurs in late July and early August, with a radiant around RA 23h, declination approximately −21° to −30° depending on epoch and solution, and geocentric speed near 44 km/s. Rates are low and overlap the much more conspicuous Southern Delta Aquariids and Alpha Capricornids. [IAU Meteor Data Center entry](https://ceresiaumdc.ta3.sk/shower/143).

A **gamma-Piscis Austrinid** candidate appears on the IAU working list; “working list” is not the same status as an established shower.

## Chronology of attestation

### Mesopotamian antecedent, first millennium BCE

**Scholarly reconstruction with cuneiform support.** Babylonian star lists include **MUL.KU₆**, “the Fish,” generally identified with the Southern Fish. The precise reconstruction is complicated because *ku₆/nūnu* can designate fish generically and because the Babylonian and Greek stellar outlines were not necessarily congruent.

The constellation belongs to the “watery” region of the sky surrounding Ea/Enki-associated figures, Aquarius, Capricorn and the celestial streams. It is plausible that the Greek fish beneath the Water-Pourer is inherited from this celestial geography. It is not safe, however, to claim that a surviving Babylonian myth explicitly says “Piscis Austrinus drinks Aquarius’s stream” in the Greek pictorial form.

### Egyptian antecedent

**Disputed/weakly supported.** No securely identified pharaonic Egyptian constellation has been demonstrated to be precisely the modern Piscis Austrinus. The Dendera zodiac is Greco-Roman Egyptian and reflects mixed traditions, not straightforward proof of a much earlier Egyptian Southern Fish.

The Isis-saving fish is a Greek/Roman mythographic report about Egypt or Syria. It establishes an ancient belief about a sacred fish; it does not by itself establish an indigenous Egyptian stellar figure.

### Aratus, c. 275 BCE

**First secure surviving astronomical description.** *Phaenomena* 385–88:

- the Fish lies below Capricorn;
- it faces Cetus;
- it is alone, separate from the former or zodiacal Fishes;
- people call it the Southern Fish.

At lines 389–99 Aratus describes the scattered stars of Aquarius’s water and a conspicuous bright star beneath Aquarius’s feet, the star later understood as Fomalhaut. His poem supplies the spatial tradition but no catasterism.

### Ctesias tradition, ultimately late fifth–early fourth century BCE

**Lost-source tradition.** Pseudo-Eratosthenes and Hyginus explicitly appeal to Ctesias. Ctesias of Cnidus wrote about Persia and the Near East around 400 BCE, but his work survives only in fragments and later reports. Therefore:

- the Derceto/sacred-fish narrative may be older than Aratus;
- its exact original wording and whether Ctesias himself made it a constellation story cannot be recovered;
- the catasterism may be the work of later mythographers applying Ctesias’s sacred-fish tale to the sky.

This is the earliest possible literary root of the constellation myth, but not a surviving direct attestation by Ctesias.

### Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi*, Hellenistic original, surviving epitome later

**Documented mythographic tradition; authorship/text disputed.** The extant *Catasterismi* is based on a work associated with Eratosthenes, c. 3rd century BCE, but is preserved in later epitomized/redacted forms. The Southern Fish chapter is numbered differently by editions—commonly 38 in complete constellation order, but 34 in some online arrangements.

It recounts that:

- the figure is called the Great Fish;
- Derceto fell into a lake near Bambyce in Syria;
- a great fish saved her;
- the fish was placed among the stars;
- the two zodiacal fishes are its offspring;
- Syrians honor fish and refrain from eating them;
- Ctesias is cited as authority.

The star count traditionally attached to the chapter is 12.

### Cicero and Roman Aratea, first century BCE

Cicero’s fragmentary Latin translation of Aratus rendered the Greek constellation vocabulary into Latin. **Piscis Australis** occurs in the Roman astronomical tradition, but no independent Cicero catasterism survives for it. Later illustrated *Aratea* manuscripts combined Aratean text with mythographic prose from Hyginus and related sources.

### Vitruvius, late first century BCE

In *De architectura* IX.4, Vitruvius describes the water said to pour from Aquarius as flowing between the head of the Southern Fish and the tail of Cetus. This is astronomical geography, not a myth of origin.

### Virgil, *Georgics* 4.234, c. 29 BCE

Virgil uses **Piscis aquosus** in a calendrical passage about beekeeping. **Disputed identification:** commentators have variously taken the “watery fish” as Piscis Austrinus or a zodiacal fish. The adjective alone cannot conclusively settle it. Later constellation books often cite Virgil as though the reference were certainly to the Southern Fish; it is not.

### Ovid, *Fasti* 2.457–74, c. 8 CE

Ovid tells how Dione/Venus and Cupid, fleeing Typhon, reached the Euphrates, appealed to the water nymphs, and were carried to safety by two fishes—or transformed into fishes in related forms of the story. The two became zodiacal Pisces.

**Important distinction:** this is primarily a myth of **Pisces**, not Piscis Austrinus. It is relevant only because pseudo-Eratosthenes makes the two zodiacal fish children of the Great/Southern Fish and because all three traditions use Syrian sacred-fish lore. It must not be quoted as Ovid’s catasterism of Piscis Austrinus.

### Manilius, *Astronomica*, early first century CE

Manilius treats constellation risings and zodiacal influence. His fish passages primarily concern zodiacal Pisces. No independent Southern-Fish catasterism comparable to Hyginus 2.41 was located. Claims that Manilius relates the complete Derceto-Isis story to Piscis Austrinus appear to be conflations with other mythographers.

### Hyginus, *De astronomia/Poetica astronomica* 2.41, first century BCE–first century CE

**Documented primary account.** Hyginus writes:

> *qui notius appellatur. Hic videtur ore aquam excipere a signo Aquarii.*

That is, “the one called southern; it seems to receive in its mouth the water from the sign of Aquarius.”

He continues that the Fish:

- once saved **Isis** when she was in distress;
- was placed among the stars with its children as a reward;
- explains why many Syrians do not eat fishes;
- explains their worship of gilded fish images as household gods;
- is also treated by Ctesias.

This is the clearest ancient text for the drinking image and the Isis variant. [Latin text, Hyginus 2.41](https://www.lateinlex.de/?call=Puc&permalink=Hyg_astr_2_41).

### Hyginus, *Fabulae*

The *Fabulae* includes related fish and Syrian material, particularly the Aphrodite/Venus-and-Cupid tradition associated with Pisces. No separate, fuller Southern-Fish narrative securely surpassing *Astronomica* 2.41 was found. References to “Hyginus, *Fabulae* 197” normally concern Pisces and the Typhon escape, not the Fish drinking from Aquarius.

### Ptolemy, c. 137–50 CE

**Documented scientific catalogue.** In *Almagest* VII–VIII Ptolemy catalogues the figure as **Ἰχθύς Νότιος**. He enumerates 12 formed and six external stars. The mouth star corresponds to Fomalhaut. The catalogue’s bodily descriptions stabilized the figure’s astronomical anatomy for Arabic and European astronomy.

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* I.9 assigns the bright star in the mouth of the Southern Fish the mixed astrological temperament of **Venus and Mercury**. That is the foundational classical astrological statement about Fomalhaut; much later character lore should not be attributed to Ptolemy.

### Diodorus Siculus, *Bibliotheca historica* 2.4.2, first century BCE

Diodorus, using Ctesian material, describes Derceto near Ascalon:

- she loved a young man;
- bore a child;
- killed or abandoned the lover and exposed the child;
- threw herself into a lake in shame;
- became fish-shaped or was represented with fish characteristics;
- Syrian abstention from fish followed.

This is not explicitly a constellation passage. It supplies the broader mythic biography of the goddess whom the *Catasterismi* links to the Great Fish.

### Lucian, *De dea Syria* 14, second century CE

Lucian reports the Syrian goddess at Hierapolis/Bambyce and discusses a sanctuary tradition connected with Derceto/Atargatis, fish and water. He distinguishes images: at Phoenician sites Derceto could be represented with a woman’s upper part and fish below, whereas the Hierapolitan image was entirely woman-shaped. Again this is evidence for cult and iconography, not a direct catasterism.

### Pausanias, Pseudo-Apollodorus and Nonnus

- **Pausanias:** no secure passage was found assigning Piscis Austrinus to the sky. His descriptions of Syrian or fish-related cults do not supply a Southern-Fish catasterism.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*:** the Typhon conflict and divine transformations provide context for the flight of Aphrodite, but no surviving passage was located that identifies the Southern Fish specifically.
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*:** contains elaborate Syrian, aquatic and Typhon-related myth, but no securely traceable independent catasterism of Piscis Austrinus was found.

Claims that these authors tell “the myth of Piscis Austrinus” require passage-level proof and should be considered **unverified** unless such a citation is produced.

### Scholia to Aratus and Germanicus

The Aratean scholia accumulated the Derceto/Great Fish material and constellation identifications around Aratus’s terse description. Germanicus’s Latin *Aratea* and its scholia transmitted the Southern Fish as a separate figure beneath Aquarius.

Because the scholia exist in multiple recensions and often incorporate pseudo-Eratosthenic and Hyginian material, a statement occurring “in the scholia” cannot automatically be dated to the third century BCE. The individual manuscript recension must be specified. The surviving tradition reinforces, rather than independently proves, the Derceto catasterism.

### al-Ṣūfī, 964 CE

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita*, “Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars,” translated and corrected the Ptolemaic sky. For the Southern Fish he distinguishes:

- the learned Ptolemaic image, **al-Ḥūt al-Janūbī**;
- star names and observations from Arabic usage;
- a diagram shown twice, as seen on the sky and as seen on a celestial globe.

Fomalhaut is the mouth, *fam al-ḥūt*. The six external Ptolemaic stars are curiously absent from known copies of this constellation’s table, although the *Almagest* has them. That uniform omission may derive from an early archetype of al-Ṣūfī’s text.

### Medieval Latin transmission, twelfth–fifteenth centuries

Gerard of Cremona’s Latin *Almagest*, c. 1175, calls it *Stellatio Piscis meridionalis*. Arabic anatomical descriptions passed into Latin star nomenclature. A 1340 almanac reportedly gives **Fom Alhout Algenubi** beside *Os Piscis Meridiani*.

Illustrated Latin al-Ṣūfī manuscripts reproduce the fish, often swimming horizontally. The early-fifteenth-century Gotha manuscript Membr. II 141 shows it swimming toward the viewer’s left.

### Dürer, Stabius and Heinfogel, 1515

The printed celestial maps designed by Conrad Heinfogel and Johannes Stabius and engraved by Albrecht Dürer include the Ptolemaic constellation tradition. Piscis Austrinus appears beneath Aquarius in the southern map. Dürer’s prints were crucial in circulating the classical figures in a reproducible Renaissance format.

### Mercator, 1551

Gerardus Mercator’s celestial globe represents the Southern Fish in the inherited drinking position. Globes reverse orientation relative to sky-view maps because they are viewed externally.

### Plancius, Keyser and de Houtman, 1597–98

**Boundary-changing event, not invention of Piscis Austrinus.** Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman observed the far-southern sky during Dutch voyages. Petrus Plancius used the observations on globes, and **Grus**, the Crane, emerged as a separate constellation.

Stars formerly treated as the extended tail or southern appendage of Piscis Austrinus were assigned to Grus. The most conspicuous surviving marker is **γ Gruis**, associated in older discussions with the fish’s tail.

Dating alternates between 1597 and 1598 because it may refer to the globe’s preparation, publication or surviving edition.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* plate **Piscis Notius**:

- preserves the fish drinking from Aquarius;
- gives Bayer letters to its prominent stars;
- accepts Grus as separate;
- labels the figure with the classical alternatives *Piscis Notius, Meridianus,* and *Austrinus* rather than inventing a new fish.

The Huntington Library identifies the surviving printed object as [“Piscis Notius in Uranometria, Johannes Bayer, 1603”](https://www.huntington.org/uranometria).

### Julius Schiller, 1627

**Documented Christian reinterpretation.** In the *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Schiller replaced pagan constellation imagery with biblical figures and objects. Piscis Austrinus became:

**Hydriae Farinae Sareptanae Viduae** — the **meal/flour vessel or barrel of the widow of Zarephath**.

The biblical source is 1 Kings 17:8–16: Elijah promises that the widow’s jar/barrel of meal and cruse of oil will not fail during the drought.

The lead “St Matthew’s barrel” is therefore **incorrect**. Schiller did not assign St Matthew to Piscis Austrinus. The correct object is the Zarephath widow’s flour vessel. Schiller’s Christian sky was an intentional seventeenth-century substitution and did not replace the conventional astronomical name.

### Hevelius, 1687/1690

Johannes Hevelius’s *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* was engraved before his death and published posthumously in 1690. Plate BBB pairs **Piscis Notius et Grus**, making the territorial separation visually explicit. [Hevelius plate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AJohannes_Hevelius_-_Prodromus_Astronomia_-_Volume_III_%22Firmamentum_Sobiescianum%2C_sive_uranographia%22_-_Tavola_BBB_-_Piscis_Notius_et_Grus.jpg).

### Flamsteed, 1725/1729

Flamsteed’s catalogue and atlas use **Piscis Austrinus**. His numbering created the still-encountered designations 1–24 PsA, but:

- 1–4 PsA now lie in Microscopium;
- Fomalhaut is both 24 PsA and 79 Aqr in historical cataloguing.

The double designation embodies rather than resolves Ptolemy’s ambiguity.

### Lacaille, 1750s

Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille’s southern observations and 1756 constellation plan established **Microscopium** southwest of Piscis Austrinus and reduced or regularized the Fish’s territory. He used **Piscis Australis** and altered some letter assignments. His new scientific-instrument constellation appropriated stars that older maps could show as unformed stars or parts of the extended fish region.

### Bode, 1782 and 1801

Bode’s 1782 popular atlas and monumental 1801 *Uranographia* synthesize Bayer, Flamsteed, Lacaille and later catalogues. In 1801:

- the classical figure appears as **Piscis Notius**;
- Fomalhaut marks its mouth;
- Aquarius’s stream physically meets it;
- τ and υ appear in Bode’s expanded lettering;
- the fish is surrounded by both accepted and experimental constellations.

Bode did not claim to invent the figure. Its inclusion in a collection of 109 figures reflects his willingness to chart both established and proposed constellations; it does not mean all 109 possessed equal historical or later official status.

### Jamieson, 1822; *Urania’s Mirror*, 1824/25

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* drew **Piscis Australis** with Aquarius and the fashionable **Ballon Aerostatique**. *Urania’s Mirror* plate 26 copied the basic composition and used pinholes for the stars. The cards were first advertised in 1824 and issued in the mid-1820s; their once-anonymous “lady” designer was later identified by Peter Hingley as probably the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam.

### Burritt, 1830s; Argelander and Heis, nineteenth century

Elijah H. Burritt’s *Geography of the Heavens* and associated atlas popularized the classical fish in American education. Friedrich Wilhelm Argelander’s and Eduard Heis’s atlases increasingly prioritized measured stars and practical outlines over elaborate mythological engraving. The fish remained, but its iconography lost scientific authority as catalogue astronomy separated constellation regions from pictures.

### IAU, 1922 and Delporte, 1930

The IAU retained the constellation and standardized **PsA**. Delporte’s boundaries finally ended the old question of whether a star belonged pictorially to Aquarius, Piscis Austrinus, Grus or Microscopium: ownership became a coordinate matter.

## Classical myths and legends

### The Fish drinking Aquarius’s water

**Astronomical image; first secure description in the Hellenistic/Roman tradition.** Aratus places the Fish beneath Aquarius and describes the water stars nearby but does not quite narrate drinking. Hyginus 2.41 makes it explicit: the Fish “seems to receive in its mouth the water” from Aquarius.

This image is consequently:

- **documented** by Hyginus;
- implicit in the spatial arrangement of Aratus;
- perpetuated in Ptolemaic star anatomy;
- repeatedly drawn by Bayer, Hevelius, Bode and nineteenth-century atlases.

No god is said to have placed it there merely so it could drink. The drinking posture is astronomical; the catasterism is supplied separately by the Isis/Derceto story.

### Derceto saved near Bambyce

**Myth; earliest possible source Ctesias, c. 400 BCE; surviving catasterismic form pseudo-Eratosthenic.**

Derceto falls into a lake near Bambyce in Syria. A great fish saves her. In gratitude or commemoration the fish is placed in heaven. Its offspring become the two zodiacal fishes. Syrian reverence for fish and abstention from eating them are explained as consequences.

Who places it in heaven is not consistently named in surviving summaries; the passive “was placed” is typical catasterismic language and implies divine celestial commemoration without identifying Zeus in every recension.

“Bambyce” is the Greek name associated with Hierapolis in Syria. Derceto is related to, or a Greek form of, the Syrian goddess Atargatis, though exact equations among Derceto, Atargatis and the “Syrian Goddess” vary by author and period.

### Isis saved by the Fish

**Myth; first surviving explicit constellation version Hyginus 2.41.**

Hyginus substitutes **Isis** for Derceto. The Fish saves her “when in distress” (*laborantem*). She places—or causes to be placed—the fish and its children among the stars as recompense. The Syrians consequently spare fish and honor gilded images.

This may represent:

- a syncretic identification of Egyptian Isis with the Syrian goddess;
- a variant in Hyginus’s source;
- or later mythographic rationalization.

It should not be reported as a securely recovered pharaonic Egyptian star myth. Its evidence is Roman mythography citing Ctesias.

### Parent of zodiacal Pisces

**Mythographic tradition; pseudo-Eratosthenes and Hyginus.** The two fishes of Pisces are said to be the offspring of the Great/Southern Fish. This genealogical device connects three distinct constellations and explains why the lone fish is “great.”

This is not the only ancient origin of Pisces. Other accounts make the two fish:

- rescuers of Aphrodite and Eros;
- the transformed Aphrodite and Eros themselves;
- fish associated with a miraculous Euphrates egg and the Syrian goddess.

The Great Fish genealogy and the Typhon escape coexist rather than forming one internally consistent canonical myth.

### Derceto/Atargatis’s fuller biography

**Myth, not itself a catasterism.** Diodorus Siculus 2.4.2, drawing on Ctesias, says Derceto fell in love with a beautiful youth, bore a daughter, killed or destroyed the youth in shame, exposed the infant and threw herself into a lake near Ascalon. The child, nourished by doves, became Semiramis. Derceto’s fish form and local fish taboo followed.

This story matters because it explains why later Greek writers could attach a sacred saving fish to Derceto. But Diodorus’s version—in which Derceto becomes fishlike—is not identical to the *Catasterismi* version in which a separate fish saves her.

### Atargatis’s fish-bodied image

**Cult tradition.** Lucian, *De dea Syria* 14, reports that the Phoenician image of Derceto has a woman’s upper half and fish below, while the Hierapolitan Syrian Goddess is entirely woman-shaped. The testimony shows regional iconographic diversity. It does not prove that the stellar Fish represents the goddess herself; pseudo-Eratosthenes represents it as her rescuer.

### Aphrodite/Venus and Eros/Cupid fleeing Typhon

**Myth primarily of zodiacal Pisces.** Ovid, *Fasti* 2.457–74, places Dione/Venus and Cupid at the Euphrates while Typhon threatens the gods. Fish bear them to safety, or in related versions the gods become fish. The rescuers receive stellar honor.

Hyginus’s Pisces chapter attributes a version to Diognetus of Erythrae. The story belongs to the two zodiacal fish. Its relevance to Piscis Austrinus is secondary: all are woven into Syrian sacred-fish etiologies, and the Great Fish is elsewhere called their parent.

### The Euphrates egg

**Syrian-goddess legend, later connected to Pisces.** Two fish find or push ashore an egg in the Euphrates; doves warm it and the Syrian goddess is born. Fish are rewarded with stellar placement. This story explains fish and dove sanctity and belongs to the larger Atargatis/Pisces complex. It is not independently assigned to the Southern Fish in the strongest surviving sources.

### Dagon and Oannes

**Antiquarian speculation, not classical catasterism.**

Nineteenth-century writers proposed that Piscis Austrinus represented:

- **Dagon**, interpreted as a fish god;
- **Oannes**, the Mesopotamian amphibious sage;
- Egyptian sacred fishes such as the oxyrhynchus or phagrus.

These equations are weak:

1. Dagon’s name is now generally derived from a Semitic word connected with grain, not securely from *dāg*, “fish.”
2. Ancient Dagon was not consistently represented as a merman.
3. No surviving Mesopotamian text directly identifies Fomalhaut or MUL.KU₆ as Dagon.
4. Oannes belongs to Berossian traditions about antediluvian sages; no primary source identifies him with Piscis Austrinus.

The 2015 exoplanet name **Dagon** was a modern public-naming choice inspired by the popular fish-god association. It does not authenticate the historical identification.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Arabic learned and Bedouin skies

al-Ṣūfī preserves the distinction between the inherited Greek constellation and indigenous Arabic star lore.

- **Learned figure:** *al-Ḥūt al-Janūbī*, the Southern Fish.
- **Fomalhaut:** *fam al-ḥūt*, the fish’s mouth.
- **Fomalhaut and β Ceti:** *al-Ḍifdiʿ al-Awwal* and *al-Ḍifdiʿ al-Thānī*, First and Second Frog.
- Some globe inscriptions call a star in the tail region **dhanab al-ḥūt**, “tail of the fish.”
- A name interpreted as **ẓalīm**, “ostrich,” occurs in the region on some Islamic globes.

These Arab asterisms do not necessarily reproduce a single fish. “Arabic astronomy” must therefore be divided into the Arabic-language Ptolemaic scientific tradition and oral/Bedouin stellar groupings.

### Persian tradition and the “four royal stars”

**Major controversy.** Popular astrology calls Fomalhaut one of four ancient Persian “Royal Stars” or “Watchers,” assigning:

- Aldebaran—East;
- Regulus—North;
- Antares—West;
- Fomalhaut—South.

Fomalhaut is often called **Hastorang/Haftorang** in this scheme.

The evidence is poor and internally contradictory. **Haftōrang**, “Seven Thrones,” is an Iranian name for the seven stars of **Ursa Major**, not securely Fomalhaut. Modern Italian reference material explicitly notes that eighteenth-century writers erroneously transferred Hastorang to Fomalhaut.

The four-star cardinal scheme is best labeled a **late antiquarian or modern astrological construction using genuine Iranian star names but questionable identifications**. It was spread especially through nineteenth-century star-name compilations and later occult astrology. Fomalhaut’s seasonal prominence and approximate ancient relation to a solstitial quarter made the association attractive, but no Avestan passage securely calls Fomalhaut Haftorang or “Watcher of the South.”

### Chinese astronomy

The lead “Chinese Northern Dipper” is **false**. The Northern Dipper, **Běidǒu**, is the seven-star Big Dipper in Ursa Major.

The stars of Piscis Austrinus belonged to several Chinese asterisms:

- **北落師門, Běiluòshīmén**, “North Gate of the Military Camp”: Fomalhaut alone. The name refers to a northern gate of the encampment, not the northern celestial hemisphere.
- **天綱, Tiāngāng**, “Celestial Net/Rope”: associated in modern naming with δ PsA.
- Stars of the region were also apportioned among **天錢, Tiānqián**, “Celestial Money,” and **羽林軍, Yǔlínjūn**, the Imperial Guards, depending on reconstruction.

These form part of the **Shi**, “Encampment,” lunar mansion. The Chinese sky contains no single Fish equivalent covering the modern Western boundary.

### Indian astronomy

No major nakshatra is defined by Fomalhaut. The Indian lunar mansions lie close to the ecliptic; most of Piscis Austrinus lies too far south. Claims that Fomalhaut is the Hindu equivalent of Avestan Tishtrya or Pushya are erroneous:

- Tishtrya is normally associated with Sirius in Iranian tradition;
- Pushya is an Indian lunar mansion in Cancer;
- neither securely designates Fomalhaut.

Fomalhaut can occur in later Indian or Persianate star catalogues derived from Greco-Arabic astronomy, but no indigenous classical Indian “Southern Fish” was verified.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No ancient Hebrew Bible or rabbinic passage was found that securely maps these specific stars as a Southern Fish. **Dagim**, “Fishes,” is the Hebrew name for zodiacal Pisces and the month Adar; it should not be transferred automatically to Piscis Austrinus.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century “Gospel in the Stars” or *Mazzaroth* literature sometimes makes the Southern Fish represent:

- the redeemed multitude;
- the Church receiving living water;
- Israel or the assembly of the Messiah.

These are **modern Christian-Hebraizing inventions**, often presented as if they preserved primordial Hebrew astronomy. No ancient Hebrew star list supports them.

No stable medieval Kabbalistic mapping specifically to Piscis Austrinus was located. General correspondences among zodiac signs, Hebrew letters and divine names concern zodiacal Pisces, not this extra-zodiacal constellation.

### Alchemical and Hermetic interpretations

No classical Hermetic or medieval alchemical text was found that assigns a distinct canonical meaning to Piscis Austrinus. Later esoteric readings interpret:

- Aquarius’s stream as divine or mercurial water;
- the fish as receptive soul or prima materia;
- Fomalhaut as vision, initiation or spiritual purity.

These are **later symbolic applications**, not demonstrated ancient constellation doctrine.

Fomalhaut is **not one of the traditional fifteen Behenian fixed stars** in the standard medieval list. Modern magical lists that treat it as Behenian are erroneous or deliberately expanded systems.

### Astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* I.9 is the primary classical testimony: the bright star in the mouth of the Southern Fish has a **Venus–Mercury** nature.

Later astrologers attribute to Fomalhaut:

- fame, ideals, eloquence and artistic gifts;
- success dependent on moral integrity;
- spectacular rise followed by downfall if corrupted;
- mystical or visionary aptitude.

These elaborate moral formulas are **post-classical tradition**, especially prominent in early-modern and modern fixed-star manuals. They should not be backdated to Ptolemy.

Piscis Austrinus is not zodiacal, so it has no zodiac sign rulership. The Chinese Encampment mansion crosses the region, but Western lunar-mansion systems usually use ecliptic stars elsewhere; there is no standard Arabic manzil defined by Fomalhaut.

## Iconography

### Antiquity

#### Farnese Atlas, Roman copy of a Hellenistic prototype, c. second century CE

The Farnese Atlas bears a relief celestial globe with many constellation figures. Identifying faint or damaged southern figures individually is difficult, and published reconstructions do not always agree. The globe is important evidence for the classical pictorial system, but a precise, uncontested Southern Fish identification on its surface should not be asserted without a dedicated relief study.

#### Dendera zodiac, Roman Egypt, first century BCE/CE

Fish figures occur on the Dendera ceiling zodiac, but it is a mixed Egyptian–Greco-Roman monument. Its usefulness as proof of a specifically pharaonic Piscis Austrinus is disputed.

### Islamic manuscripts

al-Ṣūfī manuscripts normally provide paired images:

- the figure as seen on the celestial globe;
- the mirror-reversed figure as seen in the sky.

Piscis Austrinus is a large fish, commonly horizontal, with its mouth emphasized by the bright star. Surviving manuscripts differ in color, fins, scales and orientation. The early-fifteenth-century Gotha “Sufi Latinus” manuscript depicts it swimming leftward.

### Medieval Aratea

The **Leiden Aratea**, made c. 816 in the Carolingian world, includes a single Southern Fish in the inherited late-antique style. Other Aratea manuscripts combine the image with text from Aratus, Germanicus, Hyginus or scholia. Their pictures are evidence for medieval transmission, not necessarily faithful copies of a single ancient original.

### Dürer’s celestial map, Nuremberg, 1515

Dürer’s southern-hemisphere woodcut places the Fish below Aquarius within a Ptolemaic arrangement. The map was produced with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel and became one of Europe’s first widely distributed printed star maps.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer’s *Piscis Notius* is a naturalistic engraved fish with open mouth, receiving Aquarius’s flow. The stellar grid and Bayer letters make the illustration simultaneously artistic and operational.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

Schiller suppresses the fish and substitutes the flour vessel of the widow of Zarephath. This is one of the clearest examples of deliberate Christianization of a classical nonhuman constellation.

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Hevelius pairs **Piscis Notius et Grus**. His maps are globe-oriented, so they reverse the sky view. The plate documents the now-permanent separation of the Crane from the Fish.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s copperplate gives the Fish a long body, broad fins and gaping mouth exactly where Aquarius’s stream terminates. Because Bode plots many more faint stars than Bayer or Hevelius, the image is embedded in a much denser measured sky. Its artistic anatomy does not define the later IAU boundary.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson retains the fish-and-water composition and draws boundary lines among constellations following Bode’s cartographic tendency. His figure is more pictorial than later utilitarian atlases.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/25

Card 26 combines **Aquarius, Piscis Australis and Ballon Aerostatique**. The cards derive closely from Jamieson and perforate the principal stars so they appear luminous when held against light. [Historical description of the cards](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/16.htm).

### Burritt, United States, nineteenth century

Burritt’s atlas carries the fish into mass astronomical education. Its engravings helped naturalize *Piscis Australis* in American vernacular astronomy even as scientific catalogues preferred Latin.

### Argelander and Heis

Their nineteenth-century atlases reduce the mythological figure’s dominance and emphasize star magnitudes, coordinate utility and naked-eye observing. This marks the transition from constellation picture to standardized sky region.

### Coins, monuments, portals and ceilings

No ancient coin type was securely located that can be identified specifically as Piscis Austrinus rather than a generic fish, zodiacal Pisces, dolphin or marine deity.

Likewise, medieval cathedral zodiac portals normally portray **Pisces**, the zodiacal pair, not the extra-zodiacal Southern Fish. Claims that a single fish on a church ceiling is Piscis Austrinus require accompanying labels or a complete constellation program.

Planetarium ceilings and early-modern painted celestial rooms may reproduce the figure from printed atlases, but no uniquely influential building named for Piscis Austrinus was verified.

## People, events and works

### Navigation

Fomalhaut’s brightness and isolation made it useful as a southern seasonal and directional marker. Polynesian navigators knew it under regional names, including Māori **Ō-tama-rākau** and Hawaiian traditions associated with **Kūkaniloko**, although mappings vary between islands and sources.

A strong claim that one particular European explorer “steered by Fomalhaut” requires a logbook citation. The Dutch voyages associated with Keyser and de Houtman changed the mapped neighborhood through observation of Grus, but no specific navigation episode centered on Fomalhaut was located.

### Astronomers and observers

- **Ptolemy:** catalogued the constellation’s stars.
- **al-Ṣūfī:** corrected magnitudes and transmitted Arabic and Ptolemaic nomenclature.
- **Bayer:** assigned durable letter designations.
- **Flamsteed:** supplied numbered designations and preserved Fomalhaut’s Aquarius ambiguity.
- **Lacaille:** remapped the southern field and introduced Microscopium.
- **Bode:** synthesized the eighteenth-century enlarged sky.
- **Paul Kalas and collaborators:** announced Fomalhaut b in 2008.
- **András Gáspár and George Rieke:** led the 2020 interpretation of Fomalhaut b as an expanding collision cloud.
- **JWST/MIRI team:** resolved the system’s multiple dust structures.

### Literature and popular culture

- **H. P. Lovecraft** uses Fomalhaut in the astronomical atmosphere surrounding his fiction and correspondence, though it is not a central named setting comparable to Aldebaran.
- **Philip K. Dick’s *VALIS*** associates Fomalhaut with visionary extraterrestrial communication. This is modern fiction, not astronomical tradition.
- **Ursula K. Le Guin’s Hainish universe** includes planets and systems attached to named stars, but claims of a central Piscis Austrinus setting should be checked work by work.
- Fomalhaut’s resolved dust-ring images prompted the modern nickname **“Eye of Sauron.”** This is a late-twentieth-/twenty-first-century visual analogy to Tolkien’s emblem, not an official name.
- Poems and amateur astronomy writing commonly exploit the “lonely autumn star” motif. No historically canonical poem titled *Piscis Austrinus* was identified.

### Ships, missions and institutional names

No major astronomical spacecraft has been officially named Piscis Austrinus or Fomalhaut. Fomalhaut has been used for ships and fictional spacecraft in scattered modern contexts, but a defensible comprehensive registry was not located. The scientifically important “missions attached to it” are observational: Hubble, Spitzer, Herschel, ALMA and JWST observations of the Fomalhaut system.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Piscis Austrinus or Piscis Australis?

Both are good Latin. The IAU form is **Piscis Austrinus**. *Piscis Australis* remains historically common. **Pisces Austrinus** is a grammatical and cataloguing error, though common enough to appear in book titles and databases.

### 2. Was it Babylonian?

**Probable scholarly identification, not complete identity.** MUL.KU₆, “the Fish,” is usually matched to Piscis Austrinus. The evidence supports inheritance or adaptation, but exact star-by-star continuity is not proven.

### 3. Was it Egyptian?

Only in a qualified sense. Hyginus supplies an Isis myth; Greco-Roman Egyptian monuments contain fish imagery. No securely mapped native Egyptian constellation identical to Piscis Austrinus has been established.

### 4. Isis or Derceto?

Both are ancient variants:

- pseudo-Eratosthenes: **Derceto**;
- Hyginus: **Isis**, while citing Ctesias and explaining Syrian customs.

They may reflect syncretism. Neither should erase the other.

### 5. Did the fish save the goddess, or was the goddess herself a fish?

Both motifs occur in related sources:

- constellation myth: a separate great fish saves Derceto/Isis;
- Diodorus/Lucian cult myth: Derceto becomes or is represented as part fish.

Later retellings merge them, but the ancient witnesses are not identical.

### 6. Is it the parent of Pisces?

Yes, in the pseudo-Eratosthenic/Hyginian lineage. No, not in every ancient Pisces myth. It is one competing genealogy.

### 7. Was Fomalhaut Ptolemy’s Aquarius star?

It was both the Fish’s mouth and the endpoint of Aquarius’s water. Flamsteed’s 79 Aqr and Bayer’s α PsA preserve the ambiguity. Modern IAU boundaries place it in Piscis Austrinus.

### 8. Did Grus steal the Fish’s tail?

In broad historical terms, yes. The region later mapped as Grus included stars treated as an extended southern part or tail of the Fish. However, ancient “constellation boundaries” did not exist as closed polygons, so “steal” is pictorial shorthand rather than an exact transfer of territorial jurisdiction.

### 9. Did Microscopium take stars from it?

Yes in the catalogue/territorial sense. Lacaille’s new Microscopium occupied the southwestern field; Flamsteed’s 1–4 PsA now lie there. Some were better understood historically as external or unformed stars rather than essential fish anatomy.

### 10. Is Fomalhaut a Persian Royal Star called Hastorang?

**Probably a historical misidentification.** The popular four-watchers scheme is late and poorly sourced. Iranian **Haftorang** properly denotes Ursa Major, “Seven Thrones.” Identifying it with Fomalhaut appears in eighteenth-/nineteenth-century antiquarian literature and was amplified by modern astrology.

### 11. Is Fomalhaut b a planet?

**Disputed; currently not confirmed.** It was announced as a directly imaged candidate in 2008. Its fading and extension led to the 2020 collision-cloud interpretation. The formal name Dagon does not override physical uncertainty.

### 12. Is Dagon a fish god represented by the constellation?

**Disputed and largely obsolete.** Fish-man Dagon derives substantially from older etymological and iconographic mistakes. No primary evidence joins Dagon to Piscis Austrinus. The exoplanet name was a modern cultural allusion.

### 13. Is Fomalhaut Behenian?

No in the standard medieval list of fifteen Behenian stars. Its inclusion is a modern expansion or error.

### 14. Is the Chinese equivalent the Northern Dipper?

No. Fomalhaut is **Běiluòshīmén**, North Gate of the Military Camp. The Northern Dipper is in Ursa Major.

### 15. Did Schiller make it St Matthew’s barrel?

No. He made it the **flour/meal vessel of the widow of Zarephath**, *Hydriae Farinae Sareptanae Viduae*. The biblical actors are the widow and Elijah. St Matthew is unrelated.

### 16. “The loneliest star in the universe”

This is poetic hyperbole. Fomalhaut is not physically alone: it has two wide stellar companions and a populous debris system. “Lonely star of autumn” describes its apparent isolation among bright stars.

### 17. Bode’s duplicate letters

Bode’s atlas is cited for δ/ο and γ/ξ label anomalies. These are historical nomenclatural problems, not discoveries of separate stars. Modern Bayer usage follows standardized identifications rather than every mark on Bode’s plate.

### 18. “One of Ptolemy’s 48” but also “invented in 1598”

The 1598 date belongs to **Grus**, not Piscis Austrinus. The Fish is certainly ancient. Confusion arises because modern summaries discuss the separation of Grus immediately after describing Piscis Austrinus.

## Open questions

1. **Babylonian extent:** Which exact modern stars belonged to each occurrence of MUL.KU₆, and how directly did the Greek outline descend from it?

2. **Ctesias’s original wording:** Did Ctesias connect the saving fish to a constellation, or did a Hellenistic catasterist make the astronomical application?

3. **Isis/Derceto transmission:** Was “Isis” an early syncretic variant, a Hyginian substitution, or evidence of a lost common mythographic source?

4. **al-Ṣūfī’s missing external stars:** At what stage were the six Ptolemaic “unformed” entries lost from the manuscript tradition?

5. **Ancient tail:** Star-by-star reconstruction of the transition from Ptolemy’s external stars through Bayer’s Fish and the creation of Grus remains dependent on competing identifications.

6. **Bode’s catalogue typography:** A plate-by-plate collation of all surviving 1801 impressions would clarify whether the δ/ο and γ/ξ anomalies are authorial, engraver’s errors, or edition-state corrections.

7. **Persian Royal Stars:** The exact eighteenth- or nineteenth-century publication that first equated Fomalhaut with Hastorang/Haftorang deserves a dedicated bibliographic genealogy. The equation is repeated much more often than it is evidenced.

8. **Historical vernacular star names:** Persian, Hebrew, Indian and Polynesian claims require local-language manuscript or ethnographic sources, not retrospective global star-name tables.

9. **SN 2009ip:** Continued observations of NGC 7259 are required to decide whether the progenitor survived the 2012 event.

10. **Fomalhaut planets:** The dust belts strongly motivate planet searches, but no planet in the system should presently be described as securely confirmed solely from the former Fomalhaut b observations.
