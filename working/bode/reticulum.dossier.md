# Reticulum: Research Dossier

> **Status — documented fact:** **Reticulum is one of the 88 constellations retained by the International Astronomical Union.** Its official abbreviation is **Ret**, its Latin genitive **Reticuli**, and its English gloss **the Reticle**. The IAU adopted the constellation list and three-letter abbreviations at Rome in 1922; Eugène Delporte’s boundaries, approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930, remain authoritative. Reticulum was **not abolished**, and consequently its stars did not pass en bloc to another constellation.  
> [IAU constellation history and official table](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [IAU Style Manual, table 11](https://iauarchive.eso.org/static/publications/stylemanual1989.pdf)

## Method and evidentiary labels

The dossier uses these labels:

- **Documented fact:** directly attested by a contemporary object, publication, catalogue, archive, or modern scientific record.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion inferred by historians or astronomers from surviving records.
- **Tradition:** a transmitted cultural identification, whether or not empirically demonstrable.
- **Disputed claim:** a claim for which substantial contrary evidence or competing attribution exists.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in secondary literature but not located in a dependable primary source.
- **Legend/myth:** a narrative presented as lore rather than historical or scientific fact.
- **Modern invention:** an explicitly modern constellation, renaming, myth, or popular association.

Reticulum is a modern figure. There is therefore no authentic Greek or Roman Reticulum myth to reconstruct. Its historical dossier is chiefly one of instrument-making, southern-sky surveying, cartography, rival figures, and twentieth-century UFO folklore.

**Documented fact:** The official Latin name means “little net” or “net-work.” Latin *reticulum* is the diminutive of *rete*, “net.” In early-modern optics, French *réticule*, English *reticle*, German *Fadenkreuz* and related terms designate a network or crossing of fine lines at or near an optical instrument’s focal plane. The constellation represents such a measuring grid, not a fishing net.

### Lacaille’s complete name

In his memoir printed in 1756, Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille called the figure:

> **Le Réticule rhomboïde**

He explained it as the small astronomical instrument used in making the catalogue, constructed through intersecting straight lines drawn within a square. In the posthumous *Coelum australe stelliferum* of 1763 it appears in Latin as **Reticulus** or **Reticulus rhomboidalis**, depending on the heading or descriptive context. Later astronomical usage regularized the neuter noun as **Reticulum**.

**Documented fact:** the first printed appearance is in the Académie royale des sciences volume nominally “for 1752” but actually published in **1756**. Thus “introduced in 1752” is conventional shorthand for the observing/catalogue epoch; **1756 is the publication date**.  
[Lacaille catalogue and publication analysis](https://pbarbier.com/lacaille/lacaille.html); [1756 planisphere record](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/planisphere-containing-celestial-constellations.html); [1763 facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/152572)

### Bode’s labels

**Documented fact:** Johann Elert Bode’s *Uranographia* was published at Berlin in **1801**, with twenty large engraved plates and an accompanying bilingual German/French descriptive catalogue. On the southern plate Reticulum is labelled **RETICULUS**. The image is an actual rhomboidal eyepiece grid rather than an allegorical person or animal.

Bode’s associated text belongs to the *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* / *Description et connaissance générale des constellations*, which catalogues 17,240 stars, doubles, nebulae, and clusters. Reticulum is treated as one of Lacaille’s instrumental southern constellations.  
[Bode’s *Uranographia*, digital edition](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148); [companion catalogue record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

**Iconographic caution:** A modern Spanish commentary observes that Bode shaded one quadrant differently from Lacaille’s construction and suggests an artistic misunderstanding of which part was solid metal. This is a plausible comparison of the engravings, not an explanation found in Bode’s text.  
[Spanish plate discussion](https://www.tayabeixo.org/constelaciones/Constelacion_Reticulum.htm)

### Boundaries

**Documented fact:** Delporte’s original official boundaries were defined in straight segments of right ascension and declination for equinox **B1875.0**. Modern IAU coordinate files precess those vertices to J2000. Reticulum is an irregular polygon covering about 113.94 square degrees, centred near RA 3h55m, Dec −60°.

The IAU’s boundary process was not an attempt to preserve pictures exactly. It was designed to assign every point in the sky unambiguously and, where possible, retain already named variable stars in their traditional constellation.  
[IAU explanation](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [Delporte bibliographic record](https://biblio-n.oca.eu/biblio/pmb3.0/opac_css/index.php?id=12723&lvl=notice_display)

### What changed before 1930

- **Ptolemy, second century CE — documented absence:** Reticulum did not exist. Its far-southern stars were not assigned to a Ptolemaic figure. Ptolemy’s catalogue did not furnish an inherited constellation framework over this entire region.
- **Bayer, 1603 — documented absence:** *Uranometria* preceded Habrecht’s Rhombus and Lacaille’s Reticle. Bayer had no Reticulum and therefore supplied no Bayer letters to its stars.
- **Habrecht, 1621 — documented predecessor:** Isaac Habrecht II placed **Rhombus** on a celestial globe. Its four-star pattern is reconstructed as approximately **α and β Reticuli plus γ and ν Hydri**. It was therefore larger and differently tilted than Lacaille’s later Reticle.
- **Hevelius, 1690 — documented absence:** Hevelius did not establish Reticulum as a standard figure. His atlas was predominantly northern and inherited neither a stable Rhombus nor Lacaille’s later constellation.
- **Lacaille, 1756/1763 — documented creation:** Lacaille selected and lettered a new, smaller rhomboidal instrument from stars between Dorado, Hydrus, and Horologium.
- **Bode, 1801 — documented reception:** Bode retained and illustrated Lacaille’s optical instrument as *Reticulus*.
- **Delporte, 1930 — documented legal boundary:** The modern region was fixed between Dorado, Horologium, and Hydrus.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The most precise description is that Lacaille **reworked a region where Habrecht had placed Rhombus**, not that he merely translated *Rhombus* into French. Two of Habrecht’s four principal stars now belong to Hydrus; several of Lacaille’s defining Reticulum stars were not vertices of the earlier Rhombus.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

**Documented fact:** The Greek letters in Reticulum are not Bayer’s work. Lacaille extended Bayer-style lettering to his new southern constellations in the eighteenth century.

The usual lettered set is:

**α, β, γ, δ, ε, ζ¹, ζ², η, θ, ι, κ Reticuli.**

That produces the frequently quoted count of eleven Bayer/Flamsteed-style entries when ζ¹ and ζ² are counted separately.

**Documented absence:** Reticulum has **no ordinary Flamsteed-number sequence**. John Flamsteed’s catalogue was based on stars observable from England and predates Lacaille’s constellation. Claims such as “1 Reticuli” should therefore be checked against catalogue-specific numbering rather than assumed to be Flamsteed designations.

### Proper names

**Documented fact:** No traditional Arabic, Greek, or Latin proper name belongs to Alpha Reticuli or the other principal Reticulum stars. Their normal names are catalogue designations.

**Contested modern usage:** “Rhombus” is sometimes printed as a proper name for Alpha Reticuli. It is historically derived from Habrecht’s constellation, but it is not an ancient star-name and is not securely established as an IAU-approved proper name. Treating “Rhombus” as if it were a traditional name parallel to Sirius or Canopus is misleading.

### Zeta Reticuli

ζ¹ Reticuli and ζ² Reticuli are a wide common-motion pair of Sun-like stars about 39 light-years away. They are separated widely enough to be distinct to the unaided eye under excellent southern skies. “Zeta Reticuli” can refer collectively to the pair, while the superscripts distinguish the components.

**Documented fact:** Their designation originated in Lacaille’s southern lettering, not in Bayer’s 1603 atlas.

**Scientific caution:** The pair’s common motion and broadly similar composition support a shared origin, but popular descriptions that call them a close “binary” can imply a compact orbit that is not observed. “Wide physical pair” is safer.

### Epsilon Reticuli

Epsilon Reticuli is a nearby K subgiant accompanied by a white dwarf; the primary also has a confirmed giant exoplanet. It is an instructive example of why a constellation is only a sight-line region: its components and Alpha, Gamma, and Delta Reticuli have no physical relation to one another.

### Variable stars

- **R Reticuli — documented fact:** Mira variable, approximately magnitude 6.3–14, with a roughly ten-month period. Historical cataloguing also produced the duplicate designation **S Reticuli**; the duplication is a nomenclatural error, not a second star.
- **γ Reticuli — documented fact:** semiregular or low-amplitude variable red giant.
- **δ Reticuli — documented fact:** irregular variable red giant.
- **θ Reticuli — catalogue caution:** has been listed as suspected variable; classifications should be taken from the current variable-star catalogue rather than older popular tables.
- **TV Reticuli — documented controversy:** originally treated after a 1977 outburst as a cataclysmic variable. Imaging and spectroscopy later showed an extended emission-line galaxy; the event was proposed as an ultraluminous supernova. It is a valuable example of a transient misclassified as a Galactic star.  
[TV Reticuli study](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0611141)

### Nova Reticuli 2020

**Documented fact:** **YZ Reticuli**, also **Nova Reticuli 2020**, erupted in July 2020 and became visible to the unaided eye from dark southern locations. It is a classical nova: a thermonuclear eruption on an accreting white dwarf, not the destruction of the star.

Later observations found rapidly evolving line profiles and interpreted them as precessing jets.  
[SIMBAD YZ Reticuli record](https://simbad.cds.unistra.fr/simbad/sim-id?Ident=YZ+Reticuli); [jet study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12946)

### Deep-sky objects

Reticulum has **no Messier object**. Important NGC and named objects include:

#### NGC 1313

A nearby barred, irregular-looking spiral/starburst galaxy, sometimes called the **Topsy Turvy Galaxy**. It contains strong star formation and ultraluminous X-ray sources.

- **SN 1978K:** a long-lived interacting supernova, identified as a supernova through its strong radio/X-ray emission; it remains unusually luminous decades later.
- **NGC 1313 X-1 and X-2:** ultraluminous X-ray sources; X-2 has been investigated as an accreting compact object.

[NASA technical discussion of SN 1978K](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19950033374)

#### NGC 1559

A barred spiral and prolific supernova host. Recorded events include:

- **SN 1984J**
- **SN 1986L**
- **SN 2005df**, Type Ia
- **SN 2009ib**, Type II-P

Australian amateur astronomer Robert Evans discovered several of these, including SN 2005df and the two earlier explosions.  
[ESO announcement](https://www.eso.org/public/australia/news/eso0527/)

#### Other bright catalogue galaxies

- **NGC 1543:** barred lenticular galaxy with a prominent ring.
- **NGC 1574:** lenticular galaxy.
- **NGC 1534, NGC 1536, NGC 1490, NGC 1463:** fainter galaxies in the official region.
- Numerous IC galaxies also fall inside Delporte’s boundary. A current compilation lists 42 NGC/IC objects, but “complete” depends on catalogue inclusion and boundary algorithms.  
[NGC/IC compilation](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/reticulum-deepsky-objects)

### Objects carrying the Reticulum name

#### The Reticulum globular cluster

**Documented fact:** A remote globular cluster associated with the Large Magellanic Cloud, discovered by **José Luis Sérsic in 1974**. It lies in the direction of Reticulum and was named accordingly. Time-series studies have identified 32 RR Lyrae variables—22 RRab, four RRc, and six RRd—in one surveyed field.  
[Discovery table](https://spider.seds.org/spider/MWGC/Add/gc_dis.html); [variable-star study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1307.6644)

#### Reticulum II

**Documented fact:** **Reticulum II (DES J0335.6−5403)** is an ultra-faint Milky Way satellite identified in Dark Energy Survey data in 2015 and spectroscopically confirmed as a dwarf galaxy.

It became especially important because most of its brightest member stars show exceptionally strong rapid-neutron-capture, or **r-process**, enrichment. This is evidence that a rare enrichment event—now commonly discussed in connection with neutron-star mergers—polluted its early gas.  
[Reticulum II spectroscopy](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.07916); [r-process paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27001693/)

**Disputed scientific claim:** One 2015 analysis reported a 2–10 GeV gamma-ray excess possibly compatible with dark-matter annihilation. The significance depended strongly on background modelling: about 3.7σ under one model but 2.3σ under a more model-independent treatment. Another extended-source analysis found no significant signal. Reticulum II has therefore **not provided a confirmed detection of dark matter**.  
[Initial claim](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26340176/); [non-detection analysis](https://cpc.ihep.ac.cn/article/doi/10.1088/1674-1137/42/2/025102)

#### Reticulum III

A much fainter stellar-system candidate discovered in modern southern surveys. Its classification and membership have been less secure than Reticulum II’s. It should not be confused with the 1974 Reticulum globular cluster.

### Meteor showers

**Documented negative finding:** No established major meteor shower bears the name Reticulids, and standard popular catalogues report no shower conventionally associated with Reticulum. Very weak or provisional radiants can appear in large meteor databases, so the strict claim should be “no recognized major Reticulum shower,” not “no meteor radiant has ever been measured there.”  
[IAU-linked radiant resources](https://globalmeteornetwork.org/data/)

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity to 150 CE

**Documented absence:** Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, and Roman constellation systems contain no Reticulum. The stars are too far south to have become an inherited Mediterranean figure comparable with Orion, Argo, or Centaurus.

No Reticulum occurs in:

- the Babylonian *MUL.APIN* tradition;
- Egyptian decanal lists;
- Aratus’ *Phaenomena*;
- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hipparchus’ surviving commentary;
- Ptolemy’s *Almagest* star catalogue;
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Germanicus’ Aratea;
- Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, or Pausanias.

Consequently, there is no ancient catasterism, no competing divine identity, and no ancient agent said to have placed it in heaven.

### Islamic Middle Ages, tenth century

**Documented absence:** Al-Sufi’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* revises the Ptolemaic 48 and depicts Ptolemaic constellations in paired terrestrial and celestial views. Reticulum’s stars were not an al-Sufi constellation and therefore have no al-Sufi figure-name or inherited individual Arabic names.

### European Middle Ages

**Documented absence:** No Reticulum appears in medieval Latin Aratea manuscripts, Alfonsine constellation lists, Hebrew adaptations of the Ptolemaic sky, or conventional astrological imagery.

### 1515: Dürer

**Documented absence:** Albrecht Dürer’s two celestial woodcuts show the traditional Ptolemaic constellations. Reticulum had not been invented and is not depicted.

### 1595–1603: Dutch southern surveys

Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederik de Houtman observed southern stars during Dutch voyages; Petrus Plancius and globe-makers incorporated the resulting twelve new southern figures. Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria* canonized those twelve.

**Documented fact:** Reticulum was not one of them. It should not be credited to Keyser, de Houtman, Plancius, or Bayer.

### 1621: Habrecht’s Rhombus

**Documented fact:** Isaac Habrecht II’s Strasbourg celestial globe is the earliest securely surviving representation of **Rhombus** in this area. The engraved globe was produced with Jakob van Heyden and based broadly on Plancius/van den Keere cartography, but Rhombus is the distinctive addition for which Habrecht is usually credited.  
[Christie’s globe description](https://www.christies.com/lot/lot-3998834); [Royal Museums Greenwich comparison](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-19864)

**Disputed attribution:** Some later writers speculate that Plancius may already have devised Rhombus on a lost globe. The surviving evidence does not establish this. The first currently documented object is Habrecht’s 1621 globe.

### 1624–1628: Bartsch and Habrecht’s planisphere

Jacob Bartsch is sometimes credited with an independent Rhombus around 1624. This attribution is less secure than Habrecht’s dated globe. Habrecht’s *Planiglobium coeleste et terrestre* of 1628 perpetuated the figure.

### 1627: Julius Schiller

Schiller’s *Coelum stellatum Christianum* was published at Augsburg with Bayer’s assistance and engravings associated with Johann Matthias Kager and Lucas Kilian. It replaced classical figures with Christian ones.

**Documented limitation:** Reticulum did not yet exist. Schiller therefore did **not** assign it a saint or object. Claims that “Reticulum became [specific biblical figure] in Schiller” are anachronistic. Rhombus may appear only as a minor modern geometrical group in the source star field, not as a Christian counterpart to Lacaille’s later constellation.  
[Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/)

### 1751–1752: Cape observations

Lacaille established a small observatory at the Cape of Good Hope and surveyed the southern sky. His main observing programme ran from August 1751 to July 1752.

**Documented fact:** His constellation honored the **rhomboidal reticle used in positional observation**. It was one of his family of instruments and scientific tools: telescope, microscope, clock, compass, air pump, furnace, engraver’s tools, and others.

**Scholarly interpretation:** Lacaille’s instrumental sky celebrated Enlightenment observational practice collectively rather than a single patron or monarch. Reticulum commemorates the means by which the catalogue was made.

### 1756: first publication

The Académie memoir printed the *Planisphère contenant les Constellations Célestes comprises entre le Pôle Austral et le Tropique du Capricorne*. The new figure appears as *le Réticule rhomboïde*.

This is the safest date for its public invention.

### 1763: posthumous Latin catalogue

Lacaille died in 1762. Jean-Dominique Maraldi edited the posthumous *Coelum australe stelliferum*, which supplied the Latinized instrumental constellation and systematic star catalogue.  
[1763 edition](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/152572)

### 1776 and 1795: Fortin’s Flamsteed atlas

Jean-Nicolas Fortin’s French editions of Flamsteed’s atlas added a southern planisphere based on Lacaille. This disseminated Reticulum in a prestigious “Flamsteed” atlas, although Flamsteed himself neither invented nor observed it as such.

### 1782: Bode’s popular atlas

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* helped popularize Lacaille’s southern figures in German celestial cartography. Reticulum belonged to the established instrumental set before the monumental 1801 edition.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact:** Bode engraved *RETICULUS* on the far-southern plate. The figure is a rhombus-shaped measuring grid, with crossed or converging lines and differently shaded sectors.

Bode’s atlas contains many more plotted stars than pictorial star atlases intended only for naked-eye use. The pictorial figure and its named group must therefore be distinguished from every telescopic point plotted inside or around it.

### 1810: *Marmor Sculptile*

**Documented fact:** American mapmaker William Croswell published *A Mercator Map of the Starry Heavens*, the first published American star map, and introduced **Marmor Sculptile**, a sculptured marble bust of Christopher Columbus, over the Reticulum region. It did not achieve general use.  
[Library of Congress map](https://loc.getarchive.net/media/a-mercator-map-of-the-starry-heavens-comprehending-the-whole-equinoctial-and-12); [Smithsonian history](https://amhistory.si.edu/documentsgallery/exhibitions/dewitt_6.html)

### 1822: Jamieson’s *Solarium*

**Documented fact:** Alexander Jamieson replaced Reticulum on plate 28 of *A Celestial Atlas* with **Solarium**, a sundial. This was not a mere translated title but a rival constellation figure. It failed to displace Reticulum.  
[Jamieson atlas overview](https://meteo-maarssen.nl/lib_jamieson_1822.html)

### 1824–1825: *Urania’s Mirror*

*Urania’s Mirror* drew heavily on Jamieson’s atlas but concentrated on constellations usable by its intended British audience.

**Negative finding:** No dependable evidence was found for a separate Reticulum card. Claims that every IAU constellation appears in *Urania’s Mirror* are false; its 32 cards are selective, and the deep southern instrumental constellations receive uneven treatment.  
[Smithsonian set](https://www.si.edu/object/uranias-mirror%3Anmah_1460911); [Morgan Library record](https://www.themorgan.org/printed-books/365041)

### Nineteenth century: scientific atlases

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* and Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* were primarily northern-sky projects and did not independently determine Reticulum’s survival. Southern catalogues and global atlases retained Lacaille’s name because it was useful, compact, and already embedded in star designations.

### 1922–1930: IAU standardization

- **1922, Rome:** Reticulum included in the official list; abbreviation **Ret**.
- **1925:** Delporte presented a general boundary scheme.
- **1928, Leiden:** boundaries approved.
- **1930:** *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* published.

The IAU did not “choose Reticulum over Rhombus” in a fresh two-candidate vote documented in the surviving resolution. Rather, it standardized the constellation system then in astronomical use; Habrecht’s Rhombus, Croswell’s Marmor Sculptile, and Jamieson’s Solarium had not maintained independent currency.

### 1961–1974: the Hill map and Zeta Reticuli

See the dedicated legend section below.

### 1974: Reticulum cluster

José Luis Sérsic discovered the remote LMC globular cluster now called Reticulum.

### 2015: Reticulum II

Dark Energy Survey data revealed Reticulum II; spectroscopy established it as an ultra-faint dwarf galaxy and subsequent work made it central to r-process nucleosynthesis studies.

### 2020: Nova Reticuli

YZ Reticuli erupted as a naked-eye classical nova.

## Invention, purpose, and reception

### What exactly did the reticle look like?

Lacaille’s description does not mean the now-universal simple “+” crosshair. It was a **rhomboidal line system within a square**, suited to measuring transits or relative positions. His words expressly link it to the instrument used in constructing the catalogue.

**Documented fact:** The constellation honored one of the smallest components in the observing system—the fine grid in an eyepiece—rather than the entire telescope.

**Unverified embellishment:** Popular accounts sometimes say it was “the exact reticle of Lacaille’s half-inch telescope.” The exact telescope, aperture, eyepiece assembly, and whether the engraved shape reproduces one unique physical survivor require evidence from Lacaille’s instrument or notebook. The primary printed explanation supports a functional rhomboidal reticle; it does not by itself prove every modern reconstruction of the hardware.

### Was Lacaille reviving Habrecht?

- **Fact:** Habrecht’s Rhombus predates Lacaille.
- **Fact:** it overlaps the later Reticulum region.
- **Fact:** the figures are not star-for-star identical.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Lacaille likely knew, or at least worked in a cartographic environment that knew, the Rhombus tradition.
- **Open question:** no located statement by Lacaille says, “I have renamed Habrecht’s Rhombus.” Calling Reticulum a deliberate “revival” is consequently stronger than the direct evidence permits.

### Why it survived

Reticulum’s survival can be explained without a formal nineteenth-century campaign:

1. Lacaille provided systematic coordinates and Greek-letter designations.
2. Fortin and Bode disseminated it.
3. Astronomical catalogues used *Reticuli* in star nomenclature.
4. Rival Marmor Sculptile and Solarium had little adoption.
5. The IAU generally retained established scientific usage when creating its 88-region system.

## Myths and legends by culture

## Classical Greek and Roman

**Documented absence:** There are no classical myths of Reticulum. It is not a transformed person, animal, weapon, or divine object. Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Aratus, Germanicus, Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, and Pausanias do not mention it.

Accordingly:

- no god placed it in the sky;
- it has no catasterism;
- it has no variant identity in the *Catasterismi* Epitome;
- it performs no action in another classical myth;
- it has no Ptolemaic astrological nature.

Any story calling it, for example, “the net of Hephaestus,” “the net of Aphrodite,” or “the fisherman’s net” is a **modern analogical invention**, not recovered classical lore.

## Babylonian and Egyptian

**Documented absence:** No Babylonian or Egyptian constellation can securely be identified as an antecedent of Lacaille’s Reticulum. Its stars lie well outside the inherited zodiacal and decanal identifications normally proposed for Mediterranean antiquity.

## Arabic and Bedouin

**Documented absence:** Al-Sufi supplies no Reticulum name. The individual principal stars possess no widely accepted medieval Arabic proper names.

**Geographical explanation:** At declinations near −53° to −67°, the pattern is extremely low or invisible from much of the Arabic-speaking northern world. That makes an inherited Bedouin asterism in precisely these stars improbable.

No dependable Bedouin asterism corresponding to the four-star Reticulum diamond was located. Modern Arabic names for the constellation are translations introduced through international astronomy.

## Persian

**Documented absence:** No premodern Persian constellation corresponding to Reticulum was located. Persian astronomical texts drawing on Ptolemy and al-Sufi lacked it; modern Persian terminology is translated or transliterated from the IAU constellation.

## Hebrew and Kabbalistic

**Documented absence:** No ancient or medieval Hebrew constellation-name is securely attached to these stars. Reticulum has no established biblical tribal, angelic, sefirotic, or Kabbalistic mapping.

Claims that the constellation encodes a particular sefirah, “the net of divine creation,” or another Kabbalistic symbol are **modern esoteric readings** unless accompanied by a dated premodern manuscript—which the search did not produce.

## UFO legend: Betty and Barney Hill

### The 1961 event

**Documented historical fact:** Betty and Barney Hill reported a strange-light/UFO encounter during a drive through New Hampshire on the night of 19–20 September 1961. Later narratives included a period of missing time and, under hypnosis in 1964, alleged abduction experiences.

**Legendary claim:** Betty said she had been shown a star map aboard an extraterrestrial craft.

The distinction in dates matters: the famous map is not a contemporaneous astronomical drawing made during the 1961 drive. It belongs to the later hypnosis-era evidentiary record. The surviving archive describes a **1964 star map**.  
[Rice archival record](https://archives.library.rice.edu/repositories/2/archival_objects/363024)

### Publication and Marjorie Fish

John G. Fuller’s *The Interrupted Journey* (1966) publicized the case and reproduced the diagram. Ohio schoolteacher and amateur astronomer **Marjorie Fish** constructed three-dimensional models of nearby stars and searched for a viewpoint from which selected stars resembled the Hill drawing.

By the early 1970s Fish proposed that the two principal circles were **ζ¹ and ζ² Reticuli**, with the Sun and nearby stars forming alleged trade and exploration routes.

Terence Dickinson’s article “The Zeta Reticuli Incident,” published in *Astronomy* in December **1974**, gave the proposal its broadest astronomical publicity. The magazine’s editorial framing explicitly said that the resemblance was not proof of extraterrestrial visitation.  
[Transcription of the 1974 discussion](https://www.nicap.org/articles/hillzeta2.htm)

### Criticism

**Disputed claim:** The Hill drawing uniquely or statistically identifies Zeta Reticuli.

Carl Sagan and Steven Soter objected that:

- Fish selected a subset from a larger star catalogue;
- she could rotate a three-dimensional model through many viewpoints;
- one star’s occultation was introduced ad hoc;
- some background dots from the original drawing were selected while others were omitted;
- the resulting degrees of freedom made chance resemblance much more likely than advocates claimed.

Their information-theory rebuttal estimated that the freedom to choose a viewpoint had been seriously understated.

**Evidence assessment:** The drawing contains too little unique metric information—no coordinate grid, distances, scale, orientation, or independently specified viewpoint—to identify a stellar system reliably. Changes in nearby-star catalogues further undermine claims of an exact predictive match.

### Later spread

**Modern legend:** “The Greys come from Zeta Reticuli” became a standard UFO-cultural assertion through later books, television, alleged whistle-blowers, the Majestic-12 mythology, Richard Doty’s “Falcon” persona, Bob Lazar narratives, and fiction influenced by *Alien*.

This later claim is not something the Hills themselves established astronomically. It descends from Fish’s interpretation of Betty’s later drawing.

### Common popular errors

- **False:** Betty drew an accurate map of stars unknown in 1961.  
  The stars in Fish’s model were drawn from published nearby-star catalogues.
- **False:** astronomers confirmed the map.  
  Some expressed interest; others published detailed criticism. No astronomical body validated it.
- **Misleading:** the map was made in 1961 immediately after the encounter.  
  The archival drawing is associated with the 1964 hypnosis period.
- **Unsupported:** confirmed habitable planets orbit ζ¹ or ζ² Reticuli.  
  Debris-disk claims and exoplanet searches are not confirmation of inhabited worlds.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Chinese astronomy

**Documented fact:** The stars were too far south for the ancient Three Enclosures and Twenty-Eight Mansions system. They entered Chinese uranography only after Jesuit transmission of southern star charts in the late Ming/early Qing period.

Modern reconstructions assign stars in the region to southern asterisms including:

- **蛇首, Shéshǒu — “Snake’s Head”**
- **夾白 / 夹白, Jiābái — “White Patches Attached”**

These are part of the later **Near-Southern-Polar** star region, not early indigenous mansion astronomy.  
[Reticulum in Chinese astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reticulum_in_Chinese_astronomy)

## Indian astronomy

**Documented absence:** Reticulum lies outside the nakshatra belt and has no classical Sanskrit constellation equivalent. No traditional lunar mansion crosses it. Modern Indian astronomy uses the international Latin name or a translated technical expression.

## Lunar mansions

Neither the Babylonian/Arabic *manāzil al-qamar*, Indian nakshatras, nor Chinese lunar lodges cross Reticulum, because it is far from the ecliptic.

## Schiller’s Christian sky

**Documented negative finding:** Reticulum was invented approximately 129 years after Schiller’s 1627 atlas. It therefore had no designated saint or sacred object in *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*.

## Astrology

**Documented absence:** Reticulum has:

- no entry in Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*;
- no traditional planetary “nature”;
- no Behenian fixed star;
- no zodiacal degree;
- no lunar mansion.

Modern astrologers may assign symbolism such as precision, measurement, entanglement, or networks. These are **modern inventions**, not classical doctrine.

## Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No dated early-modern alchemical emblem, Hermetic text, or Paracelsian celestial scheme securely linking Reticulum to a substance, operation, or divine net was located. The instrument lends itself easily to later symbolism—measurement, intersection, hidden order—but such interpretations must be identified as modern esotericism.

## Iconography

### Objects and atlases in chronological order

- **Farnese Atlas, Roman copy of a Hellenistic prototype:** no Reticulum; its globe carries the inherited classical constellations.
- **Al-Sufi manuscripts, from the tenth century onward:** no Reticulum.
- **Dürer celestial maps, Nuremberg, 1515:** no Reticulum.
- **Bayer, Augsburg, 1603:** no Reticulum.
- **Habrecht globe, Strasbourg, 1621:** **Rhombus**, apparently a simple geometric four-star diamond; the earliest surviving related image.
- **Schiller, Augsburg, 1627:** no Lacaille Reticulum and therefore no Christian substitution.
- **Lacaille planisphere, Paris, 1756:** the first published **Réticule rhomboïde**, shown schematically as an instrument.
- **Lacaille/Maraldi, Paris, 1763:** Latinized southern chart and catalogue.
- **Fortin’s French Flamsteed atlas, Paris, 1776 and 1795:** Lacaille’s southern planisphere transmitted into the Flamsteed atlas tradition.
- **Bode, Berlin, 1782:** Reticulum in the popular *Vorstellung der Gestirne*.
- **Bode, Berlin, 1801, plate XX:** *RETICULUS*, a substantial engraved rhomboidal grid amid Dorado, Hydrus, Horologium, and neighbouring southern instruments.
- **Croswell, Boston, 1810:** Columbus’ bust, *Marmor Sculptile*, superimposed on the region.
- **Jamieson, London, 1822, plate 28:** Reticulum replaced by the sundial **Solarium**.
- **Urania’s Mirror, London, 1824/25:** no securely identified separate Reticulum card; the card set should not be treated as a complete global atlas.
- **Modern IAU charts:** no mandatory picture, only star positions and official boundaries.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

**Negative finding:** No ancient coin, medieval cathedral portal, zodiac calendar, or Renaissance ceiling can depict Reticulum under that identity because the figure did not exist before the seventeenth/eighteenth century.

Reticle-like grids in older art are not evidence of this constellation unless a caption, chart correspondence, or contemporary description identifies the stars.

## People, events, and works

### Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille

Reticulum is unusually autobiographical: it commemorates the observing apparatus with which Lacaille measured the southern sky. The Cape survey produced one of the foundational catalogues of southern stars.

### Isaac Habrecht II

Habrecht’s Rhombus is the principal historical predecessor. His 1621 globe also plotted comets and recent novae and reused much Plancian cartography, making the attribution problem more complicated than a wholly independent atlas.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode transformed Lacaille’s modest planisphere symbol into one element of the most elaborate early nineteenth-century pictorial atlas. His accompanying catalogue integrated the figure into a global scientific star inventory.

### William Croswell and Columbus

Croswell’s *Marmor Sculptile* linked the region to Christopher Columbus through a sculptured bust. This was patriotic and commemorative celestial invention, not a continuation of Lacaille’s instrument.

### Alexander Jamieson

Jamieson’s **Solarium** demonstrates that Reticulum was not inevitable. The same stars could be recast as another precision instrument, a sundial. Astronomers nevertheless retained Lacaille’s older catalogue name.

### Robert Evans

Evans’ visual searches for extragalactic supernovae made NGC 1559 one of Reticulum’s best-known modern objects. His discoveries demonstrate the continuing scientific use of the constellation as an address even when its pictorial meaning is irrelevant.

### Science fiction and popular culture

Zeta Reticuli’s UFO reputation has fed numerous novels, games, films, and television programmes. The *Alien* franchise placed LV-426 in the ζ² Reticuli system in ancillary fictional geography, helping turn an obscure naked-eye pair into a familiar science-fiction destination.

These works inherit Fish-era UFO folklore; they are not astronomical evidence.

### Navigation and exploration

**Negative finding:** No well-documented historic voyage was found that depended specifically on Alpha Reticuli or the Reticulum diamond as a named navigational marker. Its stars are comparatively faint, and southern navigation more commonly used Canopus, Achernar, Alpha Centauri, the Southern Cross, and Magellanic Clouds.

Lacaille reached the Cape by ship, but the constellation commemorates his positional instrument, not a star by which his voyage was demonstrably steered.

### Ships, spacecraft, and missions

No major astronomical spacecraft or historically prominent ship securely named solely for the constellation Reticulum was located. Searches are complicated because *reticulum* is also a general anatomical, botanical, network, and instrument term. Objects named “Reticulum” therefore cannot automatically be attached to the constellation.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Habrecht or Lacaille?

**Resolution:** Habrecht invented **Rhombus**, not the official Reticulum; Lacaille invented the published **Réticule rhomboïde** in its recognizable modern identity. The figures overlap but differ.

### 2. Habrecht or Plancius?

**Disputed:** Habrecht’s globe depends on Plancius/van den Keere traditions, and a lost Plancius source is conceivable. But the first surviving dated Rhombus is Habrecht’s 1621 globe. Assigning it definitively to Plancius is unsupported by the surviving object record.

### 3. “Created in 1752” or 1756?

- Observed/designed during the Cape programme: **1751–52**.
- Memoir associated with the Académie’s year 1752: yes.
- Actually printed and publicly available: **1756**.
- Full Latin catalogue: **1763**.

These dates describe different stages; presenting any one without qualification produces apparent contradictions.

### 4. Reticulus or Reticulum?

Lacaille and Bode used **Reticulus** in some labels, while official modern usage is **Reticulum**. Both derive from Latin “little net,” but the IAU nominative is unequivocally **Reticulum**, genitive **Reticuli**.

### 5. “The Net” or “the Reticle”?

“The Net” is a literal but incomplete translation. Lacaille’s explanation identifies an optical measuring grid. **The Reticle** is therefore the historically precise English gloss.

### 6. Is “Rhombus” Alpha Reticuli’s proper name?

**Contested modern nomenclature:** Some databases and popular guides list it. Historically, Rhombus named a constellation, not demonstrably that individual star. It should not be described as an ancient Arabic or classical proper name.

### 7. Did Bayer name its stars?

No. “Bayer designation” describes the letter-plus-genitive system; Lacaille assigned Reticulum’s Greek letters long after Bayer’s death.

### 8. Did Flamsteed catalogue Reticulum?

Not as a constellation with numbered Reticuli stars. Later French editions of the *Atlas Coelestis* added Lacaille’s southern map, which can misleadingly be attributed to Flamsteed personally.

### 9. Did Schiller Christianize it?

No. Schiller died and his atlas appeared in 1627, long before Lacaille’s Reticulum. A claimed saintly counterpart is anachronistic.

### 10. Is Bode’s engraving an exact instrument portrait?

Possibly approximate, but not proven. Differences in shading between Lacaille and Bode may be artistic misunderstanding or reinterpretation. No direct statement by Bode explaining the discrepancy was located.

### 11. Did the IAU abolish it in 1930?

No. This is the opposite of the documented record. Reticulum was retained; Rhombus, Marmor Sculptile, and Solarium were not recognized as independent constellations.

### 12. Did Betty Hill identify Zeta Reticuli?

**Legend/disputed:** Betty drew an ambiguous diagram; Marjorie Fish later proposed the Zeta interpretation. Astronomers did not confirm a unique match, and published criticism identifies large selection and projection freedoms.

### 13. Was the Hill map ahead of astronomy?

No evidence supports this. Fish used contemporary catalogues of nearby stars. Later astrometry has not converted the resemblance into a unique or predictive identification.

### 14. Does Reticulum II prove dark-matter annihilation?

No. A reported gamma-ray excess was model-dependent and was not confirmed by other analyses. Reticulum II is, however, a securely important dark-matter-dominated dwarf galaxy and r-process laboratory.

### 15. Reticulum cluster versus Reticulum II

They are different objects:

- **Reticulum cluster:** old globular cluster associated with the LMC, discovered 1974.
- **Reticulum II:** ultra-faint Milky Way satellite dwarf galaxy, discovered 2015.
- **Reticulum III:** still fainter modern satellite candidate/system.

### 16. “Nova Reticula 2020”

This is a grammatical/popular misprint. The correct astronomical designation is **Nova Reticuli 2020**, using the Latin genitive, or **YZ Reticuli**.

## Open questions

1. **Lacaille’s knowledge of Habrecht:** Did he directly consult Habrecht’s globe or a derivative chart, or independently choose a rhombus because of his reticle’s geometry? No explicit declaration by Lacaille was found.

2. **Bartsch’s role:** Later summaries sometimes credit Jacob Bartsch with a Rhombus in 1624. The precise surviving edition, plate, and relationship to Habrecht deserve object-level comparison.

3. **Bode’s instrument shading:** Was the apparent reversal or extra shaded sector an engraver’s error, a different reticle design, or damage/variation between impressions?

4. **Exact star membership on historical plates:** A definitive comparison should geometrically register high-resolution scans of Habrecht 1621, Lacaille 1756, Lacaille 1763, Bode 1782, and Bode 1801 against precessed modern coordinates.

5. **Non-Western southern knowledge:** The absence of Babylonian, Mediterranean, Arabic, Persian, and northern Chinese antecedents does not imply that southern African, Aboriginal Australian, or Pacific peoples lacked traditions in these stars. No source sufficiently precise to identify the modern Reticulum quadrilateral was located in this survey; a responsible attribution requires community-specific ethnographic evidence.

6. **The “830 stars in 109 groups” description:** This appears to concern a particular Bode collection, exhibition, linked-data “chain,” or indexing project rather than the published structure of *Uranographia*, whose companion catalogue contains 17,240 entries. No independent bibliographical source confirming “830 stars in 109 groups” as Bode’s own characterization of Reticulum or of the atlas was located.

7. **Meteor radiants:** Current databases may include weak provisional showers whose drifting radiants temporarily enter Reticulum. None was found that justifies a traditional “Reticulids” entry.
