# Dorado: Research Dossier

> **Status — documented fact:** **Dorado is retained by the International Astronomical Union as one of the 88 official constellations.** Its official abbreviation is **Dor** and its genitive is **Doradus**. Its present territory is the area enclosed by Eugène Delporte’s boundaries, approved by the IAU in 1928 and published in 1930. It was not abolished, and its stars therefore did not have to be reassigned wholesale to another constellation.  
> **Modern identification:** the intended animal was most probably the oceanic dolphinfish or mahi-mahi, *Coryphaena hippurus*, called *dorado* in Spanish—not the freshwater goldfish and not, originally, the swordfish *Xiphias gladius*. “Xiphias” was nevertheless a genuine and influential seventeenth- and eighteenth-century alternate constellation name, and Bode used it in 1801.

## Method and evidentiary labels

The dossier uses these labels:

- **Documented fact:** directly supported by a dated primary source, official record, surviving chart, catalogue, or modern scientific literature.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a conclusion argued from several historical or astronomical sources but not stated explicitly in the earliest record.
- **Tradition:** a culturally transmitted interpretation documented by an ethnographer, manuscript, atlas, or historical compilation.
- **Disputed:** sources or scholars disagree, or the evidence admits materially different readings.
- **Unverified claim:** repeated in secondary literature but not traced to adequate evidence.
- **Legend/myth:** a narrative presented as traditional sacred, heroic, or cosmological history rather than empirical history.
- **Modern invention:** a recent symbolic association, back-formation, or popular story not present in the founding documents.

The figure-specific references to “Bode collection,” “on chain,” and “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be connected to identifiable wording in Bode’s *Uranographia* or its companion catalogue. They may describe a museum object or collection record supplied separately from the book. They should not be treated as statements by Bode without the relevant accession record.

The IAU describes constellations today as bounded regions rather than merely pictures made from selected stars. It lists **Dorado / Dor / Doradus** among the retained constellations. The official boundary system is based on right ascension and declination for equinox B1875.0; modern plotted coordinates are precessed into current coordinate systems. [IAU constellation table and historical explanation](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [IAU Style Manual](https://iauarchive.eso.org/static/publications/stylemanual1989.pdf).

### Name and etymology

**Dorado — documented linguistic fact.** Spanish *dorado* means “golden” or “gilded,” the masculine past participle of *dorar*, from Late Latin *deaurare*, “to gild,” ultimately from *aurum*, “gold.” As a noun it was applied to brilliantly colored tropical fishes. The constellation name is therefore Spanish or Iberian vernacular rather than classical Latin. [American Heritage Dictionary](https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=dorado); [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/dorado).

**The fish — scholarly reconstruction with strong zoological support.** The best contextual identification is the common dolphinfish or mahi-mahi, *Coryphaena hippurus*. A living dolphinfish flashes metallic green, blue, and gold; “dorado” was an apt sailors’ name. It is a powerful open-ocean predator and commonly pursues flying fish. The nearby constellation Volans was originally *Piscis Volans*, a flying fish. The astronomical pairing consequently makes ecological and pictorial sense.

**“Goldfish” — misleading literal translation.** English and German writers sometimes rendered *dorado* as “golden fish” or “Goldfisch.” That does not establish that the intended animal was the small Eurasian ornamental carp now called a goldfish. Sixteenth-century *dorado* denoted exotic, conspicuously golden fishes of warm seas.

**Xiphias — documented alternate name.** Greek ξιφίας, Latinized *Xiphias*, means a swordfish, from ξίφος, “sword.” Johannes Kepler’s 1627 edition of the *Rudolphine Tables* appears to be the earliest traced astronomical use of **Xiphias** for Dorado. Halley used both names in 1679; later catalogues and atlases perpetuated it. The alternate name is therefore historical, not a modern hoax, but it changes the zoological species.

**Dorado versus Doradus — documented convention, grammatically artificial.** The IAU kept the non-Latin nominative *Dorado* but standardized the stellar genitive **Doradus**. Historical explanations compare it with Greek feminine proper names in *-ō*, whose Latin genitive can end in *-ūs*. It is best understood as an astronomers’ conventional Latinization rather than natural Spanish grammar.

### Names in requested languages

Because Dorado was created in the late sixteenth century from stars invisible or barely accessible to most northern Old World observers, it has no demonstrable Babylonian, pharaonic Egyptian, Greek, Roman, classical Arabic, Sanskrit, or biblical Hebrew constellation name.

- **Dutch, 1603 — documented:** Frederik de Houtman printed **Den Dorado**, “the dorado.”
- **Latin/Greek form, 1627 onward — documented:** **Xiphias**, the swordfish.
- **Latinized modern astronomical form:** **Dorado**, genitive **Doradus**.
- **German — documented:** *Goldfisch* and, more influentially, *Schwertfisch*. Bode’s smaller 1782 atlas labels it **Schwerdt-Fisch**, an older spelling of *Schwertfisch*.
- **French:** *la Dorade*; historically also *l’Espadon* for the swordfish interpretation.
- **Italian:** *Dorado*, *Pesce Dorato*, or *Pesce Spada* in works following Xiphias.
- **Spanish:** *Dorado* or *la Dorada*; *Pez Espada* reflects Xiphias rather than the likeliest original fish.
- **Portuguese:** *Dourado*; *Peixe-Espada* where Xiphias is followed.
- **Modern Chinese:** 劍魚座, *Jiànyú Zuò*, “Swordfish Constellation.” This is a modern reception of the Xiphias interpretation.
- **Jesuit-era Chinese:** 金魚, *Jīnyú*, “Goldfish,” became one of the southern asterisms introduced after European star charts reached China.
- **Arabic:** modern astronomical works transliterate the European name or translate it as كوكبة أبي سيف, “constellation of the swordfish.” No secure premodern Arabic name for Dorado as a unit was found.
- **Persian:** modern translations likewise render the European fish; there is no attested medieval Persian constellation corresponding to Dorado.
- **Hebrew:** modern **דג זהב**, *Dag Zahav*, “golden fish/goldfish,” or forms reflecting swordfish occur in modern nomenclature. No biblical, rabbinic, or medieval Hebrew figure encompassing these stars was located.

### Position and boundaries

Dorado lies approximately between right ascensions 3h50m and 6h35m and declinations −49° to −70°, depending on which corners of Delporte’s polygon are measured. It borders:

- Caelum
- Horologium
- Reticulum
- Hydrus
- Mensa
- Volans
- Pictor

The **south ecliptic pole**, the point 90° south of the ecliptic, lies in northern Dorado near RA 6h and declination about −66.6°. This is not the south celestial pole. Precession makes the celestial pole revolve around the ecliptic pole. A seventeenth-century Latinism, **Polus Doradinalis**, is attributed to Philippus Caesius; it never became standard.

### What changed at the boundaries?

**Ptolemy — documented absence.** Dorado was not one of the 48 constellations catalogued in the *Almagest*. Ptolemy’s catalogue did not encompass this far-southern field as a separate figure. Hence Dorado did not “take” named Ptolemaic stars in the manner that later northern inventions sometimes carved up an ancient constellation.

**Keyser–de Houtman–Plancius — reconstruction.** Their figure occupied previously unfigured space between the older Argo/Eridanus region and the south celestial pole. The twelve new constellations divided the southern blank of European globes into recognizable figures.

**Bayer, 1603.** Bayer fixed Dorado’s pictorial relationship to Piscis Volans. His drawn body, rather than a legal boundary, defined which plotted stars viewers associated with it.

**Hevelius, 1690.** Hevelius retained the southern fish but represented the sky in the traditional globe-view orientation, mirrored relative to an observer looking outward from Earth. His outlines were pictorial and could overlap neighboring figures.

**Lacaille, 1750s.** Lacaille’s observations from the Cape provided much better stellar positions and crowded the region with new scientific-instrument constellations. He introduced **Mensa** around part of the Large Magellanic Cloud and **Pictor**, Caelum, Reticulum and Horologium around Dorado. This narrowed Dorado’s practical cartographic surroundings. Most importantly, the LMC was no longer wholly treated as belonging to Dorado: Mensa acquired its southern portion.

**Bode, 1801.** Bode drew provisional dividing curves or lines but still treated the constellation as a picture. His southern polar plate placed **Xiphias** between *Nubecula Major*, *Mons Mensae*, *Piscis Volans*, *Pluteum Pictoris*, Reticulum and Horologium.

**IAU/Delporte.** Dorado’s name and basic region were retained. Delporte’s rectilinear B1875 boundaries converted the old pictorial territory into an exclusive polygon. This finalized the division of the LMC between **Dorado and Mensa**. The modern borders do not certify that every star once drawn inside Bode’s fish remains “an anatomical part” of the fish; they determine only which catalogue suffix it receives today.

No evidence was found for a dramatic named-star transfer comparable to the division of Argo Navis. The most conspicuous transfer-like result is the allocation of the southern LMC to Mensa.

## Stars and objects

### The foundational figure stars

The exact membership depends on whether one means:

1. de Houtman’s original catalogue entries;
2. the stars drawn in Bayer’s fish;
3. Hevelius’s or Bode’s enlarged figure;
4. Lacaille’s Bayer-letter series; or
5. every object inside the IAU polygon.

Those sets are not identical.

A peer-reviewed reconstruction of the early southern catalogues confirms that de Houtman’s 1603 list, Kepler’s 1627 southern appendix, and Halley’s 1679 catalogue are separate textual witnesses with differing star counts and identifications. De Houtman’s heading is **Den Dorado**. [Verbunt and van Gent, “Early Star Catalogues of the Southern Sky,” *Astronomy & Astrophysics* 530, A93](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2011/06/aa16795-11.pdf).

Lacaille—not Bayer—assigned most of the Greek-letter designations now used in Dorado. His published sequence ran broadly from α through π but omitted ι, ο and ξ; duplicate η and π labels and the disappearance of faint μ from later practical catalogues produced irregularities. Statements that “Bayer lettered all Dorado’s stars in 1603” are false.

The numbered designations frequently called “Flamsteed numbers” in online tables require care. John Flamsteed did not systematically observe the far southern sky. Numbers such as 3, 8, 20, 22, 29, 33 and 40 Doradus derive from later southern-zone cataloguing, especially Benjamin Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina*, not from Flamsteed’s original *Historia Coelestis Britannica*. Calling them Flamsteed numbers without qualification is a recurrent catalogue error.

### Proper names

No bright outline star of Dorado has a securely inherited Greek or Arabic proper name comparable with Sirius, Canopus or Betelgeuse. The familiar labels α Doradus, β Doradus, and so forth are systematic designations, not ancient names.

That absence is historically meaningful:

- The field was below the practical horizon of Babylon, classical Greece, Alexandria, most of Egypt, Persia, northern Arabia, medieval Europe, India’s principal astronomical centers and China’s ancient capitals.
- Al-Sufi could not supply Arabic descriptions of Dorado stars because Dorado did not yet exist and most of the field was inaccessible from Shiraz.
- Modern websites that assign grand Arabic, Egyptian, Hebrew or occult names to α Doradus without manuscript citations are producing modern inventions.

### Variable stars and stellar classes

**β Doradus — documented scientific importance.** A bright classical Cepheid. Its pulsation changes temperature, spectrum, radius and brightness. Cepheids became fundamental extragalactic distance indicators after Henrietta Swan Leavitt’s work on Magellanic Cloud variables.

**γ Doradus — documented.** The prototype of γ Dor variables: usually young F-type dwarfs whose brightness varies through high-order, non-radial gravity-mode pulsations. The class was recognized only in modern stellar astrophysics.

**S Doradus — documented.** Prototype of S Doradus variables, now usually called luminous blue variables. These extraordinarily luminous, unstable hot stars undergo irregular spectroscopic and photometric excursions and occasional giant eruptions. “S Doradus variable” is thus one of the constellation’s major contributions to astronomical vocabulary.

**R Doradus — documented, with a qualification.** Interferometric measurements made it famous as one of the stars with the largest apparent angular diameters seen from Earth. Claims that it is unconditionally “the largest-looking star after the Sun” depend on wavelength, atmospheric layer and whether extended envelopes are included.

**Novae.** Numerous novae have erupted in the LMC portion of Dorado, including recurrent systems. A complete list changes as transient surveys discover and reclassify events. Historically conspicuous examples include Nova LMC 1991 and recurrent nova LMCN 1968-12a. These are not additions to the constellation figure; “in Dorado” is a positional designation.

### Supernova 1987A

**Documented fact.** SN 1987A occurred in the LMC, about 20 arcminutes southwest of the Tarantula Nebula. Ian Shelton saw it at Las Campanas early on 24 February 1987; photographic records constrained its appearance to the preceding hours. It was the nearest naked-eye supernova observed since the invention of the telescope and the first for which neutrinos from a stellar core collapse were detected. Its progenitor was the blue supergiant Sanduleak −69° 202. [ESO’s contemporary report](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso8705/).

Its evolving triple-ring system is not a representation of Dorado’s fish. Popular illustrations sometimes merge the two iconographies, but the rings are circumstellar material illuminated and shocked by the explosion.

### The Large Magellanic Cloud

Most of the LMC lies within modern Dorado; its southern outskirts cross into Mensa. It is a nearby irregular/barred dwarf galaxy and satellite or interacting companion of the Milky Way, approximately 160,000 light-years away. It is visible to the unaided eye as a detached luminous cloud.

Historical names include:

- Vespucci’s “bright Canopi,” if the conventional identification is accepted;
- Pigafetta’s two cloud-like accumulations of small stars;
- *Nubes/Nubeculae Magellanicae*, Magellanic Clouds;
- Lacaille’s *le Grand Nuage* and *le Petit Nuage*;
- Bode’s **Nubecula Major**;
- many independent Indigenous names discussed below.

The Clouds were not named by Magellan himself. The eponym became conventional because accounts of his expedition disseminated them in Europe. A modern historical review traces that naming process. [Dennefeld, “A History of the Magellanic Clouds and the European Exploration of the Southern Hemisphere”](https://doi.eso.org/10.18727/0722-6691/5210).

### Meteor showers

No established major annual meteor shower bears Dorado’s name in standard general astronomical references. Weak candidate radiants or video-survey streams may occasionally receive working names derived from nearby stars, but these should not be elevated to the same status as the Perseids or Leonids. The commonly repeated summary “meteor showers: none” is accurate for recognized major showers.

### Asterisms

The fish outline itself is the principal Western asterism. Modern line drawings vary substantially: some join α–β–δ–γ, while others extend through ζ, θ and η. The IAU does not standardize constellation stick figures.

The most important paired composition is:

- **Dorado**, the large predatory fish;
- **Volans**, the flying fish immediately south/east of it.

The apparent chase is present pictorially by Bayer’s 1603 atlas and later maps. Whether the Dutch observers explicitly narrated a chase is another matter: no surviving Keyser or de Houtman prose myth stating “Dorado chased Volans” was found. It is a convincing reconstruction from the figures’ orientation and the real feeding behavior of mahi-mahi, promoted in modern constellation histories, not a surviving voyage tale.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1500: the sky without Dorado

**Before written history — documented visibility, culturally under-recorded.** Southern peoples could see Dorado’s stars and the LMC throughout prehistory. Visibility is an astronomical fact; particular names or stories must be demonstrated culture by culture.

**Babylonian and Egyptian astronomy — absence of evidence.** No recognized cuneiform constellation, decan, Egyptian coffin-clock figure or monumental zodiac securely matches Dorado. The region lies too far south for useful observation from Mesopotamia and northern Egypt. Claims of an Egyptian “golden fish” here are modern projection.

**Greek and Roman astronomy — documented absence.** Dorado is absent from Eudoxus, Aratus, Hipparchus as preserved, Ptolemy’s *Almagest*, Eratosthenes’ *Catasterisms*, Hyginus, Manilius and the classical scholia. Classical *xiphias* references denote swordfish or sword-shaped phenomena, not this constellation.

Consequently, there is no Dorado catasterism in:

- Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* or its *Epitome*;
- Hyginus, *De Astronomia/Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus or the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus’ *Aratea* and scholia;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias.

This is not an omission in the sources: the constellation had not been invented.

**c. 850 — Ibn Qutaybah, tradition/hearsay.** In material on the *anwāʾ*, Ibn Qutaybah reports stars below Canopus called by inhabitants of Tihama a name transcribed as *al-aʿbār*. Their exact identification remains disputed.

**c. 964 — al-Sufi, documented hearsay rather than observation.** In the Argo Navis chapter of the *Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars*, al-Sufi reports claims about the “feet of Canopus” and bright white stars farther south, called *al-Baqar*, “the oxen/cattle,” by people of Tihama. He says they are not visible in Iraq or Najd and that he does not know whether the report is true. He neither creates Dorado nor draws its stars.

### European reports of the Clouds

**1502 — Amerigo Vespucci, probable first secure European record.** A letter attributed to Vespucci describes “three Canopi, two bright and one obscure.” The usual reconstruction identifies the two bright objects as the Magellanic Clouds and the dark one as the Coalsack. The wording is not a constellation name.

**1515–1516 — Pietro Martire d’Anghiera.** Descriptions and a diagram of southern polar phenomena circulated in *De Orbe Novo*. Surviving versions complicate the precise dating of labels on the diagram. The evidence predates Magellan’s circumnavigation.

**1519–1522 / manuscript circulation in the 1520s — Antonio Pigafetta.** Pigafetta’s account of Magellan’s voyage says that near the Antarctic pole were many small stars grouped like two clouds. He did not name them for Magellan and supplied no Dorado figure.

### Invention of Dorado

**1595 — departure of the first Dutch expedition to the East Indies.** Petrus Plancius instructed chief pilot **Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser** to observe southern stars. The four-ship expedition under Cornelis de Houtman departed in April 1595.

**1595–1596 — observations at sea and in the East Indies.** Keyser made positional observations, almost certainly with assistance from expedition colleagues. Frederik de Houtman’s exact role in the first catalogue remains debated.

**September 1596 — Keyser’s death.** Keyser died during the expedition near Bantam/Banten. His observations survived and reached Plancius when the expedition returned in 1597.

**Late 1597 or 1598 — first surviving constellation image.** Plancius and Jodocus Hondius produced a 35-cm celestial globe in Amsterdam incorporating twelve new southern figures, including Dorado. No securely surviving 1598 example may be the very first pull or globe made, so catalogues often write “1597/98.”

**1600/1601 and 1602 — globular transmission.** Hondius and Willem Janszoon Blaeu issued globes repeating the Plancian southern figures. These helped stabilize the iconography.

**1603 — de Houtman’s catalogue.** Frederik de Houtman published a Malay and Malagasy vocabulary in Amsterdam whose appendix catalogued southern stars. Its heading **Den Dorado** is the earliest directly printed vernacular catalogue name securely available.

**1603 — Bayer’s *Uranometria*.** Johann Bayer’s Augsburg atlas provided the first printed atlas plate of the twelve southern constellations. Dorado is shown as a large fish near the flying fish. Bayer’s authority carried it into European learned astronomy.

### The Xiphias period

**1627 — Kepler’s *Tabulae Rudolphinae*.** Kepler’s edition of Tycho Brahe’s material contains southern-star lists and calls the figure **Xiphias**. This is the earliest located use of the swordfish name for the constellation. The change may have been a learned zoological reinterpretation rather than a simple mistranslation; no surviving note by Kepler explains it.

**1627 — Julius Schiller.** Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Augsburg, replaced pagan and secular figures with Christian subjects. Dorado and Piscis Volans were combined as **Abel the Just** (*Abel Iustus*), plate/constellation LIII. Schiller supplied both traditional and Christianized stellar references. [Library of Congress facsimile record](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/); [ETH Zürich interactive edition](https://astrorara.library.ethz.ch/en/coelum-stellatum).

**1679 — Edmond Halley.** Halley’s catalogue of southern stars, based on observations from St Helena, used both Dorado and Xiphias terminology. His improved positions strengthened the constellation’s astronomical legitimacy independent of its fish species.

**1690 — Johannes Hevelius.** The posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia*, published at Danzig by Elisabeth Hevelius, included the constellation in an elaborately engraved, globe-view southern sky. Its mirrored orientation is intentional, not an engraving error.

### Lacaille, Bode and the atlas tradition

**1751–1752 — Lacaille at the Cape.** Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed nearly 10,000 southern stars. His work transformed the cartography surrounding Dorado.

**1756 — Lacaille’s southern map.** He labeled the clouds *le Grand Nuage* and *le Petit Nuage*, introduced Mensa around the larger cloud’s southern side, and standardized many stellar letters later printed in his 1763 catalogue.

**1763 — *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*.** Lacaille’s posthumously published catalogue supplied the durable Bayer-style Greek-letter sequence for Dorado. Describing these letters as Bayer’s own is a historical misattribution.

**1782 — Bode, *Vorstellung der Gestirne*.** Bode’s German popular atlas calls the constellation **Schwerdt-Fisch**. It was a corrected and expanded adaptation of the Fortin/Flamsteed atlas tradition. [Heidelberg’s Bode atlas history](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/einl.html).

**1799 — Christian Friedrich Goldbach.** Goldbach’s atlas displays the instability of the translation: one version labels the figure as a swordfish, while associated explanatory treatment can render it as a goldfish.

**1801 — Bode, *Uranographia*.** Bode placed the figure on the twentieth, south-polar plate and labeled it **Xiphias**. The same plate includes *Nubecula Major*, *Mons Mensae*, *Piscis Volans*, *Pluteum Pictoris*, Reticulum, Horologium and the other southern figures. A surviving atlas description lists the plate’s exact Latin labels. [David Rumsey catalogue record](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~283901~90056536%3ATitle--Uranographia-sive-Astrorum-D); [plate contents and atlas description](https://raremaps.com/gallery/detail/69577/uranographia-sive-astrorum-descriptio).

Bode’s companion volume is:

*Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne, nebst Verzeichniss der geraden Aufsteigung und Abweichung von 17,240 Sternen, Doppelsternen, Nebelflecken und Sternhaufen*.

It describes the entire atlas and catalogues 17,240 entries, not “830 stars in 109 groups.” [Digitized 1801 companion volume](https://books.google.com/books/about/Allgemeine_Beschreibung_und_Nachweisung.html?id=NUlRAAAAcAAJ).

Bode’s title page calls the work *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio viginti tabulis aeneis incisa*—a description of the stars engraved on twenty copper plates. It was issued in Berlin at the author’s expense with support acknowledged to Friedrich von Hahn. The fish on plate XX is long-bodied with a projecting snout consistent with the Xiphias/swordfish reception and positioned toward the smaller flying fish.

### Nineteenth-century reception

**1822 — Alexander Jamieson.** Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* inherited much of the late pictorial-atlas tradition. But because it was designed for constellations observable from Britain, Dorado was not a principal card-like subject in the way northern figures were.

**1824/1825 — *Urania’s Mirror*.** The famous pierced cards copied Jamieson closely. The set was advertised as showing constellations visible from Britain; consequently it does **not** supply a standard Dorado card. Claims that “every Bode constellation, including Dorado, appeared in *Urania’s Mirror*” are false. The cards’ anonymous “lady” authorship was later connected with Richard Rouse Bloxam, though possible involvement by Ann Bloxam remains discussed. [Royal Astronomical Society](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror).

**1835 onward — Elijah Burritt.** Burritt’s American *Atlas Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens* included south-polar mapping in editions broad enough to display Dorado, generally within the swordfish/golden-fish inherited convention. [Library of Congress atlas](https://www.loc.gov/resource/g3180m.gct00292/?st=gallery).

**Argelander and Heis.** Their nineteenth-century European naked-eye cataloguing advanced stellar photometry and constellation mapping but was constrained by northern observing latitudes. Dorado consequently received much less independent treatment than northern constellations. Their importance to Dorado is chiefly methodological, not iconographic.

**1877–1879 — Gould.** Benjamin Apthorp Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* catalogued the southern naked-eye sky and supplied the numbered designations now misleadingly called Flamsteed numbers in some databases.

### International standardization

**1922, Rome — documented.** At its first General Assembly, the IAU adopted the modern list of 88 constellations and their three-letter abbreviations. Dorado was retained as **Dor**. The 1922 action standardized names and abbreviations, not the final borders.

**1925, Cambridge — documented.** Delporte presented a boundary scheme at the second General Assembly.

**1928, Leiden — documented.** The IAU approved the new boundaries.

**1930 — documented.** Delporte published *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* and its atlas. Dorado became a legally exclusive astronomical area bounded by B1875 meridians and parallels. Statements that “the IAU drew the borders at Rome in 1922” compress four distinct stages and are inaccurate.

## Invention and reception

### Who invented Dorado?

Three answers occur in the literature:

1. **Keyser invented it.**
2. **Keyser and Frederik de Houtman invented it together.**
3. **Plancius selected and named the figures from navigators’ measurements.**

The evidence supports a divided attribution.

- **Documented:** Plancius commissioned or trained Keyser to observe the southern sky.
- **Documented:** Keyser made observations during the first voyage and died before returning.
- **Documented:** the data reached Plancius.
- **Documented:** Plancius’s globe is the earliest surviving image of the twelve figures.
- **Documented:** Frederik de Houtman printed the earliest surviving detailed catalogue in 1603.
- **Uncertain:** which man chose the fish figures and their exact boundaries.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** the observational labour was Keyser’s and de Houtman’s, while Plancius probably exercised substantial editorial control over how the stars became pictorial constellations.

Contemporary and later attributions conflict, and de Houtman did not clearly set out Keyser’s contribution in his published catalogue. Recent research therefore warns against assigning the entire invention exclusively to any one of the three. [“Early Star Charts of the Dutch East India Company”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12992).

### What did the figure commemorate?

It did not honor a king, patron, machine or saint. It belonged to a set dominated by natural-history curiosities and navigational encounters: bird of paradise, chameleon, crane, peacock, phoenix, toucan, flying fish and the Indigenous figure *Indus*. Dorado most plausibly memorialized a spectacular pelagic fish familiar from tropical voyages.

The story that it commemorated **El Dorado**, the legendary golden city or gilded ruler of South America, is a **modern verbal association without historical evidence**. The same Spanish adjective lies behind both words, but the founding images depict a fish and the de Houtman heading names the fish.

### Dorado chasing Volans

- **Documented iconography:** Bayer and successors place the large fish immediately behind or toward the flying fish.
- **Natural-history fact:** dolphinfish prey on flying fish.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** the pair likely represents a chase observed or known by sailors.
- **Unverified as voyage testimony:** no quoted Keyser/de Houtman journal passage explicitly telling this story has been traced.
- **Not classical myth:** it has no Eratosthenic or Hyginian source.

## Myths and legends

### Classical Mediterranean mythology

There are **no ancient Greek or Roman myths of the constellation Dorado**. It did not exist in classical uranography.

Greek mythology contains many prophetic, monstrous and rescuing fishes, and the swordfish was known zoologically, but none was attached to these stars. The following often-made associations are therefore rejected unless explicitly presented as modern analogy:

- Perseus’ sea monster is **Cetus**, not Dorado.
- Aphrodite and Eros as fishes belong to **Pisces** or Piscis Austrinus traditions, not Dorado.
- Arion’s rescuer is associated with **Delphinus**, the mammalian dolphin, not the dolphinfish.
- The fish swallowing or preserving divine beings in Syrian traditions concerns Pisces/Piscis Austrinus, not Dorado.
- Pliny’s *xiphias* or sword-shaped comet language does not attest a constellation Xiphias.

There can be no exhaustive Dorado entry from Eratosthenes, Hyginus, Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus or Pausanias because all predate the invention by more than a millennium.

### Christianized sky

**1627 tradition/modern Christian invention:** Julius Schiller combined Dorado and Piscis Volans into **Abel the Just**, son of Adam and Eve, murdered by Cain. The relevant biblical narrative is Genesis 4:1–16, but Genesis does not place Abel among these stars. The catasterism is Schiller’s Christianizing program.

Schiller’s choice can be read iconographically rather than astronomically: he replaced pagan and secular constellation pictures with biblical history, assigned Old Testament subjects to the southern heavens, and retained the stars’ coordinates beneath the new sacred figures. The system did not displace Bayer’s names in general astronomy.

### The “White Ox” of al-Sufi

This is the dossier’s most important contested prehistory.

**What al-Sufi actually reports:** beneath Canopus were said to be two “feet of Canopus,” and beneath those bright white stars not visible in Iraq or Najd, called *al-Baqar* by inhabitants of Tihama. Al-Sufi says Ptolemy did not mention them and that he did not know whether the report was true.

**What later writers claimed:** al-Sufi personally observed the LMC in 964 and named it “the White Ox” or “White Bull.”

**Assessment — disputed, probably an overstatement.**

- The text is hearsay, not an observation by al-Sufi.
- It speaks of stars, not unmistakably of a cloud or nebula.
- *Al-Baqar* is plural “cattle/oxen,” not securely a singular “White Ox.”
- Shiraz was too far north for the LMC to be a normal object from al-Sufi’s observatory.
- A fifteenth-century navigator such as Ahmad ibn Majid may offer a later and more relevant use of the name for a southern nebulous object.
- The identification with the Magellanic Clouds is geographically plausible but textually not certain.

Ihsan Hafez’s study explicitly calls the confident LMC attribution a recent claim arising from later material rather than al-Sufi’s own observation. [Hafez thesis](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/28854/14/JCU_28854_Hafez_2010_thesis.pdf); [overview of al-Sufi’s constellations and the error](https://www.ianridpath.com/startales/alsufi.html).

### Indigenous Australian traditions of the LMC

These traditions concern the conspicuous cloud inside modern Dorado, not the European fish constellation.

**Documented traditions, recorded in different places and periods:**

- In western Victoria, Dawson recorded the Large Cloud as **kuurn kuuronn**, a male “native companion” or crane, and the Small Cloud as its female partner.
- Other groups understood the Clouds as two brolgas.
- Some accounts make them camps or openings into the sky-world.
- Adelaide traditions described them as ashes of rainbow lorikeets killed treacherously.
- Kimberley traditions described exploded lily roots.
- Other recorded interpretations include two brothers who receive the dead, culture heroes, an old man and old woman with a nearby fire, or poisoned fish.

These must not be fused into a single “Aboriginal myth”: they belong to distinct peoples, languages and local cosmologies. A scholarly review surveys the variants and their sources. [Norris, “Dawes Review 5: Australian Aboriginal Astronomy and Navigation”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/publications-of-the-astronomical-society-of-australia/article/dawes-review-5-australian-aboriginal-astronomy-and-navigation/6485EEA891C19A2FC6F0C94DBC24DB75); [Dawson’s 1900 text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Dawson_-_Australian_aborigines_%281900%29.djvu/115).

### Polynesian traditions

Polynesian languages preserve a widespread name-family reconstructed or recorded as **Mahu/Mafu/Maʿafu** for one or both Magellanic Clouds. Examples include:

- Rarotongan *Ngā Mau*;
- Tahitian *Mahu-inia* and *Mahu-iraro*;
- Tongan *Maʿafu-lele* and *Maʿafu-toka*;
- Tuamotuan *Mahu-runga* and *Mahu-raro*;
- Takuu *Mahu/Mafu*.

These names demonstrate independent recognition of the Clouds. They do not prove a Polynesian constellation identical to Dorado’s fish. [POLLEX comparative database](https://pollex.eva.mpg.de/entry/maqafu.2/).

### South American traditions

Tupi-Guarani names reported for the Clouds include **Tapi’i Huguá**, “tapir’s watering place,” and **Coxi Huguá**, “peccary/skunk-pig watering place.” The surviving documentation is comparatively modern and must be handled community by community; it is not evidence that Vespucci learned or transmitted those names. [VISCACHA cultural-naming discussion](https://www.astro.iag.usp.br/~viscacha/nameclouds.html).

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Arabic and Bedouin astronomy

Dorado itself is absent from the classical Arabic adaptation of Ptolemaic astronomy. The nearest relevant material is the southern Arabian report of *al-Baqar* below Canopus. It is not a Bedouin Dorado asterism and cannot securely be mapped star-for-star onto α, β, γ and δ Doradus.

No Arabic lunar mansion crosses Dorado. The lunar mansions follow the Moon’s path near the ecliptic; Dorado is roughly 60° or more from it.

### Persian astronomy

Al-Sufi was Persian, but his catalogue used Arabic scholarly language and Ptolemaic constellation structure. No premodern Persian Dorado figure has been found. The frequent formulation “the Persian constellation White Ox” incorrectly blends al-Sufi’s nationality, a southern Arabian reported name, and a modern identification of the LMC.

### Chinese astronomy

Ancient Chinese astronomers did not include Dorado in the Three Enclosures and Twenty-Eight Mansions because it lay below their effective southern horizon.

After European charts entered China through Jesuit collaboration, the far-southern sky was organized as the **Twenty-three Southern Asterisms**. Stars now in Dorado were assigned to:

- **夾白, Jiābái**, “White Patches Attached”;
- **金魚, Jīnyú**, “Goldfish.”

Modern Chinese calls the Western constellation **劍魚座, Jiànyú Zuò**, “Swordfish Constellation.” Thus Chinese nomenclature preserves both phases of the European ambiguity: Goldfish in the Jesuit southern system and Swordfish in the modern constellation name.

### Indian astronomy

Traditional nakshatras lie along the ecliptic and do not cross Dorado. No Sanskrit constellation securely equivalent to the Dutch fish was found. Modern Indian-language lists translate the IAU figure, but those translations are modern nomenclature, not Vedic antecedents.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic traditions

Dorado is absent from the Hebrew Bible, rabbinic constellation lists and the zodiacal/mansion schemes used in medieval Jewish astronomy. The constellation is also absent from the fifteen Behenian stars and from the standard magical star lists inherited through Arabic and Latin sources.

Claims connecting Dorado with the biblical fish of Jonah, Leviathan, the tribe of Zebulun, a sephirah, or an occult “golden fish” are **modern inventions or unverified correspondences** unless supported by a specific modern author. They are not ancient Hebrew mappings.

### Astrology, magic, alchemy and Hermetism

Dorado has no entry in Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*: Ptolemy did not know the constellation. None of its stars is one of the standard fifteen Behenian fixed stars. No traditional lunar mansion traverses it.

Later astrologers sometimes assign a generic “fish” nature, sea travel, wealth from the word “golden,” or Mars/Neptune symbolism to Dorado. These are modern analogical practices. They should not be attributed to Ptolemy, Hermes Trismegistus, Agrippa or the *Picatrix*.

No historically grounded alchemical interpretation of Bode’s Xiphias was found. Associations with gold, the philosophers’ stone or *aurum philosophicum* arise naturally from the word *dorado* but remain modern wordplay unless a dated text is produced.

## Iconography

### What is not part of Dorado’s iconographic history

- **Farnese Atlas:** no Dorado; the sculpture preserves the classical northern/Ptolemaic tradition and predates the constellation.
- **Al-Sufi manuscripts:** no Dorado image. Their 48 figures follow Ptolemy. The southern-Arabian hearsay occurs under Argo Navis, not as a golden fish drawing.
- **Dürer’s 1515 star maps:** no Dorado. Dürer mapped the Ptolemaic constellations before Keyser’s voyage.

### Dated sequence

**1597/1598, Amsterdam — Plancius/Hondius globe.** Earliest surviving depiction. Dorado appears among twelve new figures around the southern pole.

**1600/1601, Amsterdam — Hondius globes.** Repetition and dissemination of Plancius’s southern scheme.

**1602, Amsterdam — Blaeu globe.** Dorado and Piscis Volans appear as neighboring natural-history figures.

**1603, Augsburg — Bayer, *Uranometria*.** First printed atlas depiction. The image makes the large fish–flying fish pairing famous.

**1627, Augsburg — Schiller, *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*.** Dorado and Volans disappear zoologically beneath **Abel the Just**. The engravings were by Lucas Kilian. Surviving convex and concave versions help distinguish the observer’s and globe-maker’s orientation.

**1660, Amsterdam — Cellarius, *Harmonia Macrocosmica*.** Southern planispheres reproduce the enlarged post-Bayer sky in lavish Baroque cartography, though individual editions and plates vary in labeling.

**1679, St Helena/London — Halley’s catalogue.** Primarily scientific rather than pictorial, but crucial to the fish’s continued cartographic presence.

**1690, Danzig — Hevelius.** Hevelius’s posthumous atlas engraves the constellation in mirrored globe view. Confusing that reversal with an accidental left-right printing error is a common modern mistake.

**1756, Paris — Lacaille.** A restrained scientific map surrounds the fish with new instrument constellations and labels the Magellanic Clouds as large and small clouds.

**1782, Berlin/Stralsund — Bode, *Vorstellung der Gestirne*.** The figure is a **Schwerdt-Fisch**.

**1801, Berlin — Bode, *Uranographia*, plate XX.** **Xiphias** is a streamlined, sharp-snouted fish next to *Piscis Volans* and above/near *Nubecula Major* and *Mons Mensae*. The great sheet integrates scientific coordinates, thousands of catalogue objects and full pictorial art. The atlas contains 20 copperplates and the companion catalogue 17,240 stellar and nebulous entries. [Science Museum Group copy](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8563366/uranographia-sive-astrorum-descriptio).

**1822, London — Jamieson.** The British atlas tradition reproduces many Bode-derived figures but does not make far-southern Dorado a central British observing subject.

**1824/1825, London — *Urania’s Mirror*.** No dedicated Dorado card; the set’s practical northern visibility limits matter.

**1835 and later, United States — Burritt.** South-polar planispheres make the southern constellation accessible to American readers even though it cannot be seen from much of the continental north.

### Coins, ceilings, portals and calendars

No securely documented ancient coin, Greco-Roman ceiling, medieval cathedral portal or manuscript calendar depicts Dorado, because all predate its invention.

Later decorative celestial ceilings and globes may include it as part of a complete post-Bayer sky, but no historically important Dorado-specific architectural program was verified. Images of generic fish on churches, zodiac calendars or coins cannot be identified as Dorado without accompanying stars or labels.

## People, events and works

### Navigators and astronomers

**Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser:** chief pilot and principal observer of the first Dutch voyage; died in 1596 before publication.

**Frederik de Houtman:** expedition participant and publisher of the 1603 southern catalogue; later made additional observations. His printed *Den Dorado* is a foundational witness.

**Petrus Plancius:** theologian, cartographer and organizer who commissioned the observations and first placed the figures on a surviving globe.

**Jodocus Hondius and Willem Blaeu:** globe makers who disseminated the constellation.

**Johann Bayer:** established its printed iconography in *Uranometria*.

**Johannes Kepler:** introduced or at least first printed the alternate **Xiphias** name in the *Rudolphine Tables*.

**Edmond Halley:** observed the southern sky from St Helena and retained both name traditions.

**Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille:** transformed Dorado’s scientific stellar content from the Cape and assigned most of its surviving Greek letters.

**Johann Elert Bode:** canonized the swordfish interpretation visually and linguistically in his influential German and Latin atlases.

**John Herschel:** from the Cape, catalogued hundreds of clusters and nebulae in the Magellanic Clouds; his 1847 results made the LMC a richly resolved astronomical system rather than merely a cloudy patch.

**Henrietta Swan Leavitt:** her study of Magellanic Cloud variables established the Cepheid period–luminosity relation, a foundation of the cosmic distance scale. Her variables were largely in the Clouds’ modern constellation territories, especially Dorado.

**Ian Shelton, Oscar Duhalde and Albert Jones:** independently associated with the discovery or earliest visual recognition of SN 1987A; photographic plates supplied additional timing evidence.

### Navigation

Dorado’s stars are not bright enough to rival Canopus, Achernar or the Southern Cross as principal traditional navigation stars. The Dutch observations were themselves navigational astronomy, but no evidence was found that mariners routinely “steered by Dorado” as a named constellation.

The Magellanic Clouds, however, were useful conspicuous southern-sky markers, and the Dutch project sought accurate southern reference stars for latitude, orientation, globes and long-distance navigation. It would be safer to say explorers **mapped** Dorado during navigation than that Dorado became a major navigational compass.

### Ships

**USS Dorado (SS-248) — documented.** A Gato-class submarine, named for the dolphin fish, commissioned in 1943 and lost with all hands during its transfer to the Panama Canal Zone. The precise cause of loss remains unresolved; wartime friendly-fire and German U-boat hypotheses have both been examined. [U.S. Naval History and Heritage Command](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/d/dorado.html).

**USS Dorado (SS-526) — documented but incomplete.** A projected Tench-class submarine whose construction was cancelled. The name came from the fish, not demonstrably from the constellation.

No major astronomical spacecraft officially named simply *Dorado* was identified. “30 Doradus,” “Tarantula” and LMC appear in survey and observing-program names, but they are targets rather than spacecraft.

### Literature, music, art and buildings

Dorado has a thinner independent cultural afterlife than Orion, Andromeda or the Southern Cross. Searches found many modern poems, songs, businesses and fictional vessels called *Dorado* or *El Dorado*, but most refer to gold, the legendary city, or the fish—not demonstrably the constellation.

The constellation’s most consequential cultural works remain the atlases themselves:

- Bayer’s *Uranometria*;
- Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*;
- Hevelius’s *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*;
- Lacaille’s southern map and catalogue;
- Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* and *Uranographia*.

“Dorado” in a title should not be assumed to refer to the constellation without internal astronomical evidence.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Dolphinfish, goldfish or swordfish?

**Finding:** the original name most plausibly denotes the dolphinfish/mahi-mahi; goldfish is a misleading literal translation; swordfish is a historically real alternate interpretation.

Evidence:

- de Houtman’s *Den Dorado* preserves the Iberian sailors’ fish-name.
- The fish’s tropical-ocean setting and proximity to Volans favor *Coryphaena hippurus*.
- *Xiphias* first appears in the astronomical record in 1627.
- Kepler, Halley, Flamsteed-derived catalogues, Bode and German vernacular atlases spread the swordfish.
- Modern Chinese and the IAU’s popular English gloss retain “Swordfish,” even though many contemporary constellation historians identify the original as a dolphinfish.

It is therefore too simple to call “swordfish” merely a modern mistranslation. It is zoologically secondary but historically entrenched.

### 2. Was Bayer the inventor?

No. Bayer published the first printed atlas image in 1603, but the constellation was already on Plancius’s 1597/98 globe. Calling it “Bayer’s invention” confuses publication with creation.

### 3. Who among Keyser, de Houtman and Plancius deserves credit?

Disputed. Keyser’s observational priority, de Houtman’s printed catalogue and Plancius’s first surviving figurative arrangement are all documented. Exact responsibility for naming and connecting the stars is not.

### 4. Did al-Sufi discover the Large Magellanic Cloud?

Probably not in the strong form commonly stated. He transmitted hearsay about southern stars and explicitly withheld judgment. The confident statement that he personally observed a single “White Ox” is unsupported by his wording.

### 5. Did Magellan discover or name the Clouds?

No. European descriptions predate his voyage; southern peoples knew the Clouds from antiquity; Pigafetta described them but did not call them “Magellanic.” The eponym arose through later reception of the voyage.

### 6. Is the LMC wholly in Dorado?

No. Most is in Dorado; part lies in Mensa. Statements that the entire LMC is in Dorado ignore Delporte’s boundary.

### 7. Are 3, 8, 20, 22, 29, 33 and 40 Doradus Flamsteed numbers?

Not properly. They belong to later southern numbering, particularly Gould’s, because Flamsteed’s original observing programme did not cover this far-southern field. Database columns frequently flatten all constellation-number designations into “Flamsteed.”

### 8. Did Bayer assign Dorado’s Greek letters?

No. Lacaille assigned the sequence in the eighteenth century. The generic phrase “Bayer designation” describes the notation system, not necessarily Bayer’s personal act.

### 9. Does Dorado have classical mythology?

No. Modern constellation books sometimes give it “myths” by borrowing stories from Pisces, Delphinus, Cetus or Jonah. Those are analogies or inventions.

### 10. Is the chase of Volans a documented sailors’ story?

The visual relationship and biological behavior are convincing, but an explicit sixteenth-century narrative has not been traced. It should be labeled scholarly reconstruction.

### 11. Is *Polus Doradinalis* standard?

No. It is an antiquarian Latin expression associated with Caesius and the fact that the south ecliptic pole lies near the fish’s head. Modern astronomy uses “south ecliptic pole.”

### 12. Hoaxes and popular errors

No major Dorado-specific astronomical hoax was located. Recurring misinformation consists instead of:

- invented ancient myths;
- conflation with El Dorado;
- calling it a pet goldfish;
- claiming al-Sufi drew or observed it;
- saying Magellan discovered the Clouds;
- attributing Lacaille’s letters to Bayer;
- calling Gould numbers Flamsteed numbers;
- saying the IAU abolished Xiphias as a separate constellation—Xiphias was chiefly an alternate name for Dorado, not a separate adjacent territory;
- placing Dorado on a dedicated *Urania’s Mirror* card;
- confusing the south ecliptic pole with the south celestial pole.

## Open questions

1. **Who selected the name and figure?** No surviving instruction from Keyser, de Houtman or Plancius decisively assigns the act.

2. **What exact fish did the earliest globe engraver intend?** “Dorado” and the Volans relationship strongly favor mahi-mahi, but early pictures are stylized and zoological nomenclature was unstable.

3. **Why did Kepler choose Xiphias?** The 1627 label is clear; his reasoning has not been located. It may be translation, learned classicization or pictorial reinterpretation.

4. **How precisely should the early catalogue stars be matched to modern stars?** Proper motion is minor, but transcription errors, crude positions, duplicate entries and shifting constellation anatomy produce contested identifications among faint stars.

5. **What exactly were Ibn Qutaybah’s and al-Sufi’s southern “cattle”?** The LMC, both Clouds, bright southern stars, or a mixed report remain possible. Al-Sufi’s own agnosticism should be retained.

6. **When did “Magellanic Clouds” become universal?** The eponym spread gradually through seventeenth- to nineteenth-century European literature; different languages retained “Cape Clouds,” “large/small cloud” and Indigenous names.

7. **What is the requested “Bode collection on chain, 830 stars in 109 groups”?** No matching phrase appeared in the digitized *Uranographia*, its companion text, or consulted catalogue descriptions. A museum catalogue number or image of the label would be necessary to investigate it responsibly.

8. **How many novae and supernova remnants are “in Dorado”?** The answer changes with surveys and with classification of LMC remnants. A date-stamped SIMBAD/VizieR query is required for a truly exhaustive scientific inventory.
