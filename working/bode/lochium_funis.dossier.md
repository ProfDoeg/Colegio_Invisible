# Lochium Funis: Research Dossier

> **Status:** **not kept by the IAU.** *Lochium Funis* was Johann Elert Bode’s short-lived “Log and Line,” introduced in the *Uranographia* of 1801. It never acquired generally accepted boundaries, an official abbreviation, or an IAU genitive. In 1922 the IAU adopted a canon of 88 constellations that included **Pyxis** but omitted *Lochium Funis*; Delporte’s boundaries, approved in 1928 and published in 1930, placed the relevant sky principally in **Pyxis**, with adjacent territory in **Hydra, Puppis, Vela, and Antlia**. Strictly speaking, the IAU did not pass a recorded motion “abolishing Lochium Funis”: omission from the accepted list ended any possible official status. [IAU history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

## Basic identification

### Name and meaning

**Documented fact — Bode, 1801.** The plate title is *Pixis Nautica et Lochium Funis*. Bode’s accompanying German name was **Logleine**, “log line.” The established English rendering is **the Log and Line**.

The Latin is somewhat artificial:

- *lochium* is a Neo-Latin technical noun for a nautical log or log apparatus, ultimately associated with European nautical *loch/log* rather than a normal classical Latin word.
- *funis* is ordinary Latin for “rope,” “cord,” or “line.”
- The intended sense is therefore “the log and its rope,” not “a log made of rope.”
- A grammatical genitive sometimes supplied by modern obsolete-constellation lists is **Lochii Funis**, but this was never standardized by the IAU.
- Modern unofficial abbreviations such as **Loc** occur on websites. They have no historical or official authority; one such page even gives the nominative *Lochium Funis* as its “genitive,” illustrating the danger of treating modern databases as nomenclatural evidence. [SkyEye](https://www.obliquity.com/skyeye/88const/Loc.html)

Translations encountered after Bode include:

- German: *Logleine*, later also *Log und Logleine*.
- French: *le Loch et la ligne*, or descriptions such as *ligne de loch*.
- Italian: *Solcometro e Sagola*.
- Spanish: *la corredera y la línea*, *cuerda náutica y línea*, or simply *el Loch*.
- Portuguese: *log marítimo* or *barquinha e linha*.
- Chinese modern translations include 測定索座/测定索座, literally a “measuring-line constellation.” This is a retrospective translation, not a traditional Chinese asterism.
- Eliza A. Bowen’s *Astronomy by Observation* (1888) apparently printed **Linea Nautica**, “Nautical Line,” in approximately the same area. This is best regarded as a late renaming or adaptation, not evidence that Bode’s exact constellation enjoyed continuous use. [Bowen atlas discussion and reproduction](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/Bowen%201888.htm)

No Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, or Sanskrit name for *Lochium Funis* exists: it was a modern European invention.

### What the instrument was

**Documented technological context; Bode’s celestial application is a modern invention.** A traditional ship log consisted of:

1. a buoyant wooden **log**, commonly a quadrant-shaped “chip log” weighted so that it resisted being pulled through the water;
2. a long **line** knotted at calculated intervals;
3. a reel from which the line paid out;
4. a sandglass, often of about half a minute.

The log was thrown astern. With the piece of wood relatively stationary in the water, the number of knots paid out during the timed interval provided the vessel’s approximate speed. This practice is the source of the nautical unit “knot.”

Bode’s engraving shows the rope looped repeatedly around Lacaille’s compass, with plotted stars serving visually as knots. The wooden log lies to the east of the compass and the long cord winds southward and back around it. John Barentine interprets the composition as a matched navigational ensemble: the compass gives the ship’s direction while the log gives its speed. That interpretation is strongly supported by the iconography, although Bode left no known extended essay explaining his artistic intention. [Barentine, *Uncharted Constellations* chapter abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299642367_Lochium_Funis)

### Position and boundaries

**Documented fact.** Bode put the figure on Plate XVIII, above or north of the ancient ship Argo and beside:

- *Pixis Nautica* at its center;
- Hydra and Bode’s *Felis* to the north;
- *Antlia Pneumatica* to the east;
- Argo Navis—principally the later Puppis and Vela—to the west and south.

Bode did not provide a Delporte-style polygon for *Lochium Funis*. His pictorial boundary enclosed Pyxis and the rope together. In the accompanying *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, the stars were catalogued under the combined heading for **Pyxis Nautica and Lochium Funis**, preventing a clean reconstruction of two independent star lists. Barentine therefore calls it an “elaboration” of Pyxis rather than a completely autonomous catalogue unit.

**Modern boundaries.** Pyxis now covers about 220.8 square degrees, bordered by Hydra, Puppis, Vela, and Antlia. Its official abbreviation is **Pyx** and its genitive **Pyxidis**. Those forms belong to Pyxis, not to the abolished figure. [IAU style manual](https://iauarchive.eso.org/static/publications/stylemanual1989.pdf)

## Stars and objects

### Stars actually forming Bode’s device

The surviving engraving, not a separate Bayer catalogue, is the governing evidence.

**Secure identification:**

- **ε Pyxidis** is on Bode’s wooden log.
- **λ Pyxidis** is also associated with the log.
- Numerous fainter stars, mostly around fifth magnitude or below, punctuate the knotted line surrounding the compass. A modern observing account independently identifies ε and λ as the stars on the log. [Astronomy magazine, May 2026](https://www.astronomy.com/observing/may-2026-whats-in-the-southern-hemisphere-sky-this-month/)

**Open reconstruction problem.** No reliable primary-source concordance located during this research assigns every individual knot to a modern HD, HIP, or Gaia source. Because Bode catalogued the compass and line jointly, lists that simply make all Pyxis stars members of *Lochium Funis* overstate the evidence.

### The present Pyxis sequence

Lacaille assigned ten Greek letters in this region, omitting iota:

**α, β, γ, δ, ε, ζ, η, θ, κ, and λ Pyxidis.**

The brightest are approximately:

- **α Pyxidis**, magnitude 3.7, a blue giant;
- **β Pyxidis**, magnitude 4.0, a yellow bright giant;
- **γ Pyxidis**, magnitude 4.0, an orange giant;
- **κ Pyxidis**, about magnitude 4.6 and slightly variable;
- **λ Pyxidis**, about magnitude 4.7;
- **ε Pyxidis**, about magnitude 5.6.

There are **no IAU-approved proper names** for the principal Pyxis stars. Consequently there are no Arabic-derived names comparable to Aldebaran or Betelgeuse, no Greek mythological proper names, and no surviving name taken from Bode’s rope.

There are also **no Flamsteed numbers in Pyxis**. Flamsteed’s numbered catalogue did not establish a sequence for this far-southern Lacaille constellation. Claims such as “1 Pyxidis” should be checked for later catalogue usage rather than assumed to be Flamsteed designations.

### Asterisms

The familiar modern outline is a nearly straight run of **β–α–γ Pyxidis**, sometimes expanded with δ. It represents the compass rather than the vanished rope.

Bode’s rope itself was an asterism in the pictorial sense: its many bends connected faint stars as a series of “knots.” It has no current observing-club standard, though the Royal Astronomical Society of Canada’s World Asterisms Project lists “Log & the Line / Lochium Funis / Logleine” retrospectively in Pyxis. That entry is a historical index, not IAU restoration. [RASC World Asterisms Project](https://rasc.rocks/sites/default/files/World%20Asterisms%20Project%20List%20V%202025.6.pdf)

### Variables, novae, and possible supernova progenitors

These belong to modern Pyxis, not demonstrably to the log itself.

- **T Pyxidis** is a recurrent nova. Recorded eruptions occurred in 1890, 1902, 1920, 1944, 1966, and 2011. It normally remains telescopically faint but has reached roughly seventh magnitude. Suggestions that its white dwarf might eventually produce a Type Ia supernova are astrophysical hypotheses, not a prediction that an explosion is imminent.
- **XX Pyxidis** is a multiperiodic δ Scuti pulsator studied in asteroseismology.
- **β Pyxidis** has been classified as a low-amplitude variable in some catalogues.
- **κ Pyxidis** is listed as a variable giant.
- **CS Pyxidis/HD 73256** is a chromospherically active star hosting a hot-Jupiter planet.

No historical naked-eye supernova can be securely assigned to *Lochium Funis*. Because the figure did not exist until 1801 and lacked fixed borders, retroactively calling an earlier transient a “supernova in Lochium Funis” would be anachronistic.

### Deep-sky objects now in Pyxis

There are **no Messier objects** in Pyxis and none bearing the name Lochium Funis.

Prominent present-day objects include:

- **NGC 2613** — a large, nearly edge-on spiral galaxy.
- **NGC 2627** — open cluster.
- **NGC 2635** — open cluster.
- **NGC 2658** — open cluster.
- **NGC 2663** — elliptical galaxy and radio source.
- **NGC 2818** — planetary nebula projected against the open cluster NGC 2818A. Earlier literature often described the nebula as a cluster member; modern radial-velocity work has questioned or rejected physical membership, so “within the cluster” should not automatically mean “physically belonging to it.”
- **NGC 2818A** — the open cluster behind or around the nebula’s line of sight.
- **NGC 2717, NGC 2772, NGC 2821, NGC 2883, NGC 2888**, and **IC 2469** — galaxies.
- **K 1-2** — planetary nebula with a close binary central system and jets.
- **Pyxis globular cluster** — a remote Galactic-halo globular cluster discovered photographically in the late twentieth century; it is noteworthy for possible associations with accreted halo structures or the Magellanic system.

A catalogue query gives thirteen NGC/IC objects in modern Pyxis. [TheSkyLive catalogue](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/pyxis-deepsky-objects)

### Meteor showers

The broad statement “Pyxis has no meteor showers” occurs in many constellation handbooks but is now too absolute.

The American Meteor Society reports two very weak components collectively called the **theta Pyxidids (TPY)**, with radiants near central Pyxis and peaks separated by roughly two weeks. They are minor modern radiant solutions, not a shower named after *Lochium Funis*. [American Meteor Society](https://www.amsmeteors.org/2024/11/meteor-activity-outlook-for-november-23-29-2024/)

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1750: no Lochium Funis

**Documented absence.** No Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Greco-Roman, medieval Arabic, Persian, Jewish, or European source is known to depict a log line in these stars.

The area’s low southern declination made it poorly observed from the Mediterranean. Ptolemy’s *Almagest* did not have Pyxis or a log-line constellation. Stars in the broader region were either unformed stars outside Argo or parts of Argo’s mast and hull in later cartographic interpretation.

The Farnese Atlas, medieval Aratus manuscripts, al-Sufi’s *Book of the Fixed Stars*, Dürer’s 1515 southern map, Bayer’s *Uranometria* of 1603, and Hevelius’s *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* of 1690 contain no *Lochium Funis*. That absence is decisive: the figure cannot possess an ancient catasterism.

### Antiquity: Argo, not a compass or log

The neighboring ancient constellation was **Argo Navis**, the ship of Jason and the Argonauts. Aratus describes Argo sailing stern-first and only partly visible (*Phaenomena* 342–352); Ptolemy catalogued its stars in *Almagest* VII–VIII. Eratosthenes’ *Catasterisms* 35 and Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* 2.37, give catasteristic accounts of the ship.

These myths belong to Argo. They do **not** mention a magnetic compass, chip log, reel, or knotted line. Associating Bode’s instrument with Jason’s actual equipment is a picturesque modern conflation.

### 1515 — Dürer and the Ptolemaic southern sky

Albrecht Dürer’s printed southern celestial map of 1515 follows the classical-Ptolemaic sky. It shows Argo but neither Pyxis nor *Lochium Funis*.

### 1603 — Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* retains Argo Navis. Because the log figure did not exist, it has no Bayer-letter sequence of its own.

### 1627 — Julius Schiller

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, published at Augsburg with Bayer’s assistance and engravings by Lucas Kilian, replaced Argo Navis with **Noah’s Ark**. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/)

This is relevant only to the same broad sky. Schiller cannot have assigned a saint or object specifically to *Lochium Funis*, which was invented 174 years later. Its future stars lay within or near the chart of Noah’s Ark and unformed surrounding territory. Claims that Schiller “Christianized Lochium Funis” are chronologically impossible.

### 1690 — Hevelius

Hevelius had no such figure. His maps retained the established Argo tradition. Therefore Hevelius neither transferred stars to nor from *Lochium Funis*.

### 1751–1756 — Lacaille’s *la Boussole*

During his Cape of Good Hope survey, Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed and catalogued the southern sky. His planisphere published with the French Academy memoirs in 1756 introduced **la Boussole**, the mariner’s compass.

**Documented fact:** this is the direct precursor around which Bode later wound his rope.

### 1763 — *Pixis Nautica*

Lacaille’s posthumous *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* Latinized the name as **Pixis Nautica**—with *Pixis*, not the classically regular *Pyxis*. Later astronomers normalized the spelling.

The Latin *pyxis* originally means a small box or casket, from Greek πυξίς, and by extension the box of a compass. It does not itself mean “magnetic needle.”

### 1776 — Fortin’s revision of Flamsteed

Jean-Nicolas Fortin’s French edition of Flamsteed’s atlas incorporated Lacaille’s southern constellation as *la Boussole*. This was a later editorial expansion; Flamsteed, who died in 1719, neither drew nor accepted it.

### 1782 — Bode before the log line

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* used an earlier German designation for the compass reported as **Windelmaße/Windmaße** in secondary transcriptions. The proposed reading “Wintelmasse” in a modern asterism handbook appears to be an OCR or transcription problem. The 1782 charts do **not** securely attest *Lochium Funis* as a constellation.

Thus the supplied description of it as part of a pre-existing “Bode collection” should not be read as evidence for an 1782 invention. The secure first appearance is 1801.

### 1801 — invention in the *Uranographia*

**First secure attestation.** Johann Elert Bode published the enormous *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* at Berlin. It comprised twenty large copperplate charts and was accompanied by the bilingual German–French *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, cataloguing 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. [ETH Zürich copy](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148)

Plate XVIII bears the combined designation **“PIXIS NAUTICA ET LOCHIUM FUNIS.”** It depicts:

- Lacaille’s compass inside a rectangular or box-like mounting;
- a wooden log to its east;
- a rope paying out in many loops;
- plotted stars acting as knots;
- the whole ensemble on or immediately above Argo’s deck.

Bode placed the two within one pictorial enclosure and catalogued their stars together. His German label **Logleine** is recorded by R. H. Allen and later histories. [Allen, *Star Names and Their Meanings*, Argo Navis](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Argo_Navis%2A.html)

The invention honored no monarch, patron, expedition, or particular inventor. It celebrated an ordinary nautical measuring instrument and completed a functional pair with Lacaille’s compass—an especially pure example of the Enlightenment “instrument sky.”

### 1817–1820 — Bode’s later combined label

A modern asterism compilation reports that Bode’s *Nachtrag zu seiner Anleitung zur Kenntniss des gestirnten Himmels* used **“Compas Logleine.”** If the transcription is correct, this is evidence that Bode himself continued to regard compass and log line as a combined figure rather than two fully independent constellations. The claim needs confirmation against a clean primary scan; the online handbook contains several OCR errors in the title and earlier German name.

### 1822 — Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* shows **Pyxis Nautica**, not a clearly independent *Lochium Funis*. The log line was therefore not received into one of the most influential nineteenth-century pictorial atlases.

### 1835 — Hoffmann

Hoffmann’s *Himmels-Atlas für Freunde und Liebhaber der Sternkunde* reproduced some Bode novelties, including the electric machine. The surviving online comparison does not establish a corresponding autonomous Lochium figure. This is further evidence of its unusually rapid disappearance.

### 1843–1844 — Malus, the Mast

John Herschel proposed suppressing Pyxis and restoring these stars to Argo as **Malus**, the Mast; Francis Baily adopted the proposal in the British Association catalogue. The United States Naval Observatory consequently used designations such as **α Mali**.

This reform competed with Pyxis, not specifically with Bode’s rope. Benjamin A. Gould later restored Pyxis in the *Uranometria Argentina* (1877/1879).

A modern RASC handbook says Admiral W. H. Smyth’s *Bedford Catalogue* of 1844 “lists Lochium Funis.” The searchable evidence located here does not show that Smyth mapped or formally adopted it; at most this may be a textual mention. It therefore does not overturn the conclusion that no major post-Bode atlas maintained the figure.

### 1888 — Bowen’s *Linea Nautica*

Eliza A. Bowen’s school text and atlas *Astronomy by Observation* contains a **Linea Nautica** between Argo, Antlia, Felis, and Pyxis. The resemblance in location and meaning makes descent from Bode highly probable. Nevertheless, the altered name and pedagogical context make it a revival or adaptation, not uninterrupted canonical survival.

### 1899 — Allen

Richard Hinckley Allen wrote: “From other stars Bode formed Lochium Funis, his Logleine, our Log and Line, now entirely fallen into disuse.” This is the clearest nineteenth-century assessment of its reception. [Allen text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Argo_Navis%2A.html)

### 1911 — encyclopedic afterlife

The eleventh edition of the *Encyclopædia Britannica* listed *Lochium funis* among Bode’s additions. This was historical notice, not astronomical use. [Britannica text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/30935/pg30935-images.html)

### 1922 — IAU canon

At its first General Assembly in Rome, the IAU’s Commission on Notations and Units agreed on 88 constellations and three-letter abbreviations. **Pyxis/Pyx** was included; *Lochium Funis* was not. [IAU](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

Therefore:

- “abolished in 1922” is acceptable shorthand;
- “formally rejected in a separate vote” is not documented;
- it had already been effectively obsolete for more than a century.

### 1925–1930 — Delporte

Eugène Delporte proposed rectilinear boundaries in 1925. The IAU approved the system in 1928 at Leiden, and *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* appeared in 1930. [Delporte bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

The bounded area called Pyxis retained the compass stars and most of Bode’s instrument. Parts of the decorative rope that strayed beyond that polygon necessarily fell into Hydra, Puppis, Vela, or Antlia. Since the rope never had numerical borders, an exact star-by-star “transfer” cannot be computed without defining a modern interpretation of the drawing.

### Present

*Lochium Funis* survives in histories of obsolete constellations, reproductions of Bode’s plate, educational lists, and planetarium lore. It is not used in professional object nomenclature.

Calling it “the shortest-lived of Bode’s inventions” is a reasonable characterization of its reception but not a measurable official record. Several Bode novelties were essentially dead on arrival. *Lochium Funis* is exceptional because Bode alone clearly depicted it under that precise name and because it was already described as wholly disused in 1899.

## Myths and legends

### Classical myths: explicit negative finding

**Documented absence.** *Lochium Funis* has no Greek or Roman myth and no classical catasterism. It consequently has:

- no entry in Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- no story in Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- no identity in the scholia to Aratus or Germanicus;
- no account in Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, or Pausanias;
- no ancient person, beast, or object whom a god placed in heaven.

Any exhaustive classical section must stop there rather than transfer all Argo mythology to an eighteenth-century speed-measuring instrument.

### Argo as neighboring mythic context

**Myth, first attested in archaic Greek literature.** The ship Argo belongs to the Argonautic cycle: Athena assisted its construction; Jason sailed in it to recover the Golden Fleece; Hera and Athena protected the expedition. Homer already knows “Argo, famed of all” at *Odyssey* 12.69–72, conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE.

**Myth/catasterism.** Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 35, says the ship was placed among the stars because it was the first ship constructed and because Athena made it speak. Only part appears in heaven, preserving the rudder, mast, and oars while omitting the prow.

**Myth/catasterism.** Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* 2.37, similarly connects the celestial ship with the Argonauts and divine commemoration. Hyginus also transmits variants concerning the speaking timber from Dodona.

**Important distinction:** none of these texts contains a magnetic compass or log line. The claim that Bode supplied “Jason’s log” is modern storytelling.

### Christian transformation

**Christian iconographic invention, 1627.** Julius Schiller substituted **Noah’s Ark** for Argo. That biblical ship may occupy much of the same chart, but no biblical text or Jewish tradition identifies the future Pyxis stars as a compass or measuring rope. [Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/12-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum/)

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian and Egyptian

No secure Babylonian constellation or Egyptian decanal figure has been identified specifically with ε and λ Pyxidis or Bode’s looping rope. The area’s southerly position and the lack of a stable Bode star list make retrospective equations especially hazardous.

### Arabic and Persian

Al-Sufi’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* (c. 964) follows Ptolemy’s 48-constellation framework. It therefore contains Argo and its associated unformed stars, not Pyxis or *Lochium Funis*. No al-Sufi name for Bode’s group exists.

No securely documented Bedouin asterism precisely matching the log’s stars was found. The bright Pyxis sequence also carries no surviving IAU-approved Arabic proper names. Persian astronomers working in the Ptolemaic tradition likewise could not name a constellation created in 1801.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No traceable medieval Hebrew constellation or Kabbalistic celestial mapping corresponds specifically to *Lochium Funis*. General attempts to correlate the tribes of Israel, Hebrew letters, or the paths of the Tree of Life with the zodiac do not supply evidence for a far-southern Bode invention.

### Chinese astronomy

Traditional Chinese uranography divided this area principally between two asterisms:

- **Tiāngǒu 天狗, “Celestial Dog”**, in the Ghost lunar mansion:
  - β Pyxidis = Tiāngǒu 4;
  - α Pyxidis = Tiāngǒu 5;
  - γ Pyxidis = Tiāngǒu 6;
  - δ Pyxidis = Tiāngǒu 7.
- **Tiānmiào 天廟, “Celestial Temple,”** in the Extended Net mansion:
  - θ Pyxidis = Tiānmiào 1;
  - the rest of the temple extended through neighboring Antlia.

This corrects a common compressed statement that α, β, and γ Pyxidis all formed the Celestial Temple: in the cited Chinese mapping, they belong to the **Celestial Dog**, while θ belongs to the Temple. [Chinese identification table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyxis_in_Chinese_astronomy)

The modern Chinese name for Pyxis is **羅盤座/罗盘座**, “Compass Constellation.” It is a translation of the Western figure, not an ancient name.

### Indian astronomy

No major nakshatra is centered on these stars. The twenty-seven or twenty-eight lunar mansions follow the ecliptic, whereas Pyxis lies far south of it. Modern Sanskrit or Indian-language translations of “Pyxis” are therefore adaptations of Western astronomy, not evidence of an indigenous equivalent.

### Astrology

*Lochium Funis* is not zodiacal, is absent from Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*, and has no classical planetary temperament assigned to it.

None of its securely identified stars is one of the traditional fifteen Behenian fixed stars. No Arabic lunar mansion crosses the constellation as a literal sky boundary; lunar mansions are divisions tied to the Moon’s ecliptic path.

Modern astrological sites sometimes assign Pyxis traits such as guidance, navigation, or invention. These are **modern symbolic inventions**, extrapolated from the compass image. They have no ancient authority and still less connection to Bode’s rope.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No pre-1801 alchemical or Hermetic text can refer to *Lochium Funis*. Searches located no documented post-Bode occult school that made it a standard emblem. Interpretations of the coiled line as a serpent, initiatory cord, alchemical measure, or “thread of fate” are possible modern free associations, not a recovered historical tradition.

- **Lacaille, Paris, 1756:** *la Boussole* first appears as an Enlightenment navigational instrument.
- **Lacaille, Paris, 1763:** *Pixis Nautica* in the posthumous *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*.
- **Fortin, Paris, 1776:** *la Boussole* incorporated into the revised Flamsteed atlas.
- **Bode, Berlin, 1801, Plate XVIII:** first and definitive image of *Lochium Funis*. The log is east of the compass; the knotted rope coils around and below the compass; the pairing is bounded and catalogued together.
- **Jamieson, London, 1822:** shows Pyxis Nautica, not Bode’s complete rope.
- **Burritt, New York, 1833–1856 editions:** no verified adoption of *Lochium Funis*.

- **Urania’s Mirror, London, c. 1824–1825:** no separate card for *Lochium Funis*.
- **Delporte, Cambridge, 1930:** featureless scientific boundaries replace pictorial ownership; only Pyxis remains.

No verified ancient coin, cathedral portal, ceiling, manuscript calendar, or civic monument represents *Lochium Funis*. Images of Argo or Noah’s Ark on such objects should not be relabeled as Bode’s instrument.

## People, events, and works

### Navigation and explorers

Sailors and explorers used the real log line extensively during the early-modern period. The device was integral to dead reckoning: compass heading, elapsed time, estimated leeway, and speed by log supplied a running position between astronomical fixes.

No explorer is known to have navigated by “the constellation Lochium Funis.” Its stars are faint and southern; Bode’s pattern was never a standard navigational asterism. Claims that mariners “steered by Lochium Funis” confuse the celestial picture with the physical instrument it commemorates.

### Bode

Johann Elert Bode (1747–1826), director of the Berlin Observatory, compiled one of the largest and most elaborate pictorial atlases ever printed. Its more than 17,000 plotted stars crowded traditional figures with new telescopic data and recently invented constellations. *Lochium Funis*, *Machina Electrica*, and *Officina Typographica* were among Bode’s own additions; *Felis* and *Globus Aerostaticus* arose from Lalande’s suggestions.

### Discoverers of objects in the modern area

The significant discoveries attached to modern Pyxis—T Pyxidis, NGC objects catalogued through Herschel-era and Dreyer cataloguing, and the late-discovered Pyxis globular cluster—postdate Bode. None was named for his log line.

No major ship, spacecraft, astronomical mission, composition, building, or canonical literary work was found bearing the name **Lochium Funis**. Modern online games and fan catalogues occasionally reuse obsolete constellation names; these constitute cultural afterlives, not historical continuity.

## Controversies and disputes

### Was it “abolished by the IAU in 1922”?

**Popular shorthand, technically imprecise.** The IAU adopted 88 constellations and their abbreviations at Rome. It did not need to abolish every omitted historical figure individually. Delporte’s boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in 1930, not created at Rome in 1922.

### Was 1930 the year of abolition?

Also shorthand. By 1899 Allen already called the constellation “entirely fallen into disuse.” The 1930 system made its exclusion geometrically final but did not cause the initial disappearance.

### Was Bode the only astronomer to use it?

**Substantially true for the precise figure; literally overstated.** Bode appears to be the only major uranographer who clearly drew and catalogued the complete figure under the name *Lochium Funis*. Later authors mentioned it historically; Bowen drew *Linea Nautica*; Smyth may have listed the name in text. Therefore “never adopted by another major working atlas” is safer than “never used by anyone else.”

### Was it independent of Pyxis?

**Disputed classification.** Many modern lists call it a separate obsolete constellation. Barentine and the primary layout show that Bode enclosed and catalogued it with Pyxis. It can consequently be described as:

- a new constellation attached to Pyxis;
- an asterism elaborating Pyxis;
- or the second component of a compound constellation.

The 1801 typography supplies a separate noun phrase, but the common boundary and joint catalogue favor the compound interpretation.

### What stars belonged to it?

**Partly unresolved.** ε and λ Pyxidis are securely associated with the wooden log. The rope passes through numerous faint plotted stars. Published summaries that assign all ten Greek-letter Pyxis stars to the log confuse the log with the compass; summaries that say only ε and λ formed the entire constellation ignore the rope’s many stellar knots.

### Did Lacaille divide Argo and create Pyxis from its mast?

**Oversimplified and disputed.** Lacaille reorganized Argo’s nomenclature and introduced Pyxis near the former mast area, but Pyxis had its own Bayer-letter sequence. It was not treated simply as a fourth surviving subdivision of Argo. The later proposal to call it *Malus* was an attempt to restore a mast, not proof that Lacaille’s compass was historically the Greek mast.

### Did ancient Greeks recognize “the four main Pyxis stars” as Argo’s mast?

This is often repeated in modern summaries but should be treated as **scholarly reconstruction**, not a quotation from an ancient source. Ancient catalogues describe stars of Argo and its mast without modern Pyxis designations. Precession and catalogue identification allow comparisons, but an exact one-to-one four-star statement requires a published concordance.

### Etymology and spelling

- *Lochium* is not ordinary classical Latin.
- *Funis* alone means the line; therefore translations such as “the rope and the line” are tautological mistakes.
- Spanish pages sometimes render the name “la cuerda y la línea,” which loses the wooden log.
- Italian *solcometro* accurately denotes the speed log; *sagola* is the attached small rope.
- *Pixis Nautica* is Lacaille’s printed spelling; **Pyxis** is the later normalized form.
- “Loc” and “Lochii Funis” in modern lists are reconstructed conveniences, not IAU forms.

### Bode’s “830 stars in 109 groups”

The supplied “Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be traced to Bode’s *Uranographia* or its catalogue. The documented catalogue contains **17,240** stellar and nonstellar entries, while modern descriptions vary over whether Bode illustrated 99, 103, or more than 100 constellation figures, depending on how compounds and subdivisions are counted. The figures 830 and 109 may belong to a modern database, collection, or exhibition metadata scheme, not to Bode’s printed accounting. They should not be used as historical facts without a specific source.

### Hoaxes and popular errors

No dedicated hoax concerning *Lochium Funis* was found. The principal misinformation consists of cumulative copying:

- assigning it an official abbreviation;
- stating that the IAU voted separately to abolish it;
- treating it as ancient Argonautic equipment;
- giving it Arabic or Greek names;
- saying Schiller replaced it with Noah’s Ark;
- confusing *funis* with the wooden log;
- asserting that all current Pyxis stars were its stars;
- claiming there are no Pyxidid meteor radiants;
- or treating Bowen’s *Linea Nautica* as continuous official survival.

## Open questions

1. **Complete star concordance.** A plate-level astrometric solution comparing every engraved knot with Bode’s 1800 coordinates and Gaia positions would be needed to identify the full rope securely.
2. **Exact catalogue wording.** A diplomatic transcription of the combined *Pixis Nautica et Lochium Funis* section in Bode’s bilingual companion volume would clarify whether he explicitly called the two one constellation.
3. **Later Bode spelling.** The reported “Compas Logleine” in the 1817–1820 *Nachtrag* requires verification against a clean primary scan.
4. **Smyth reception.** The purported 1844 *Bedford Catalogue* listing should be located by volume and page before being treated as an adoption.
5. **Bowen’s source.** Bowen’s *Linea Nautica* is almost certainly related to Bode’s figure, but a preface or acknowledged cartographic source would establish whether she consciously renamed it.
6. **“Shortest-lived” comparison.** Establishing this superlative would require consistent definitions of invention, adoption, and last cartographic appearance for all Bode novelties.
7. **Bode plate boundary.** Because pictorial enclosures are not coordinate polygons, different tracing conventions will allocate marginal rope stars differently among modern Pyxis, Hydra, Antlia, Puppis, and Vela.
