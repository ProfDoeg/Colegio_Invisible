# Circinus: Research Dossier

> **Status: kept.** Circinus is one of the 88 constellations recognized by the International Astronomical Union. Its abbreviation is **Cir**, its Latin genitive **Circini**, and its official boundaries are Eugène Delporte’s fourteen-segment polygon, adopted by the IAU in 1928 and published in 1930. It was not abolished and its principal stars remain in Circinus.

## Method and evidentiary labels

- **Documented fact** — directly attested in a historical text, chart, institutional record, or scientific publication.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an interpretation supported by comparison of historical charts, catalogues, or cultural evidence.
- **Tradition** — a culturally transmitted identification recorded by ethnographers or historical astronomers.
- **Disputed** — competing identifications or interpretations exist.
- **Unverified claim** — repeated assertion for which no adequate primary or scholarly evidence was located.
- **Legend/myth** — narrative not asserted here as historical fact.
- **Modern invention** — a recent symbolic, commercial, literary, or popular association rather than inherited constellation lore.

Dates refer to publication unless an observation date is separately given.

### Etymology

**Documented fact.** Latin *circinus* means a drawing compass or pair of dividers. It is related to *circus*, “circle,” and ultimately to the Indo-European family underlying Latin *circulus*. The regular second-declension genitive is *Circini*. [American Heritage Dictionary](https://ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=+CIRCINUS), [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/circinus_%28Dizionario-delle-Scienze-Fisiche%29/)

**Documented fact.** German *Zirkel* passed through Old High German *circil* and Middle High German *zirkel*, under the influence of Latin *circinus/circulus*. [Duden](https://www.duden.de/rechtschreibung/Zirkel)

**Important distinction.** Here “compass” means the hinged drafting or measuring instrument, not a magnetic mariner’s compass. The latter is represented by **Pyxis**, Lacaille’s *la Boussole*, later *Pyxis Nautica*. Confusing Circinus and Pyxis is the most persistent popular error concerning the figure.

### What the original figure represented

**Documented fact.** On Lacaille’s 1756 southern planisphere it is *le Compas*: an opened pair of drawing compasses. It stands beside:

- *l’Équerre et la Règle*, the set square and ruler, later Norma;
- *le Triangle Austral ou le Niveau*, the Southern Triangle adapted as a surveyor’s level.

Together they form a draftsman’s or surveyor’s instrument set. The visual and spatial grouping is explicit in Lacaille’s plate and was preserved by Bode. [Lacaille catalogue and map study](https://pbarbier.com/lacaille/lacaille.html), [Lacaille maps](https://pbarbier.com/lacaille/lacaille-maps.html), [Bode/Lacaille instrument-group discussion](https://ung.si/documents/3293/AstronomiVKmici-27.pdf)

**Scholarly reconstruction.** It is reasonable to connect this group with Lacaille’s work as astronomer, cartographer, geodesist, and surveyor. No statement by Lacaille has been located saying that Circinus honored a named instrument-maker, patron, king, voyage, or specific physical compass owned by him. Claims that it was a personal memorial to his own tool are interpretive rather than documentary.

### Boundaries

**Documented fact.** Delporte’s official Circinus is a fourteen-segment polygon based on right ascension and declination for equinox B1875.0. In modern coordinates its approximate span is:

- right ascension: about **13h 38m to 15h 30m**;
- declination: about **−55.4° to −70.6°**.

Its boundaries were designed to partition the entire celestial sphere and, where possible, preserve established variable-star designations. The IAU approved the general boundary system at Leiden in 1928; Delporte published the tables and atlas in 1930. [IAU history](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), [Delporte, *Délimitation scientifique des constellations*](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

### Boundary history and neighboring constellations

**Ptolemy, second century CE — documented absence.** Circinus did not exist. Most of its present field lies too far south for reliable observation from Alexandria. It was neither a Ptolemaic constellation nor an “unformed” appendage bearing a compass identity.

**Bayer, 1603 — documented absence as Circinus.** Bayer’s *Uranometria* included the Dutch southern constellations derived from the voyages of Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederik de Houtman, including Triangulum Australe, Apus, and Musca, but not Circinus. Stars in the later Circinus field were left in the spaces among Centaurus, Musca, Apus, and Triangulum Australe or were not distinctly catalogued. The modern Circinus letters are therefore not Bayer’s own assignments despite being called “Bayer designations.”

**Schiller, 1627 — documented absence.** Julius Schiller’s Christian sky preceded Circinus by more than a century. He could not replace it with a saint or biblical object. His southern plates Christianized the constellations then known, principally Bayer’s set. [Schiller facsimile record](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720), [Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/12-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum/)

**Hevelius, 1690 — documented absence.** Hevelius reproduced Triangulum Australe and the established Dutch southern figures but did not invent or chart Circinus as an independent constellation.

**Lacaille, 1756/1763 — documented creation.** Lacaille carved the compass from a previously unnamed interstitial field beside Centaurus and the Southern Triangle. His chart assigned eight Greek letters, α through θ.

**Bode, 1782 and 1801 — documented reception.** Bode accepted Lacaille’s compass with essentially its existing identity. He did not take it from another modern inventor, and Circinus was not one of Bode’s own new constellations.

**Gould, 1877 — documented catalogue expansion.** Benjamin Apthorp Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* catalogued **48 stars through seventh magnitude** in Circinus. Gould’s constellation assignments helped stabilize the southern sky before Delporte. [R. H. Allen’s Circinus entry](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Circinus%2A.html)

**Delporte, 1930 — documented fixation.** The pictorial compass became a bounded region. No major bright star traditionally called α–θ Circini was transferred to a neighbor. Numerous faint catalogue objects became “in Circinus” solely because they lay inside the polygon.

### Did Circinus take or lose stars?

**Documented/scholarly reconstruction.**

- It took its original stars from an unassigned gap in the early-modern southern sky, not through a formally recorded partition of a Ptolemaic figure.
- No well-established Circinus star comparable to the displaced stars of Norma or Telescopium was lost at Delporte’s settlement.
- α through θ Circini remain inside the official constellation.
- A faint star, HD 136003, appears as **ν Circini** in Bode’s *Uranographia*, but that letter never became a widely used modern Bayer designation. This is an atlas-label survival, not evidence that modern Circinus lost ν to another constellation. [Historical-star list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stars_in_Circinus)
- There are no genuine Flamsteed numbers in Circinus: Flamsteed observed from England and his catalogue did not cover the far-southern Lacaille constellations. [Flamsteed-designation history](https://www.ianridpath.com/startales/flamsteed.html), [designation overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flamsteed_designation)

## Stars and objects

## The original and principal stars

Lacaille assigned eight Greek letters. Approximate modern data follow; magnitudes and classifications vary slightly among catalogues.

| Star | Visual magnitude | Modern description | Role or interest |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **α Cir — Xami** | 3.18–3.21 | chemically peculiar A star, A7Vp SrCrEu; visual companion | hinge or apex of the compass; brightest roAp star |
| **β Cir** | 4.07 | A3 V star | one end of the narrow main figure; has a very wide L-dwarf companion |
| **γ Cir** | 4.48 | close B-type plus F-type visual/spectroscopic system | other end of the main figure |
| **ε Cir** | 4.85 | K giant | secondary outline star |
| **δ Cir** | about 5.04 | massive O-type hierarchical triple containing an eclipsing binary | young massive system |
| **θ Cir** | about 5.08 | B-type emission-line variable | γ-Cassiopeiae-type variability |
| **η Cir** | about 5.16 | G giant | secondary outline star |
| **ζ Cir** | about 6.09 | early-type star | faintest of Lacaille’s canonical eight |

A current observational table with positions for α–θ is reproduced in the [Guide to Observing Deep-Sky Objects](https://naedine.astrolab.ru/sites/default/files/books/farinacci.pdf).

### α Circini and Xami

**Documented fact.** α Circini is HR 5463, HD 128898, HIP 71908. It lies about 54 light-years away and is the brightest known rapidly oscillating Ap star visible in the night sky. Its dominant pulsation is approximately 6.8 minutes; its rotational modulation is about 4.48 days. It has a much fainter K-dwarf visual companion. [Bruntt et al., 2009](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/396/2/1189/1030214), [Bruntt et al. preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.3967)

**Documented fact.** The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Xami** for α Circini on 12 December 2024. This is therefore an extremely recent official proper name, not a name found in Lacaille, Bode, Arabic star catalogues, or classical sources. [IAU announcement](https://iau.org/Iau/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx)

**Tradition and modern naming decision.** *Xami* derives from the /Xam or related Khoekhoe/San tradition of a beast or lion whose eyes were α and β Centauri. If the Centauri pair forms the eyes, α Circini falls within a reconstructed facial area. The exact outline of the indigenous figure is not preserved. Assigning its name specifically to α Circini was an IAU cultural-naming decision, not evidence that the star itself bore that individual name historically. [All Skies Encyclopaedia/WGSN documentation](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Xami)

**Catalogue error.** One current WGSN constellation profile displays HIP **71906**, whereas the IAU adoption announcement and astronomical catalogues give HIP **71908**. The latter is the supported identification. [WGSN Circinus profile](https://exopla.net/circinus), [IAU announcement](https://iau.org/Iau/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx)

### β Circini

**Documented fact.** β Circini, HD 135379/HIP 74824, is an A3 main-sequence star of magnitude about 4.07. In 2015 investigators identified a very wide L-dwarf/brown-dwarf companion at a projected separation near 6,656 au. [Smith et al., discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.09226)

It has no traditional Arabic, Greek, or Latin proper name.

### γ Circini

**Documented fact.** γ Circini, HD 136415/HIP 75323, is a close double system of combined magnitude about 4.48. Its brighter component is an early B star; the fainter component is approximately F-type. It has no inherited proper name.

### δ Circini

**Documented fact.** δ Circini is a massive hierarchical triple. Its inner O-type eclipsing binary has a period of roughly 3.9 days and is accompanied by a more distant massive tertiary. Modern orbital solutions continue to be refined. It should not be described simply as a single “Algol star.”

### α–β–γ asterism

**Modern convention.** α, β, and γ form a long, narrow triangle. In line drawings α is normally the hinge, with the two arms spreading toward β and γ. This is the only broadly recognizable asterism intrinsic to Circinus. It has no separately standardized name.

### Flamsteed and Gould numbers

- **Flamsteed numbers:** none.
- **Gould designations:** Gould numbered southern stars in Circinus in increasing right ascension, but those numbers are now rarely used.
- **Greek letters:** α, β, γ, δ, ε, ζ, η, θ are Lacaille assignments, often generically called Bayer letters.
- **Bode-only or weakly received letters:** ν Circini for HD 136003 is attested on Bode’s chart but did not become standard.

## Variables and unusual stars

### α Circini

Already discussed: the brightest roAp star, with minute-scale non-radial pulsations. The observational discovery of its rapid variability dates to work by Kurtz and Cropper in 1981 and the emerging roAp class described by Kurtz in 1982. This is a modern astrophysical classification, unrelated to the compass figure.

### AX Circini

**Documented fact.** AX Cir is a classical Cepheid whose visual brightness varies approximately from magnitude 5.6 to 6.1 in a 5.27-day cycle. Walter Strohmeier announced its variability in 1964. It is a binary; long-baseline interferometry directly detected its companion. [Gallenne et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.1950)

### BX Circini

**Documented fact.** BX Cir is a hot, hydrogen-deficient extreme-helium pulsator of about magnitude 12.6. Its short-period variability was discovered in the 1990s. It has been proposed, with V652 Herculis, as a prototype of a rare pulsational group. Evolution through the merger of two helium white dwarfs is a scientific model, not directly observed history.

### δ Circini

An eclipsing, massive multiple system; its light variation is orbital.

### θ Circini

A B-emission or γ-Cassiopeiae-type variable with low-amplitude changes.

### AE Circini

**Documented fact.** AE Cir is a symbiotic star. Spectroscopic work established a cool approximately K5 donor and emission features characteristic of the class. [Mennickent et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.3180)

### BW Circini

A low-mass X-ray binary and black-hole candidate, also known as GS 1354−64. Its distance and quiescent behavior have been subjects of continuing research.

### Massive and obscured stars

Because the Milky Way crosses Circinus, the small official region contains numerous early-type, emission-line, Wolf–Rayet, and heavily reddened stars. The reported number depends strongly on the catalogue epoch and detection wavelength; it should not be frozen as a timeless count. [Roman-Lopes, Wolf–Rayet discoveries](https://arxiv.org/abs/1010.1172)

## Circinus X-1

**Documented fact.** Circinus X-1 is a neutron-star X-ray binary, not a star in the visual compass outline. The earliest unambiguous detection came from an Aerobee rocket flight on **14 June 1969** and was published by Margon and collaborators in 1971 as **Circinus XR-1**. “X-1” means the first catalogued X-ray source in the constellation, not “star X” or an aircraft reference. [NASA technical history](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19840003015/downloads/19840003015.pdf)

The source is highly variable, with an eccentric orbit of about 16.6 days, radio flares, relativistic jets, and an associated radio/X-ray nebula. It has at different times shown properties resembling both Z sources and young supernova-remnant systems. Its age and exact relation to a surrounding remnant have been debated. [Tudose et al., radio nebula](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0607612), [complete Z track](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/9901003)

## Deep-sky objects

### Messier objects

**Documented fact.** There are **no Messier objects** in Circinus. Messier’s Paris-based catalogue emphasized objects observable from northern mid-latitudes and did not survey this far-southern field comprehensively.

### NGC and IC objects within the Delporte polygon

Modern NGC/IC databases place five entries in Circinus:

| Object | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **NGC 5288** | open cluster | faint, about magnitude 11.8 |
| **NGC 5315** | planetary nebula | compact, high-surface-brightness object |
| **NGC 5359** | putative/open cluster | existence or physical reality less secure than the others |
| **NGC 5715** | open cluster | magnitude about 9.8 |
| **NGC 5823 / Caldwell 88** | open cluster | brightest major cluster in Circinus |

[Revised NGC/IC constellation listing](https://deepskycorner.ch/catalogs.en.php?cat=NGCIC&const=Cir), [NGC/IC summary](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/circinus-deepsky-objects)

#### NGC 5823

**Documented fact.** James Dunlop observed the cluster on 8 May 1826; John Herschel independently observed it in 1836. Dreyer’s NGC description calls it considerably large and rich, with stars chiefly of 13th–14th magnitude. Its historical identity is secure. [Historical NGC analysis](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/ngc58.htm)

It is sometimes described visually as S-shaped, reversed-S, box-like, or tulip-like. Those are observational impressions, not historical constellation figures.

**Identification pitfall.** NGC 5823 is sometimes confused with NGC 5822 in neighboring Lupus. Their similar numbers and proximity cause the error; they are distinct clusters.

#### NGC 5715

A smaller and fainter open cluster, approximately seven arcminutes across and around magnitude 9.8. Modern photometric work supports a real cluster, though membership lists change with Gaia astrometry.

#### NGC 5288

A faint open cluster whose membership and dynamical state have been re-examined with Gaia DR3. [Gaia study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10099)

#### NGC 5359

**Disputed/uncertain.** It entered the NGC as a very large, slightly rich cluster, but modern catalogues sometimes mark it “cluster?” Its reality as a gravitationally coherent open cluster deserves caution.

#### NGC 5315

A compact planetary nebula discovered during John Herschel’s southern survey. It is about magnitude 9.8 integrated, with a much fainter central star. High magnification is needed to distinguish its small disk.

### Pismis 20

A small, compact open cluster identified in Paris Pişmiş’s twentieth-century southern-cluster work. It is not an NGC object and should not be confused with the far more massive obscured cluster Pismis 24 in Scorpius.

### Bernes 145

A combined dark/reflection-nebula entry from Bernes’s 1971 catalogue. Its inclusion in popular Circinus lists is modern and does not imply connection with the constellation’s name.

### MSH 15−52, RCW 89, and PSR B1509−58

**Documented fact.** PSR B1509−58 is an energetic young pulsar embedded in the supernova-remnant complex MSH 15−52. Chandra images of its pulsar-wind nebula are popularly called the “Hand of God.”

**Modern invention.** “Hand of God” is a media nickname based on the X-ray image’s apparent shape. It is neither an indigenous asterism nor a traditional Circinus myth.

## The Circinus Galaxy

**Documented fact.** The Circinus Galaxy, ESO 97-G13/PGC 50779, was recognized by Kenneth Freeman, Gösta Lyngå, Peter Atherton, and colleagues from an optical plate and radio work published in **1977**. It had escaped earlier prominence because it lies only a few degrees from the Galactic plane, behind heavy foreground dust and dense Milky Way star fields. [Jones et al., H I study and discovery history](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/302/4/649/1012596), [ESO Messenger, 1977](https://www.eso.org/sci/publications/messenger/archive/no.9-jun77/messenger-no9.pdf)

It is roughly 13 million light-years away and is among the nearest active/Seyfert galaxies. Its nucleus contains an accreting supermassive black hole, obscuring dust, star-forming rings, ionization cones, and outflows. “Circinus” is a location-name: it does not mean the galaxy is compass-shaped. [NASA Webb overview](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/webb/nasas-webb-delivers-unprecedented-look-into-heart-of-circinus-galaxy/), [Spitzer gas and star-formation study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4102)

## Novae and supernovae

### The guest star of CE 185 and RCW 86

**Documented ancient observation.** The *Hou Hanshu* records that in the second year of the Zhongping reign, tenth month—CE 185—a “guest star” appeared within **Nánmén**, remained visible for many months, and disappeared in the following year. The chronicle itself does not call the event a supernova or use the name Circinus. [Chinese text, *Hou Hanshu* 86](https://www.chinese-classics.org/read/history/hou-han-shu/hou-han-shu-086)

**Scholarly reconstruction.** RCW 86, now inside Circinus’s official boundaries, is widely identified as the remnant of that event. The association is supported by remnant age estimates and position near the Chinese Nánmén asterism, but historical positional language is not precise enough to make the match a literal catalogue identification. [Historical-remnant analysis](https://mail.tku.edu.tw/einmann/papers/sn185.html)

**Contested terminology.** Calling it “SN 185 in Circinus” is convenient modern shorthand. It must not be read as evidence that Han astronomers recognized Circinus, nor that the guest star was certainly assigned to a compass figure.

### Nova Circini 1926 — X Circini

**Documented fact.** Nova Circini 1926, variable-star designation **X Cir**, was recorded around magnitude 6.5 on 3 September 1926. It later faded with fluctuations near magnitudes 12–13. Old summaries sometimes call it one of “two twentieth-century novae,” but that count depends on catalogue definition and has become obsolete as surveys find more transients.

### FQ Circini

FQ Cir is a nova/cataclysmic system now receiving renewed study. A 2025 analysis proposed a massive B-type donor transferring material through a decretion disk. That proposed evolutionary category is new and should not be treated as settled historical nomenclature. [FQ Cir study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.16594)

## Meteor showers

### Alpha Circinids

**Documented but weakly established.** The Alpha Circinids, code **ACI**, were reported from a short outburst observed in 1977. Later searches have treated them as a possible recurrent minor shower rather than one of the secure major annual streams. [Meteor-section report](https://www.mnassa.org.za/html/Jun2012/2012MNASSA..71..Jun.pdf)

The name refers to a radiant near α Circini, not to meteoroids physically associated with that star.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1500: no Circinus constellation

**Babylonian and Mesopotamian astronomy — documented absence.** No Circinus-equivalent occurs in *MUL.APIN*, the Babylonian zodiacal tradition, or recognized Mesopotamian star lists. The present field is far south of their best-observed sky. No Babylonian name for α, β, or γ Circini was located.

**Egyptian astronomy — documented absence.** No secure decanal, monumental, coffin-lid, or zodiacal identification maps this star group to drawing compasses. Claims that “the Egyptians saw the compass and square here” conflate terrestrial symbolic tools with Lacaille’s eighteenth-century constellation.

**Greek and Roman astronomy — documented absence.** Neither Eudoxus/Aratus nor Hipparchus/Ptolemy includes the figure. It is absent from Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi*, the Epitome, Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica*, Germanicus and the Aratean scholia, Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, Pausanias, and the Latin mythographers.

**Arabic and Persian astronomy — documented absence.** Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābitah* follows the 48 Ptolemaic constellation figures; it contains no Circinus chapter, image, or inherited name. No Bedouin asterism securely identified specifically with α–γ Circini was located. Modern Arabic *al-bīkār* and Persian *pargār* are translations of the Western constellation, not al-Ṣūfī names.

### 1590s–1603: southern charts without Circinus

**Documented fact.** Keyser and de Houtman’s observations supplied twelve new southern constellations used by Plancius and Bayer. Triangulum Australe, Musca, and Apus were present; Circinus was not. Research on the early southern catalogues shows that other new stars were entered under older groups such as Centaurus and Lupus or left outside the new pictorial figures. [Verbunt and van Gent](https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.0946)

### 1627: Schiller

**Documented fact.** *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* replaced pagan figures then known with biblical ones. Since Circinus did not yet exist, it had no Schillerian saint, patriarch, or sacred object.

### 1690: Hevelius

**Documented fact.** Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* did not include Circinus. Assertions that Circinus was invented by Hevelius are unsupported.

### 1751–1752: observation at the Cape

**Documented fact.** Lacaille conducted his intensive southern-star survey from the Cape of Good Hope, principally during 1751–1752. He measured nearly 10,000 stars and observed numerous nebulous objects. [Astronomical Society of Southern Africa](https://assa.saao.ac.za/sections/deep-sky/lacailles-catalogue/)

### 1756: first named publication

**Documented fact.** The memoir and southern planisphere printed in the *Mémoires de l’Académie royale des sciences* for the year 1752—but actually published in 1756—give the first recoverable published identity: **le Compas**.

This date explains the competing statements “invented in 1752,” “1756,” and “1763”:

- 1751–1752: observational campaign;
- 1752: nominal Academy memoir year;
- 1756: actual publication of planisphere and French name;
- 1763: posthumous Latin catalogue and map.

### 1763: *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*

**Documented fact.** Lacaille died in 1762. His *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* appeared posthumously in Paris in 1763, edited from his observations. It uses **Circinus** and lists the constellation’s stars. [Digitized 1763 edition](https://books.google.com/books/about/Coelum_australe_stelliferum.html?id=LIo_AAAAcAAJ), [PDF facsimile](https://www.astromyth.ru/uploads/Library/LaCaille/CoelumAustraleStelliferum.pdf)

**Counting controversy.** Lacaille is often said to have invented fourteen constellations. That total can include Pyxis and the renamed/reworked Reticulum, while other countings distinguish thirteen wholly new figures or treat the Argo division separately. Circinus itself is not disputed.

### 1776 and 1795: Fortin’s Flamsteed atlas

**Documented fact.** Jean-Nicolas Fortin’s reduced French edition of Flamsteed’s atlas incorporated Lacaille’s far-southern chart. This does not mean Flamsteed himself drew or catalogued Circinus; Fortin and later editors supplemented Flamsteed with southern material.

### 1782: Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne*

**Documented fact.** Bode’s German enlargement of Fortin’s atlas received the Lacaille constellations. In this edition the new instrumental figures are labeled and outlined, often more schematically than in his later monumental atlas. [Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/further-out/06-bode-johann-elert/), [Heidelberg digital introduction](https://www.lsw.uni-heidelberg.de/foerderkreis/bode/einl.html)

The German vernacular label is **Zirkel**. Claims that Lacaille originally called it *Le Compas du Géomètre* occur in modern explanatory literature, but the securely visible label on the 1756 planisphere is simply *le Compas*.

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode’s *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* appeared in Berlin in 1801, accompanied by the bilingual German/French *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*. Its 20 large copperplates plot more than 17,000 stars and about 2,500 nebular objects. [Bode facsimile, ETH](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148), [Marseille record and facsimile](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035), [University of Oklahoma exhibit](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/map-heavens.html)

**Bode’s plate.** Circinus appears on **Plate XX**, the far-southern regional plate, beside *Norma et Regula* and *Triangulum Australe seu Libella*. It is drawn as an opened pair of metal compasses. Bode’s Latin label is **CIRCINUS**; German discussions call it **der Zirkel**. The plate preserves Lacaille’s intended surveyor’s set. [Plate-XX identification](https://astrocultura.uai.it/mitologia/antiche/costellazioniperdute3.htm), [Bode facsimile index/OCR](https://www.scribd.com/document/733251494/OAM-19-Bode-Uranographia)

**Documented conclusion.** Bode did not claim Circinus as his invention. In the atlas’s constellation index it belongs among the received southern figures. The inventions associated particularly with Bode’s 1801 atlas include Machina Electrica; Circinus is Lacaille’s.

**Untraced lead.** The phrase supplied in the brief, “Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups),” could not be matched to Bode’s title pages, catalogue statistics, institutional descriptions, or searchable scholarly literature. Bode’s accompanying catalogue states 17,240 stellar/nebular entries; atlas descriptions variously count slightly over one hundred depicted constellation figures. “830 stars in 109 groups” should not be repeated as a property of *Uranographia* without a precise collection citation.

### 1822: Jamieson

**Documented fact.** Alexander Jamieson included Circinus in *A Celestial Atlas* (London, 1822). Jamieson drew extensively on Bode, simplifying the scientific detail and emphasizing picturesque figures. [Jamieson atlas history](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=e88349382fdcfa294ff6ccf80007dbf2e9311237&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

### 1824–1825: *Urania’s Mirror*

**Documented absence.** *Urania’s Mirror* adapted many Jamieson figures but consisted of 32 cards directed chiefly to constellations visible from Britain. Circinus received no independent card. Statements that a particular *Urania’s Mirror* card depicts Circinus are unsupported.

### 1833 onward: Burritt

**Documented fact.** Elijah H. Burritt’s American *Atlas Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens* included a southern polar chart on which the received southern constellations, including Circinus, could be shown. It was a popular educational atlas, not a redefinition of Circinus. [Smithsonian catalogue](https://www.si.edu/object/elijah-h-burritt-atlas-designed-illustrate-geography-heavens%3Anmah_1823231), [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/resource/g3180m.gct00292/?sp=1&st=list)

### 1843–1872: Argelander and Heis

**Documented limitation.** Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* and Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* were northern naked-eye atlases. They influenced the selection and representation of northern constellations but are not evidence for Circinus’s southern boundary or iconography.

### 1877: Gould

**Documented fact.** Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* catalogued 48 Circinus stars through magnitude seven and helped standardize southern constellation membership.

### 1922: Rome

**Documented fact.** At its first General Assembly in Rome, the IAU accepted the standard constellation list and three-letter abbreviations proposed through Commission 3. Circinus was retained as **Cir**. Credit for the abbreviation system was shared by Henry Norris Russell and Ejnar Hertzsprung; Russell emphasized Hertzsprung’s priority for the suggestion. [Original-list discussion](https://www.ianridpath.com/iaulist1.html), [IAU archive](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### 1925–1930: Delporte

- 1925, Cambridge: Delporte presented the boundary principle.
- 1928, Leiden: the IAU approved the completed boundaries.
- 1930: *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* and *Atlas Céleste* appeared.

Circinus was kept, not dropped.

### 1977: galaxy and meteor shower

The Circinus Galaxy was published as a nearby obscured galaxy; a short Alpha Circinid meteor outburst was also reported during 1977. These are unrelated discoveries sharing only the constellation locator.

### 2024: Xami

The IAU assigned the official proper name Xami to α Circini, introducing a South African cultural name into the modern stellar nomenclature of the constellation.

## Invention and reception

### Inventor

**Documented fact:** Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille.

**When:** conceived during or immediately after the Cape survey of 1751–1752; first published by name in 1756; Latinized in 1763.

**Where:** observations at the Cape of Good Hope; publication in Paris.

**Medium:** Academy memoir and southern planisphere, followed by the posthumous catalogue *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*.

### Purpose

**Documented fact.** The figure is an instrument, one of Lacaille’s group representing “the principal instruments of the arts” or sciences.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Circinus, Norma, and Lacaille’s level-like treatment of Triangulum Australe form a coherent surveying/drafting ensemble. This likely reflects the mathematical and geodetic culture of Lacaille’s work.

**Unsupported elaborations.** No evidence was found that the compass:

- honored a specific king or patron;
- commemorated a named surveyor;
- represented a particular commercial model;
- memorialized a ship or maritime disaster;
- was created by Keyser, de Houtman, Plancius, Bayer, Hevelius, Lalande, Poczobut, Hell, Lemonnier, or Bode.

Those astronomers matter in the broader history of invented constellations, but not as rival creators of Circinus.

### Bode’s reception

Bode’s important contribution was diffusion. His 1782 and 1801 atlases carried Lacaille’s sparse southern instrument into German and international celestial cartography. His 1801 rendering makes the relation among compass, square/rule, and level especially legible.

### Nineteenth-century acceptance

Unlike many patronal and technological constellations—Globus Aerostaticus, Machina Electrica, Officina Typographica, Honores Friderici—Circinus was consistently useful as a compact name for a previously unallocated southern field. Gould’s catalogue and variable-star nomenclature further entrenched it. It therefore survived the IAU’s reduction to 88.

## Myths and legends

## The absence of a Circinus catasterism

**Documented fact.** Circinus has no ancient Greek or Roman catasterism. Consequently:

- no god placed it in the sky;
- Eratosthenes gives no identity or variant;
- Hyginus gives no astronomical Circinus story;
- the Aratean scholia and Germanicus do not explain it;
- Ovid and Manilius do not treat it as a constellation;
- Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, and Pausanias supply no Circinus myth.

Any page offering a Greek “myth of the Circinus constellation” is a modern fabrication or a confusion between the constellation and myths about the invention of terrestrial compasses.

## Perdix/Talos and the invention of compasses

This is a myth attached to the **instrument**, not to Lacaille’s stars.

**Myth, earliest surviving narrative family: fifth–fourth century BCE and later.** Daedalus’s gifted nephew is variously named Talos, Perdix, or Calos. He invents carpentry tools, provoking Daedalus’s jealousy.

- **Diodorus Siculus, *Library of History* 4.76.4–7** (first century BCE) calls the youth Talos and credits him with inventions including the saw and turning tools; Daedalus kills him from jealousy.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 8.236–259** (c. CE 8) gives the nephew no personal name in the immediate narrative but describes his imitation of a fish’s backbone to make the saw and the joining of two iron arms so that one stands fixed while the other traces a circle: an unmistakable drawing compass.
- **Pseudo-Hyginus, *Fabulae* 39 and 274** (text compiled in the early imperial period) calls him Perdix in transmitted versions and credits him with the saw; manuscript traditions vary.
- **Apollodoran and later mythographic traditions** preserve variants in names and kinship.

Athena/Minerva changes the falling youth into a partridge in Ovid; the bird avoids heights in memory of the fall. This story was never connected by an ancient source with a constellation called Circinus.

**Controversy.** Reference works sometimes conflate Perdix the nephew with Perdix the sister/mother, or merge Talos the inventor with Talos the bronze guardian of Crete. These are distinct but entangled mythographic traditions. None provides a catasterism for Circinus.

## “God the Geometer”

**Medieval Christian iconographic tradition, not constellation lore.** Medieval manuscripts, notably thirteenth-century *Bibles moralisées*, portray God creating the world with compasses. The image expresses mathematical creation, Wisdom, and cosmic order. It predates Lacaille by centuries but was not mapped onto these stars.

## Freemasonry, square, and compasses

**Modern symbolic comparison.** Because Norma is a square and Circinus a compass, modern writers sometimes call the pair a celestial Masonic emblem. The resemblance is real; evidence that Lacaille intended a Freemason reference was not located. Lacaille’s inclusion of Triangulum Australe as a level favors the more straightforward explanation of a surveyor’s set.

## Alchemical and Hermetic readings

**Unverified as historical constellation tradition.** Compasses, circles, the point and circumference, and geometric construction occur in Hermetic, alchemical, architectural, and esoteric symbolism. No pre-nineteenth-century alchemical or Hermetic text was found that identifies α–γ Circini with such symbolism. Modern esoteric treatments are retrospective readings of Lacaille’s figure.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Babylonian

**Finding: no traceable equivalent.** The field falls outside the principal Babylonian constellation tradition. No Babylonian names for α–γ Circini were located.

## Egyptian

**Finding: no secure equivalent.** No decan or pharaonic figure can be reliably equated with the compass stars. Comparisons with Egyptian measuring cords or divine architects are symbolic analogies only.

## Greek and Latin

**Finding: no stellar figure.** Latin *circinus* was an ordinary instrument word. Greek terms included **διαβήτης** (*diabētēs*, divider/compass) and **τόρνος** (*tornos*, turning or circular instrument), but neither designated this constellation in antiquity.

## Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

**Finding: no al-Ṣūfī Circinus.** Al-Ṣūfī catalogued the Ptolemaic constellation figures. The field was not an Arabic compass constellation and its principal stars have no established al-Ṣūfī proper names.

Modern Arabic **البيكار**, **البركار**, or **الفرجار** are translations of the international Western constellation. [International Astronomical Center terminology](https://astronomycenter.net/cons.html), [Arabic technical encyclopedia](https://arab-ency.com.sy/scitech/details/169699)

## Bedouin asterisms

**Finding: none securely identified.** No documented Bedouin asterism consisting specifically of α, β, and γ Circini was found. General claims that “all southern stars had Arabic names” are incorrect; much of this field lay very low or below the horizon from major medieval Islamic observatories.

## Persian

**Modern reception.** Persian **پرگار**, *pargār*, is a translation of the drawing compass and the approved modern astronomical name. It is not attested as a classical Persian asterism. [Persian Academy terminology](https://vajehyab.com/farhangestan/%D9%BE%D8%B1%DA%AF%D8%A7%D8%B1?q=%D9%BE%D8%B1%DA%AF%D8%A7%D8%B1+%D8%AA%D9%86%DA%AF)

## Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

**Modern Hebrew.** **מחוגה**, *meḥugah*, means a drawing compass. It is a modern translation of Circinus. [Hebrew constellation list](https://www.leskoff.com/s02404-0)

**Finding: no ancient Hebrew or Kabbalistic mapping.** Circinus does not correspond to an identifiable biblical *mazzarah*, rabbinic constellation, Sefer Yetzirah zodiacal mapping, or inherited Kabbalistic star figure. Assertions linking it to Solomon, the Temple architect, or the compass-and-square are modern invention unless accompanied by a specific post-Lacaille text.

## Chinese

### Nánmén

**Tradition/documented historical astronomy.** **南門**, *Nánmén*, “Southern Gate,” is an ancient Chinese asterism centered on α and β Centauri. The CE 185 guest star was recorded “within Nánmén.”

### α Circini as an added star

**Documented later catalogue convention.** α Circini is called **南門增二**, *Nánmén zēng èr*, “Second Added Star of Southern Gate,” in later Chinese extension nomenclature. “Added star” (*zēng*) ordinarily indicates incorporation during the encounter between traditional Chinese uranography and fuller Western catalogues. It is not proof that α Circini belonged to the earliest Nánmén. [Chinese-astronomy cross-identification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circinus_in_Chinese_astronomy)

### Modern Chinese Circinus

**Modern translation.** 圓規座/圆规座, *Yuánguī-zuò*, literally “drawing-compass constellation.”

### Fuxi and Nüwa

**Contested popular analogy.** Chinese and Central Asian images sometimes show Fuxi and Nüwa holding square and compass-like instruments. Modern constellation websites juxtapose them with Norma and Circinus. No traditional Chinese star map identifies α–γ Circini as the compasses of Fuxi or Nüwa. The comparison is iconographic, not an inherited asterism.

## Indian

**Finding: no nakshatra.** Circinus is far from the ecliptic and lies in no lunar mansion. No Sanskrit or regional Indian asterism specifically matching α–γ Circini was located in the sources consulted. Modern Indian-language names are translations or transliterations of the IAU constellation.

## Indigenous southern African traditions

### Xami

**Tradition with incomplete geometry.** Khoekhoe/San material records a great beast or lion associated with the α–β Centauri region. Modern South African cultural-astronomy work reconstructed those two stars as its eyes. α Circini lies in the implied face, producing the 2024 IAU name Xami.

The surviving evidence does not establish:

- an exact boundary;
- that α Circini alone was called Xami;
- that the beast was mapped identically by all Khoekhoe and San speakers;
- any connection with Lacaille’s compass.

### Southern African reception of Lacaille

**Historical context.** Lacaille worked in a colonial Cape environment where European, Khoekhoe, enslaved, and imported communities coexisted. No evidence was found that he recorded local names for the Circinus stars or incorporated local astronomy into *le Compas*.

## South American indigenous traditions

Some South American traditions recognize large rhea or ñandú figures across the southern Milky Way and the Centaurus–Crux region. A modern summary says a Great Rhea can encompass parts of present Centaurus and Circinus. Exact inclusion varies among communities; it is unsafe to assign α–γ Circini without a community-specific source.

## Astrology

### Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*

**Documented absence.** Ptolemy’s fixed-star effects concern ancient constellations and selected bright stars. Circinus did not exist and is not listed. Retroactively attributing “Mercury–Mars” or similar planetary natures to Circinus is modern astrological extrapolation.

### Behenian stars

**Documented absence.** None of the fifteen traditional Behenian fixed stars lies in Circinus.

### Lunar mansions

**Documented absence.** Circinus is remote from the ecliptic. No Arabic *manzil*, Indian *nakshatra*, Chinese lunar mansion determinative, or European lunar mansion crosses it in the ordinary positional sense. Its placement by some modern Chinese tables within the broad eastern Azure Dragon sector is not equivalent to lying on the lunar path.

### Modern constellation astrology

**Modern invention.** Websites assign Circinus themes of measurement, precision, geometry, navigation, justice, or “drawing boundaries.” These are transparent deductions from the instrument and have no ancient textual pedigree.

### Farnese Atlas

**Documented absence.** The Farnese Atlas preserves the Greco-Roman constellation sphere. It cannot depict Circinus. Likewise, no Circinus exists on these ancient celestial globes.

## Dürer, 1515

**Documented absence.** Albrecht Dürer’s northern and southern celestial maps derive from classical constellation material and precede both the Dutch southern figures and Lacaille. Circinus is absent.

## Bayer, 1603

**Documented absence.** Bayer depicts Triangulum Australe, Apus, and Musca but no compass in this gap.

## Schiller, 1627

**Documented absence.** No Christian substitute, because no Circinus yet existed.

## Hevelius, 1690

**Documented absence.** Hevelius depicts the already established Southern Triangle but no Circinus.

**First iconography.** The published southern planisphere depicts *le Compas* as an open hinged instrument. Its placement makes it one member of the square–compass–level group.

The posthumous chart retains the instrument under the Latin label *Circinus*.

## Bode, 1782

The *Vorstellung der Gestirne* labels and outlines the Lacaille figure in a German adaptation of Fortin.

**Major iconographic witness.** Plate XX of *Uranographia* shows a realistically engraved pair of compasses alongside *Norma et Regula* and *Triangulum Australe seu Libella*. Jamieson received the compass in his Bode-derived atlas. Its style is simplified and decorative, part of the transition from scientific copperplate atlas to popular celestial picture book.

## Burritt, 1833 and later editions

Circinus belongs on Burritt’s southern polar map. Burritt’s educational use helped naturalize Lacaille’s technical instruments for English-speaking readers.

## *Urania’s Mirror*, 1824/1825

No dedicated Circinus card has been verified.

## Argelander and Heis

Their northern coverage prevents them from being meaningful Circinus iconographic witnesses.

## Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

**Finding.** No securely documented ancient coin, cathedral portal, Renaissance ceiling, or medieval calendar labels this star field as Circinus. Compasses occur abundantly in art—God as architect, personifications of geometry, architects, surveyors, Freemasonry—but those images are not depictions of the constellation unless accompanied by an astronomical label or recognizable chart.

## People, events, and works

### Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille

Circinus’s central historical figure. His Cape campaign transformed the southern sky into a dense positional catalogue and supplied instrument constellations that embodied eighteenth-century observational science.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode internationalized the figure through his German atlas of 1782 and monumental *Uranographia* of 1801. His role is receiver, illustrator, cataloguer, and popularizer—not inventor.

### Benjamin Apthorp Gould

Gould’s Argentine survey converted pictorial southern regions into dense, systematic naked-eye catalogues, recording 48 Circinus stars through seventh magnitude.

### James Dunlop

From Parramatta, New South Wales, Dunlop discovered or first recorded important southern clusters, including NGC 5823 in 1826.

### John Herschel

His Cape survey supplied observations that entered the General Catalogue and later the NGC, including Circinus clusters and NGC 5315.

### Circinus Galaxy discoverers

Freeman, Lyngå, Atherton, and collaborators recognized the obscured galaxy in 1977. Subsequent teams established its nearby Seyfert nucleus, masers, rings, warped H I disk, outflows, and circumnuclear dust.

### Circinus X-1 observers

The source’s history runs from sounding-rocket astronomy through Uhuru, radio interferometry, Chandra, XMM-Newton, and later X-ray missions. It became a test case for neutron-star accretion, relativistic jets, and the relation between young binaries and supernova remnants.

### Navigation

**Negative finding.** No documented explorer or voyage was found that steered specifically by α, β, or γ Circini. The stars are faint and lie beside the vastly more useful α and β Centauri and Crux. Claims that Circinus was “used by ancient navigators” usually confuse:

1. the constellation with the physical dividers used on charts;
2. Circinus with Pyxis;
3. its location with the navigational use of neighboring Centaurus/Crux.

### Ships and missions

**Negative finding.** No historically important naval ship or space mission securely named for the Circinus constellation was located. “Circinus” appears in scientific project names, software, fictional settings, and biological taxonomy, but those cannot automatically be traced to the constellation.

### Literature, music, visual art, buildings

**Finding.** Circinus has not accumulated a canonical literary and artistic tradition comparable to Orion or the classical zodiac. Modern songs, albums, companies, games, and fictional worlds occasionally use the Latin word. Unless the work explicitly invokes the southern constellation, the title may instead refer to geometry, a biological genus, or a fictional place.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Drawing compass versus magnetic compass

**Status: documented error.** Circinus is the drawing compass; Pyxis is the mariner’s compass. French and Spanish *compas/compás* can mean either according to context, encouraging mistranslation. English popular pages that call Circinus “the navigator’s compass” without specifying dividers blur the distinction.

## 2. 1752, 1754, 1756, or 1763?

**Status: resolved bibliographic ambiguity.**

- 1751–1752: observations;
- 1752: memoir volume’s nominal year;
- 1754: Lacaille returned from the expedition; an unnamed or preparatory drawing is sometimes dated here;
- 1756: actual printed French planisphere and first named publication;
- 1763: Latin catalogue.

Thus “introduced in 1756” is the clearest publication statement.

## 3. “Le Compas” versus “Le Compas du Géomètre”

**Status: partly disputed wording.** The primary chart supports *le Compas*. *Le Compas du Géomètre* is a later explanatory expansion used to distinguish it from a magnetic compass. It accurately describes the object but should not be quoted as Lacaille’s chart label without a separate primary citation.

## 4. “Fourteen constellations”

**Status: counting convention.** Popular histories routinely say Lacaille invented fourteen. The total changes if one treats Reticulum as a renaming of Rhombus, Pyxis as a restructuring of Argo, or the Argo subdivisions separately. Circinus’s authorship is unaffected.

## 5. Bayer letters that are not Bayer’s

**Status: nomenclatural convention.** α–θ Circini were assigned by Lacaille in the eighteenth century. Calling them “Bayer designations” describes the Greek-letter system, not personal authorship by Johann Bayer.

## 6. Flamsteed numbers

**Status: false claim when applied literally.** Circinus has no original Flamsteed numbers. Later southern numbering systems should be called Gould or other catalogue designations unless their provenance is demonstrated.

## 7. Bode as inventor

**Status: false attribution.** Bode depicted Circinus but did not invent it. Lacaille’s 1756 chart predates Bode’s first atlas treatment by 26 years.

## 8. Hevelius or Plancius as inventor

**Status: false attribution.** Their genuine southern constellations encourage confusion, but neither published Circinus.

## 9. The “surveyor’s set”

**Status: strong scholarly reconstruction based on iconography.** The coordinated square/rule, compasses, and level are plainly depicted. What remains unproven is any narrower claim that they reproduce Lacaille’s personal equipment or encode membership in a secret society.

## 10. Freemasonry

**Status: unverified intention.** Norma plus Circinus resembles square and compasses. No statement by Lacaille makes it Masonic; the additional level and his geodetic profession offer an ordinary technical explanation.

## 11. Ancient Greek myth

**Status: modern fabrication when applied to the stars.** The Perdix/Talos compass-invention story is ancient; its placement in Circinus is not.

## 12. Al-Ṣūfī or Bedouin proper names

**Status: unsupported.** No such Circinus constellation occurs in al-Ṣūfī. Modern Arabic translations must not be presented as medieval star names.

## 13. SN 185 “in Circinus”

**Status: useful modern reconstruction.** The Chinese recorded a guest star in Nánmén, not Circinus. RCW 86 lies inside today’s Circinus boundary and is a strong remnant candidate. The equation is persuasive but not a word-for-word ancient identification.

## 14. NGC 5359

**Status: object-identification uncertainty.** Some NGC compilations retain it as an open cluster; others qualify it as “cluster?” Gaia-based membership work is needed before treating it as a secure physical system.

## 15. NGC 5823 versus NGC 5822

**Status: recurrent misidentification.** NGC 5823 is in Circinus; NGC 5822 is in Lupus. Their similar designations and proximity have produced caption and observing-list errors.

## 16. Xami as an ancient name of α Circini

**Status: misleading if unqualified.** Xami is rooted in genuine southern African tradition, but its assignment to α Circini is an IAU decision of 2024. The surviving tradition identifies a larger beast centered on the Centauri “eyes,” not an individually named α Circini.

## 17. HIP 71906 versus HIP 71908

**Status: apparent modern database typo.** α Circini is HIP 71908. The WGSN Circinus profile’s HIP 71906 conflicts with the IAU adoption table and standard catalogues.

## 18. Alpha Circinids

**Status: weak/minor shower.** A reported 1977 outburst exists, but it should not be presented as a prominent, securely recurring annual shower.

## 19. “Bode’s 830 stars in 109 groups”

**Status: unverified.** No consulted Bode edition or institutional description substantiated this statistic. The documented companion catalogue contains 17,240 entries; Bode’s atlas index contains over one hundred figures, including obsolete ones. A collection shelfmark, database record, or URL would be required to identify the alleged “830/109” corpus.

## 20. Commercial star naming

**Status: modern commercial fiction.** Services selling a “named star in Circinus” do not confer an IAU-recognized proper name. Xami is the only current IAU proper name among the constellation’s principal stars.

## Open questions

1. **Lacaille’s drafting process.** Do surviving Paris Observatory manuscripts show the exact moment at which he separated the Circinus stars from the neighboring fields?

2. **The earliest preparatory image.** A claim that the compass appears unnamed on a 1754 map circulates, but the exact object, date, and publication history require direct manuscript comparison.

3. **Bode’s subsidiary letters.** A systematic comparison of Bode’s plate XX and catalogue with Gould and modern catalogues could establish every faint letter, including ν Circini, and why particular labels disappeared.

4. **Pre-Lacaille indigenous star geometry.** More community-led work is needed on the precise extent and variants of Xami. The 2024 name should stimulate documentation, not replace it.

5. **Chinese added-star chronology.** The exact catalogue or star map in which α Circini first became Nánmén zēng èr should be identified rather than described generically as “ancient Chinese.”

6. **RCW 86 and CE 185.** The association is strong but continuing remnant-age, explosion-type, and historical-position research may refine or challenge it.

7. **NGC 5359.** Its status as a genuine open cluster remains less secure than the other major Circinus NGC objects.

8. **Alpha Circinids.** Further orbit and recurrence data are needed to establish whether the 1977 outburst represents a persistent stream.

9. **The supplied “830 stars in 109 groups” lead.** Its source remains unidentified.

10. **Regional vernacular histories.** Modern translations—Arabic *al-bīkār*, Persian *pargār*, Hebrew *meḥugah*, Chinese *Yuánguī-zuò*—need dated national-standard sources to determine their earliest formal adoption.
