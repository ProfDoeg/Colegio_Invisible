# Tarandus vel Rangifer: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact** — directly attested in a contemporary chart, catalogue, institutional record, or primary text.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern identification or interpretation derived from historical charts and coordinates.
- **Tradition** — a culturally transmitted identification recorded ethnographically or historically.
- **Disputed claim** — competing readings exist or the evidence does not establish the commonly repeated conclusion.
- **Unverified claim** — repeated in secondary literature but not located in a contemporary source during this investigation.
- **Modern invention** — an early-modern or modern celestial figure, rather than an inherited ancient constellation.
- **Myth/legend** — a narrative belonging to a religious or folkloric tradition, without implying literal historicity.

## Basic identification

### Status

**ABOLISHED / NOT ADOPTED AS AN IAU CONSTELLATION.**

Tarandus vel Rangifer has no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, area, or boundary. Its former stars now lie chiefly in **Cepheus** and **Cassiopeia**, with the historically important northern tail-star **2 Ursae Minoris** falling inside the modern boundary of **Cepheus** despite retaining its Flamsteed designation.

The familiar statement that the IAU “abolished Tarandus in 1922” needs qualification:

- **Documented fact:** the IAU adopted a canonical list of 88 constellation names and three-letter abbreviations at its first General Assembly in Rome in 1922. Tarandus/Rangifer was not included.
- **Documented fact:** Eugène Delporte subsequently drew exact boundaries for those 88 constellations, published in 1930 in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations*. His work assigned every point of the sky to one of the retained constellations.
- **Interpretive shorthand:** saying that Tarandus was “abolished in 1922” is reasonable, but no evidence was found for a separate IAU motion naming Tarandus and formally suppressing it. It disappeared through **non-selection**, followed by the 1930 boundary settlement.

The surviving irony is that in December 2025 the IAU Working Group on Star Names adopted:

- **Rangifer** for **49 Cassiopeiae A** — HR 592, HIP 9763.
- **Tarandus** for **2 Ursae Minoris** — HR 285, HIP 5372, physically inside modern Cepheus.

Thus the **constellation remains obsolete**, but both of its principal historical names now survive as official **individual star names**. The IAU announced the 2025 additions publicly in February 2026. [IAU 2026 announcement](https://www.iau.org/IAU/News/Ann2026/New-Star-Names-2026.aspx)

### Position

**Documented in early-modern charts:** a small, faint circumpolar reindeer near the north celestial pole, variously described as between or adjoining:

- Cassiopeia;
- Cepheus;
- Camelopardalis;
- Ursa Minor;
- and, in later atlases, the equally obsolete **Custos Messium**.

Descriptions that place it simply “between Cassiopeia and Camelopardalis” compress a figure whose versions changed substantially. Le Monnier’s northernmost form reached 2 UMi near declination +86°. Bode’s 1801 body extended across what is now northern Cassiopeia and Cepheus.

### Etymological complications

**Documented ancient usage:** Pliny, *Natural History* 8.34, describes a Scythian beast called *tarandus* that allegedly changes color like a chameleon. This is natural history or travelers’ lore, not a constellation. [Pliny, Book VIII](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/holland/pliny8.html)

**Scholarly caution:** Greek τάρανδος and Latin *tarandus* are old animal terms, but the ancient creature cannot always be identified zoologically with complete confidence. Later naturalists applied the word to the reindeer.

**Documented early-modern taxonomy:** Linnaeus used *Cervus tarandus* in 1758. The modern genus *Rangifer* was established later; Charles Hamilton Smith is commonly credited with it in 1827. Therefore claims that Le Monnier himself named his figure from the already-established binomial *Rangifer tarandus* are anachronistic.

**Disputed etymology:** medieval writers offered *rangifer* as though it meant “branch-bearing,” referring to antlers—Albertus Magnus has *Dicitur Rangyfer quasi ramifer*. This is commonly treated as folk etymology. Possible connections to Scandinavian or Sámi reindeer words have been proposed, but no single derivation was verified as universally accepted.

### No official abbreviation or genitive

Because the figure did not enter the IAU 88:

- abbreviation: **none**;
- official genitive: **none**;
- official boundary: **none**.

Occasional reference lists supply **Tarandi** or **Rangiferi** as grammatical Latin genitives. Those are editorial Latin forms, not IAU designations.

## Stars and objects

## The problem of defining “its stars”

Tarandus never enjoyed stable boundaries. Three different things must be distinguished:

1. the stars touched by Le Monnier’s 1743 drawing;
2. the stars assigned by Bode’s 1801 catalogue;
3. stars enclosed by a modern polygon reconstructed from Bode’s artwork.

Le Monnier did not publish a numbered star catalogue for his Reindeer. Bode’s catalogue assigned **47 stellar entries** according to one direct modern count; the often-repeated description “46 stars plus one nebula” counts the nonstellar object separately. This is the likely source of the apparently contradictory totals 46 and 47.

A recent chart reconstruction associated with the IAU Working Group on Star Names identifies 45 stars inside or on the Bode figure’s hull, but this is explicitly a **modern graphical reconstruction**, not Bode’s own numbered anatomy. [All Skies Encyclopaedia reconstruction](https://ase.exopla.net/index.php/Rangifer)

### Principal and brightest stars

| Historical role | Modern designation | V magnitude, approximately | Modern constellation | Notes |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Northern tail in Le Monnier 1743 and Bode 1782 | **2 UMi**, HR 285, HIP 5372 | 4.23–4.25 | Cepheus | Brightest star associated with the early figure; officially named **Tarandus** in 2025. Not incorporated into Bode’s 1801 drawn body according to the modern overlay. |
| Bright interior/body star | **HD 19275**, HR 932, HIP 14862 | 4.83–4.92 | Camelopardalis | Officially named **Shangcheng** in 2025 from Chinese tradition. |
| Later reconstructed member | **OV Cep**, HR 2609, HIP 37391 | about 5.07 | Cepheus | Variable-star designation; its inclusion depends on which historical outline is used. |
| Principal Bode-outline star | **49 Cas**, HR 592, HIP 9763 | 5.22 | Cassiopeia | Officially named **Rangifer** in 2025. Binary system; G8 III primary. |
| Bode-region star | **40 Cas**, HR 456, HIP 7650 | about 5.25 | Cassiopeia | In the Chinese Huagai asterism. |
| Bode-region star | **47 Cas**, HR 581, HIP 9727 | about 5.27–5.38 | Cassiopeia | Also associated by some atlas reconstructions with neighboring Custos Messium. |

The prominence of 2 UMi demonstrates why a single answer to “Tarandus’s brightest star” is misleading:

- **Le Monnier/early Bode figure:** 2 UMi.
- **Bode 1801 body reconstruction:** HD 19275 may be brightest.
- **Star chosen to preserve the name Rangifer:** 49 Cas.

### Bayer and Flamsteed designations

No Bayer Greek-letter sequence was ever created for Tarandus. Bayer’s *Uranometria* appeared in 1603, 140 years before the figure.

Relevant Flamsteed-style numbers belong to older surrounding constellations:

- 2 Ursae Minoris;
- 40 Cassiopeiae;
- 47 Cassiopeiae;
- 49 Cassiopeiae.

Their survival shows that the Reindeer was superimposed upon an already catalogued sky rather than replacing the standard designations.

### Proper names and derivations

#### Rangifer — 49 Cassiopeiae A

**Modern official name, historical source:** adopted by the IAU WGSN on 25 December 2025 to preserve the obsolete constellation’s Latin name. It is not an inherited Arabic proper name for the star.

#### Tarandus — 2 Ursae Minoris

**Modern official name, historical source:** adopted on 25 December 2025. The designation memorializes the obsolete figure and the Greek/Latin animal word.

#### Shangcheng — HD 19275

**Chinese-derived official name:** adopted in 2025 for HR 932/HIP 14862. It belongs to traditional Chinese uranography, not to Tarandus as such.

No historical Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Greek, or Babylonian proper name was found for 49 Cas, 2 UMi, or the other principal faint stars specifically because they belonged to the Reindeer.

### Internal or overlapping asterisms

Tarandus itself functioned as an asterism or proposed constellation. No stable named “sub-asterism” inside it was found in European literature.

The same stars participate in Chinese figures:

- **40 Cas** was the second star of **Huagai** (華蓋), the imperial canopy.
- **49 Cas** was the third star of **Gang** (杠), the canopy’s supporting pole.
- **HD 19275** was identified as the second star of Gang in one reconstruction.
- **47 Cas** belonged to **Wudi Neizuo** (五帝內座), the Interior Seats of the Five Emperors.

Because Chinese identifications can vary between catalogues and historical periods, these are correspondences between star lists, not claims that a Chinese “reindeer” occupied the same exact figure. [Chinese Cassiopeia concordance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassiopeia_in_Chinese_astronomy)

### Deep-sky objects

#### NGC 1184

The only deep-sky object regularly and specifically associated with Bode’s Tarandus is **NGC 1184**:

- modern constellation: Cepheus;
- type: lenticular or early spiral, generally S0/a;
- coordinates: approximately RA 03h 16m 45s, Dec +80° 47′ 36″, J2000;
- apparent magnitude: approximately 12.2–13.4 depending on band and catalogue;
- angular size: about 2.8′ × 0.6′;
- distance estimate: roughly 34 Mpc or 110–115 million light-years;
- discoverer: William Herschel;
- discovery date: 16 September 1787;
- Herschel designation: II 704;
- other designations: UGC 2583, PGC 12174.

Bode’s “nebula” was therefore an object then unresolved into its modern galactic nature. [SIMBAD service](https://simbad.cfa.harvard.edu/simbad/) [NGC 1184 observational summary](https://in-the-sky.org/data/object.php?id=NGC1184) [historical identification](https://cseligman.com/text/atlas/ngc11a.htm)

No Messier object bears the name Tarandus or Rangifer. No accepted nebula, cluster, or galaxy is formally called the “Reindeer Nebula” by virtue of Le Monnier’s constellation.

### Variable stars, novae, and supernovae

- **V408 Cep** and **OV Cep** occur in modern reconstructions of the old territory or figure.
- They received their variable-star names under the modern constellations, not Tarandus.
- No historical variable designation of the form “R Tarandi” or “R Rangiferi” was established.
- No securely documented classical guest star, nova, or supernova was found that was described historically as occurring “in Tarandus.”
- No supernova remnant is formally named for Tarandus.

The famous SN 1572 and Cassiopeia A lie in the broader Cassiopeia region but were not demonstrated to fall within the little Reindeer’s historical body; attributing them to Tarandus would be anachronistic or geometrically unsupported.

### Meteor showers

No meteor shower recognized by the IAU Meteor Data Center was found under **Tarandids**, **Rangiferids**, **Reindeerids**, or a cognate title. Meteors with radiants in the same modern sky would be named for Cepheus, Cassiopeia, Camelopardalis, or Ursa Minor.

### Boundary history

#### Ptolemy

**Documented absence:** Ptolemy’s *Almagest* had no reindeer constellation here. The faint polar stars concerned were either unassigned background stars or described relative to Ursa Minor, Cepheus, and Cassiopeia.

#### Bayer, 1603

**Documented absence:** Tarandus did not exist when Bayer published *Uranometria*. The relevant sky belonged to the inherited northern figures and to spaces not incorporated into a separate reindeer.

#### Hevelius, 1690

**Documented absence:** Hevelius did not include Tarandus in *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*. Claims that Hevelius depicted it confuse his atlas with later works or reproduce a juxtaposed comparison image.

#### Flamsteed, 1729

**Documented absence:** John Flamsteed’s original *Atlas Coelestis* predates Le Monnier’s chart. It cannot contain Tarandus. The Reindeer appears in Fortin’s modified French Flamsteed atlas of 1776, not in Flamsteed’s original work.

#### Le Monnier, 1743

The first securely dated printed figure ran from near 2 UMi downward between the older constellations. It lacked Custos Messium, which had not yet been invented.

#### Fortin, 1776 and 1795

Fortin inserted **le Réene** into his reduced French adaptation of Flamsteed. His 1795 revision regularized the spelling to **le Renne**.

#### Bode, 1782

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* reproduced and developed the Fortin tradition. In this version, 2 UMi still marks the tip of the animal’s tail.

#### Bode, 1801

Bode redrew the Reindeer on his much larger *Uranographia* and placed it beside Custos Messium. Its anatomy and star selection were not identical to Le Monnier’s. The modern reconstruction finds that 2 UMi no longer lies in the 1801 body.

#### Argelander and Heis

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* (1843) and Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* (1872) helped establish a more restrained practical northern constellation system. No evidence was found that either retained Tarandus as an independent scientific constellation. Their influence favored the familiar classical/early-modern set that became the basis of the IAU list.

#### Delporte, 1930

Delporte divided the old area principally among:

- **Cepheus** — most of the high-declination territory and NGC 1184;
- **Cassiopeia** — including 40, 47, and 49 Cas;
- **Camelopardalis** — portions toward the east/southeast;
- with 2 UMi, despite its old catalogue number, geometrically inside Cepheus.

The boundaries were defined in straight segments of right ascension and declination for epoch B1875.0. [Delporte bibliographical record](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity: the animal word, not the constellation

**Fourth century BCE — documented natural-history vocabulary:** Aristotle and Theophrastus are reported in the later zoological tradition as using a form of *tarandos*. This does not establish a reindeer constellation.

**First century CE — documented literary attestation:** Pliny, *Natural History* 8.34, places the *tarandus* in Scythia and attributes color-changing properties to it. This is the earliest directly checked classical text pertinent to the name. It contains no catasterism.

### Medieval and Renaissance animal-name tradition

**By 1413 — lexicographically documented:** Renaissance/medieval Latin *rangifer* is attested with the meaning reindeer.

**Thirteenth century — tradition/disputed etymology:** Albertus Magnus explains *rangyfer* as if derived from *ramifer*, “branch-bearing.” It is important as reception history but not reliable historical linguistics.

**1621 — documented zoological reception:** Ulisse Aldrovandi treated *tarandus* in his animal history. Early naturalists sometimes distinguished *tarandus* from *rangifer* as separate beasts.

### 1627 — Schiller’s Christian sky

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* was published at Augsburg, with engravings by Lucas Kilian and assistance connected to Johann Bayer.

It predates Tarandus. Therefore it did not “replace Tarandus” with a saint. In the same general region:

- Cassiopeia became **Mary Magdalene**;
- Cepheus became **St Stephen the Protomartyr**.

The Reindeer occupied interstitial portions of those older figures only in the eighteenth century. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/)

### 1736–1737 — Maupertuis’s Lapland expedition

**Documented fact:** the French Academy sent a northern geodetic expedition to the Tornio/Torne Valley to measure a meridian arc and test competing models of Earth’s figure.

The principal participants included:

- Pierre-Louis Moreau de Maupertuis;
- Alexis-Claude Clairaut;
- Pierre-Charles Le Monnier;
- Charles-Étienne-Louis Camus;
- the abbé Réginal Outhier;
- Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius;
- additional assistants and local laborers.

Measurements were made in the region of Tornio, Pello, Kittisvaara, and neighboring stations during 1736–37.

**Documented scientific significance:** the result supported Newtonian oblateness—Earth is flattened at the poles—against models that made it prolate.

**Qualification:** “proved the Earth flattened at the poles” is historically conventional but rhetorically stronger than the experiment alone warrants. The Lapland result, compared with lower-latitude measurements, gave powerful empirical support; later geodesy refined the conclusion.

### 1742–1743 — invention and first publication

A comet traversed the north-polar region in 1742.

**First secure constellation attestation:** Le Monnier printed a chart of its course in *La théorie des comètes* (Paris, 1743). On that chart appears a little reindeer labeled **le Reene**.

The book’s bibliographical record is secure: Paris, Gabriel Martin, J.-B. Coignard, and the Guérin brothers, 1743. [ETH Zürich digital edition](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/694761)

**Modern-invention status:** the reindeer was not inherited from Ptolemy, Arabic astronomical catalogues, or Renaissance globes. It first appears as Le Monnier’s cartographic addition.

**Purpose — scholarly reconstruction:** the polar animal is interpreted as a memorial of Le Monnier’s Lapland journey and of Maupertuis’s geodetic expedition. This interpretation is compelling because of the animal, position, inventor, and date.

**Important absence:** no checked passage in *La théorie des comètes* explicitly says, in substance, “I place this Reindeer in the sky to commemorate Maupertuis’s expedition.” Modern accounts generally infer the dedication from circumstances. It should not be presented as a recovered dedicatory quotation.

### 1746 — repetition

Le Monnier republished the polar comet chart in *Institutions astronomiques*. The Reindeer remained, apparently without a new explanatory narrative.

### 1758 — Linnaeus

Linnaeus published *Cervus tarandus*. This postdates Le Monnier’s first chart by fifteen years. It helped standardize *tarandus* zoologically but cannot explain the original French label.

### 1776 — Fortin

Jean-Baptiste Fortin published at Paris a reduced and updated French *Atlas céleste de Flamsteed*, engraved by C.-E. Voisard and drawn by Beaublé. It admitted recent constellations absent from Flamsteed’s original atlas, including **le Réene**. [Fortin atlas record](https://mediatheques.montpellier3m.fr/BIBNUMERIQUE/doc/CAMO/1138589/atlas-celeste-de-flamsteed-approuve-par-l-academie-royale-des-sciences-et-publie-sous-le-privilege-d)

The work’s preface explicitly attributes additions to later makers—among them “le Renne, d’après Lemonnier”—and connects it with the polar voyage and measurement of Earth. This is early documentary evidence for the commemorative reading, though later than the 1743 chart.

### 1775–1779 — Custos Messium arrives

Jérôme Lalande introduced **Custos Messium**, the Harvest Keeper, in honor of Charles Messier. The pun joined *Messier* with Latin *messis*, harvest.

Bode and later artists placed the human Keeper beside or overlapping the Reindeer. Because Le Monnier’s original drawing predates Custos, later adjacency altered the Reindeer’s outline and helped displace some of its stars.

### 1782 — Bode’s first version

Johann Elert Bode published *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln*, a German adaptation of Fortin’s reduced Flamsteed atlas, enlarged with newer observations. The Reindeer appeared under a German vernacular label. [1782 bibliographical record](https://bcul.lib.uni.lodz.pl/dlibra/publication/105973/edition/95502)

### 1787 — discovery of the nebula later called NGC 1184

William Herschel observed the object on 16 September 1787, cataloguing it as II 704. Bode later placed it within the Reindeer.

### 1795 — corrected French spelling

Fortin’s revised edition used **le Renne**, correcting *Reene/Réene*.

### 1801 — Bode’s definitive grand atlas

Bode issued:

- *Uranographia sive astrorum descriptio viginti tabulis aeneis incisa*;
- its bilingual German/French catalogue companion, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*.

The atlas consisted of twenty enormous engraved double-page maps. The companion catalogue advertised 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. [Bode catalogue and institutional facsimile](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

On Bode’s north-polar plate the figure appears as a naturalistic antlered reindeer beside Custos Messium and above Camelopardalis. Bode’s label is **Rangifer** in the atlas tradition; later references commonly call it **Tarandus vel Rangifer**.

Bode’s catalogue gives it roughly 46 stars of fifth to seventh magnitude and a nebular entry, now NGC 1184. A modern direct count reports 47 stellar entries; the discrepancy remains an editorial counting question.

### 1822 — Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson included the figure in *A Celestial Atlas*. His engraved figure follows Bode’s general arrangement but uses the zoological-looking label **Rangifer tarandus**.

### 1824–1825 — *Urania’s Mirror*

Card 2 of *Urania’s Mirror* depicts:

- Camelopardalis;
- Tarandus/Rangifer;
- Custos Messium.

The cards were engraved by Sidney Hall and published by Samuel Leigh in London. They were long advertised as designed “by a lady.” Peter Hingley later identified the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam as the likely author, while the possibility of some participation by his wife Ann has not been completely excluded.

The artwork copied Jamieson closely. [Royal Astronomical Society account](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror) [Public Domain Review reproductions](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/uranias-mirror-or-a-view-of-the-heavens/)

### 1830s–1840s — American educational reception

Elijah Hinsdale Burritt’s popular *Geography of the Heavens* and atlas tradition reproduced the northern figures, though editions differ. Tarandus became familiar chiefly through educational plates rather than professional observing catalogues.

Ezra Otis Kendall’s 1845 *Uranography* also lists Tarandus on its northern plates. [Kendall scan](https://books.google.co.in/books?id=y6cXAAAAYAAJ)

### 1843–1872 — scientific contraction

Argelander and Heis emphasized standardized observational constellations and did not perpetuate Tarandus as an operative catalogue constellation. Its removal was already effectively underway decades before the IAU.

### 1877 — late literary/catalogue survival

E. Chambers’s nineteenth-century astronomical handbook still mentioned Tarandus. Such survival in reference works should not be mistaken for universal astronomical acceptance.

### 1899 — Allen

Richard Hinckley Allen’s *Star-Names and Their Meanings* described:

> “Tarandus vel Rangifer, the Reindeer, a small and faint asterism between Cassiopeia and Camelopardalis…”

Allen connected it with Le Monnier’s 1736 geodetic stay in Lapland. This helped popularize the shorthand “formed in 1736,” though the chart’s publication was in 1743. [Allen transcription](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Tarandus%2A.html)

### 1922–1930 — disappearance from formal astronomy

Tarandus was omitted from the IAU 88 and its territory was partitioned by Delporte.

### 2016 — Barentine

John C. Barentine devoted a chapter to Rangifer in *The Lost Constellations: A History of Obsolete, Extinct, or Forgotten Star Lore*. His modern overlays are a major source for proposed identifications of individual stars.

### 2025–2026 — official star-name afterlife

The IAU WGSN preserved four connected names:

- Rangifer — 49 Cas;
- Tarandus — 2 UMi;
- Sarvvis — δ Per;
- Áldu — ε Per.

The conjunction deliberately recognizes both the early-modern European Reindeer and Sámi celestial-reindeer traditions. [IAU Division C 2025 report](https://www.iau.org/common/Uploaded%20files/Division-C-Annual%20Report%202025.pdf)

## Invention and reception

### Was it invented “in 1736”?

**Disputed chronology.**

- Le Monnier was in Lapland in 1736–37.
- No globe, chart, manuscript, or publication dated 1736 containing the figure was located.
- The first securely documented chart is dated **1743**.
- Therefore “invented in 1736” probably means “conceived in connection with the 1736 expedition,” or derives from Allen’s formulation, rather than identifying a surviving 1736 artefact.

The safest formula is:

> **Le Monnier first published the constellation in 1743, apparently commemorating the 1736–37 Lapland expedition. Its exact date of conception is unknown.**

### What did it honor?

**Strong scholarly reconstruction:** Maupertuis’s Lapland expedition and the geodetic determination of Earth’s polar flattening.

Evidence:

1. Le Monnier personally accompanied the expedition.
2. A reindeer was emblematic of Lapland in European imagery.
3. He placed it beside the north celestial pole.
4. Fortin’s 1776 atlas explicitly connected the addition to the polar voyage and terrestrial measurement.
5. Maupertuis’s own iconography included Lapland, sledges, and reindeer.

No evidence was found that it honored:

- a king;
- an individual patron;
- a mechanical instrument;
- a ship;
- a specific named reindeer;
- Santa Claus.

The Santa association is a much later popular joke.

### Bode’s role

Bode did **not** invent Tarandus. He:

- received it through Le Monnier and Fortin;
- incorporated it into his 1782 German atlas;
- redrew it for the 1801 *Uranographia*;
- Latinized or standardized the name as **Rangifer**;
- supplied a catalogue grouping, which Le Monnier had not;
- placed it in a crowded modern-constellation landscape beside Custos Messium.

### Why it failed

No contemporary manifesto announcing its rejection was found. Its decline is explained cumulatively:

- the stars are extremely faint;
- the figure lacked a memorable naked-eye pattern;
- it overlapped older catalogued territory;
- its outline changed from atlas to atlas;
- it had no Greek mythological authority;
- it was not universally adopted by working astronomers;
- nineteenth-century practical atlases reduced the profusion of eighteenth-century inventions;
- the IAU favored a manageable inherited set of northern constellations.

## Myths and legends by culture

## Classical Greek and Roman

### No constellation myth

**Documented absence:** Tarandus is modern. It has no catasterism in:

- Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* or the Epitome;
- Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica*;
- Hyginus, *Fabulae*;
- Aratus or the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus or his scholia;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias.

There is consequently:

- no god or hero who placed Tarandus in the sky;
- no competing Greek identities;
- no punishment, rescue, metamorphosis, or memorial catasterism;
- no ancient astrology specific to it.

### The classical *tarandus* legend

The relevant ancient legend concerns an earthly animal. Pliny says the Scythian *tarandus* changes the color of its coat, eyes, tail, and body to match its surroundings, except red and white. It supposedly has little flesh and hibernates like a lizard.

Classification: **classical natural-history marvel/legend**, not astronomical myth.

First directly verified appearance in this dossier: Pliny, *Natural History* 8.34, first century CE. Pliny or his sources spread a fabulous animal description that Renaissance zoologists later associated with northern deer.

## Sámi

### Sarvvis/Sarva, the celestial wild reindeer or elk

**Tradition, ethnographically recorded:** Sámi star lore contains a large animal called **Sarvvis**, **Sarva**, or related dialectal forms—variously translated bull reindeer, wild reindeer, or elk/moose.

It is not identical to Le Monnier’s little figure. Reconstructions place its body over a much larger region involving Auriga, Perseus, Cassiopeia, and sometimes Cepheus.

The variation “reindeer” versus “elk” reflects both Sámi dialect/geography and translation. Northern Sámi sources commonly interpret it as a reindeer bull; southern and eastern traditions may favor elk.

### The cosmic hunt

**Sámi myth/tradition:** Sarvvis is pursued across the sky by hunters and dogs. Figures reconstructed from Orion, the Great Bear, Sirius, the Pleiades, and other stars participate in versions of the hunt.

One widely circulated version makes **Fávdná/Favdna** a hunter whose bow is associated with the Big Dipper. Polaris is **Boahjenásti**, the nail or support of the sky. If the hunter’s arrow strikes the support while aiming at Sarvvis, the sky collapses and the world ends.

Classification: **recorded celestial myth with regional variants**. It should not be converted into a single canonical Sámi system. Modern popular charts combine information gathered at different places and dates. [Stellarium Sámi source notes](https://fossies.org/linux/stellarium/skycultures/sami/description.md) [Norwegian Sámi-sky educational source](https://www.naturfag.no/artikkel/vis.html?tid=815466)

### Áldu, the reindeer cow

**Tradition and modern scholarly discussion:** *Áldu* means a female reindeer/reindeer cow. The IAU assigned the name to ε Persei in 2025.

A biological observation has entered the interpretation: adult female reindeer retain antlers through winter longer than males. Some modern researchers consequently ask whether a winter-antlered celestial animal described grammatically as male may preserve or conflate a female form. This is an interpretive question, not a settled correction of Sámi tradition.

### Did Le Monnier borrow Sarvvis?

**Plausible but unproved reconstruction.**

For:

- Le Monnier spent months in Sápmi;
- he could have encountered Sámi celestial lore;
- he returned with an interest in placing a reindeer in the northern sky;
- the coincidence is culturally suggestive.

Against:

- no diary entry, letter, or passage by Le Monnier was located stating that a Sámi informant taught him Sarvvis;
- his figure is much smaller and uses different stars;
- a reindeer was already an obvious European emblem of Lapland.

Conclusion: “Inspired by the Sámi constellation” should be labeled **possible**, not documented. “Inspired by his Lapland expedition” is much more securely supported.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

No Babylonian constellation equivalent to Tarandus was located. The extreme northern faint stars were not shown to form a Mesopotamian reindeer.

Classification: **absence of evidence**, not proof that every local Mesopotamian observer lacked an informal pattern.

### Egyptian

No pharaonic Egyptian reindeer figure or exact equivalent in these polar stars was located. Reindeer are ecologically foreign to Egypt, and popular claims of an “Egyptian antecedent” lack identified texts or artefacts.

### Greek

No Greek constellation in these stars corresponds to a reindeer. The word *tarandos* belongs to zoology, not Greek uranography.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* follows the Ptolemaic constellations. No *Rangifer* or *Tarandus* occurs.

The relevant stars would have been catalogued, where visible and included, relative to:

- al-Dubb al-Aṣghar, the Lesser Bear;
- Qayqāwūs/Cepheus;
- Dhāt al-Kursī, the Lady of the Chair/Cassiopeia;
- or as stars outside the formal figures.

No verified Bedouin camel, gazelle, reindeer, or other asterism matching the Bode Reindeer’s precise stars was found. That is a meaningful negative result; generic statements that “the Arabs saw animals here” are insufficient.

### Persian

Persian astronomical catalogues largely transmitted the Ptolemaic-Arabic constellations. No premodern Persian reindeer figure in this exact region was traced.

### Hebrew and Jewish astronomical literature

No ancient or medieval Hebrew name specifically for Tarandus or this star group was found. Hebrew terminology for the retained neighboring constellations does not establish a reindeer figure.

### Kabbalistic mappings

No pre-1743 Kabbalistic mapping corresponding to Tarandus was located. Systems assigning zodiacal constellations, planets, Hebrew letters, tribes, or divine names cannot be retroactively applied to an eighteenth-century circumpolar figure without a specific source.

Modern occult tables that include every atlas figure would constitute **modern synthesis**, not inherited Kabbalah.

### Indian

No Vedic, Sanskrit, siddhāntic, or nakṣatra figure matching Tarandus was located. The region is far from the lunar zodiac, and no lunar mansion crosses it.

### Chinese

The strongest independent equivalences are the Chinese asterisms already noted:

- **Huagai** — imperial canopy;
- **Gang** — canopy support;
- **Wudi Neizuo** — interior seats of the Five Emperors.

These belong to the circumpolar **Purple Forbidden Enclosure**, a celestial imperial complex.

They are historically independent of Tarandus and considerably older. They demonstrate that the same faint stars carried coherent functions before Le Monnier, but not as a reindeer.

### Christian sky

Schiller’s 1627 atlas made:

- Cassiopeia → St Mary Magdalene;
- Cepheus → St Stephen.

Because Tarandus was still nonexistent, statements such as “Schiller replaced the Reindeer with St X” are chronologically false. At most, later Tarandus used stars that Schiller had incorporated into or placed near those Christian figures.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No historically documented alchemical emblem, Hermetic decan, planetary intelligence, metal, operation, or *Book of Thoth* image was found for Tarandus.

The reasons are chronological and positional:

- it is outside the zodiac;
- it was invented in a scientific atlas culture in the eighteenth century;
- it had only limited reception.

Later esoteric writers may freely interpret the reindeer, north, antlers, renewal, or psychopomp symbolism; without a cited historical source these are **modern inventions**.

### Astrology

Tarandus has:

- no Ptolemaic planetary nature in *Tetrabiblos*;
- no traditional judicial-astrological doctrine;
- no Behenian fixed star;
- no paran tradition under its own name;
- no lunar mansion;
- no zodiacal rulership.

The individual stars may receive modern astrological interpretation under Cassiopeia, Cepheus, or Ursa Minor. That is not ancient Tarandus astrology.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas, second century CE Roman copy

**Documented absence:** the Farnese globe cannot show Tarandus, which was invented roughly sixteen centuries later. Its relevance is comparative: it preserves ancient figural uranography against which modern additions can be recognized.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts, tenth century onward

**Documented absence:** their paired internal/external constellation drawings preserve the Ptolemaic canon. No reindeer occupies this polar gap.

### Dürer’s 1515 celestial maps

**Documented absence:** Dürer’s northern map predates Tarandus by more than two centuries. Any modern reproduction labeling a Reindeer on a “Dürer map” is an overlay or error.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

No Tarandus appears in *Uranometria*.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

No Tarandus. Its future region is absorbed into Christianized Cassiopeia/Mary Magdalene and Cepheus/St Stephen. The Library of Congress copy comprises 49 reversed-sky charts. [Schiller facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbc0001.2017rosen1317/?st=grid)

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

No Tarandus. The common inclusion of a Hevelius page in modern articles about the Reindeer usually illustrates neighboring Camelopardalis or the general history of obsolete constellations.

### Le Monnier, Paris, 1743

The first image: a small, lightly constructed reindeer inserted on the chart of the 1742 comet’s path. The animal stands in the polar region with the tail extending toward 2 UMi. Label: **le Reene**.

### Fortin, Paris, 1776

The Reindeer is absorbed into the elegant reduced-French-Flamsteed visual system. Label: **le Réene**. The atlas was engraved by C.-E. Voisard from drawings by Beaublé.

### Bode, Berlin, 1782

A compact German engraved Reindeer; 2 UMi remains attached to its tail. Label in the German vernacular tradition: **Rennthier** or spelling variant.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

The best-known scientific-artistic image. The Reindeer is shown in profile, antlered, above Camelopardalis and adjoining Custos Messium.

Bode’s figure differs materially from Le Monnier’s:

- it is more naturalistically modeled;
- the body is shifted and compressed;
- the tail no longer demonstrably reaches 2 UMi;
- Custos Messium crowds the adjacent space;
- more faint stars and a nebula are catalogued.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson recast Bode’s composition in a softer, more naturalistic Regency style. His labelling helped associate the figure with **Rangifer tarandus**.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824–1825

Card 2 is the best-known colored popular image. The Reindeer, Giraffe, and Harvest Keeper share a card pierced with holes corresponding to stellar magnitudes.

Its iconography comes substantially from Jamieson; it should not be described as an independent observational reconstruction.

### Burritt, United States, nineteenth century

The figure migrated into American pedagogical celestial geography. Burritt’s engravings simplified the body and star relations for classroom use.

### Calendars, ceilings, churches, coins

No securely identified medieval calendar, cathedral portal, Renaissance ceiling, ancient coin, or early-modern commemorative medal was found that depicts Tarandus as a constellation.

Reindeer occur frequently in Sámi material culture, northern heraldry, expedition imagery, Christmas art, and Maupertuis-related portraiture, but resemblance to a reindeer does not establish an image of the constellation.

## People, events, and works

### Pierre-Charles Le Monnier

Astronomer, geodesist, lunar observer, and later repeated observer of Uranus before its recognition as a planet. His most direct connection here is authorship of the 1743 chart.

### Maupertuis

Leader and public face of the Lapland expedition. The celestial Reindeer is best understood as an emblem of his expedition, though no evidence shows it was officially named “Maupertuis’s Reindeer.”

### Alexis Clairaut

Clairaut contributed the theoretical treatment of Earth’s figure and gravitation. His presence in the expedition is historically important, but no star of Tarandus was named for him.

### Anders Celsius

Celsius supplied Scandinavian expertise and participated in the measurements. His later temperature scale is unrelated to the constellation.

### Réginal Outhier

Outhier’s expedition journal is an important eyewitness narrative for the physical journey, travel conditions, instruments, and measurements. It does not appear to be the original source of the constellation.

### Charles-Étienne-Louis Camus

A member of the geodetic party; no individual Tarandus star was dedicated to him.

### William Herschel

Discovered the galaxy now called NGC 1184 in 1787. Bode subsequently incorporated Herschel’s “nebula” into Tarandus.

### Johann Elert Bode

Responsible for the figure’s most famous chart, Latin nomenclature, and only substantial catalogue existence.

### Jean-Baptiste Fortin

A crucial transmitter. His reduced Flamsteed atlas introduced the Reindeer to a broader atlas-buying public and preserves early explicit language connecting it with Le Monnier and the Lapland expedition.

### Alexander Jamieson, Sidney Hall, Richard Rouse Bloxam

They carried the figure into British visual culture through *A Celestial Atlas* and *Urania’s Mirror*.

### Explorers and navigation

No voyage log was found in which an explorer explicitly says that he steered by “Tarandus” or “Rangifer.”

This is unsurprising:

- the stars are faint;
- the constellation was little known;
- navigators used Polaris, Ursa Minor, Ursa Major, Cassiopeia, and measured altitudes rather than the Reindeer figure.

2 UMi could serve in polar orientation, but its use as a star does not constitute navigation “by Tarandus.”

### Literary, musical, architectural, maritime, and space namesakes

No major poem, opera, symphony, building, ship, spacecraft, or astronomical mission was verified as being named specifically for the obsolete constellation.

Items called *Rangifer* usually refer to the animal or its zoological genus—not the star figure. These include the scholarly journal *Rangifer* and many biological projects.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. 1736 or 1743?

**Finding:** 1743 is the first verified publication.  
**Source of confusion:** 1736 is the expedition year and was emphasized by Allen and later popular histories.  
**Conclusion:** “invented in 1736” is unverified unless used loosely; “first published in 1743” is documented.

### 2. Was it explicitly dedicated to Maupertuis?

**Finding:** the commemorative purpose is extremely plausible and early later sources connect it with the Lapland measurement.  
**Gap:** no explicit dedicatory declaration by Le Monnier was located.  
**Conclusion:** strong scholarly reconstruction, not a verbatim documented dedication.

### 3. Was it borrowed from Sámi Sarvvis?

**Finding:** possible cultural contact, but no transmission document.  
**Conclusion:** plausible hypothesis; often overstated online as fact.

### 4. Is *Le Reene* merely a misprint?

Le Monnier’s chart reads *Reene*; Fortin reproduced *Réene* and later corrected it to *Renne*.

Possible explanations include:

- engraver’s error;
- nonstandard spelling;
- typographical transposition;
- archaizing or idiosyncratic form.

No authorial correction by Le Monnier himself was found. “Misprint” is likely, but not conclusively demonstrated.

### 5. Did Bode call it *Tarandus vel Rangifer*?

Bode’s atlas is commonly cited under **Rangifer**, while later reference works use **Tarandus vel Rangifer**. The double title should not automatically be treated as a verbatim caption on every Bode plate or as Le Monnier’s own Latin name.

### 6. Is *Tarandus* Latin or Greek?

It is properly a Greek animal word transmitted and Latinized as *tarandus*. Calling it simply “Latin for reindeer” is serviceable in an atlas glossary but conceals its classical Greek history and uncertain ancient zoological referent.

### 7. Is *Rangifer* ordinary classical Latin?

No. It is medieval/Renaissance Latin or a Latinized northern European animal name. The attractive interpretation “branch-bearer” is probably a learned folk etymology.

### 8. Did Linnaeus give the constellation its two Latin names?

No. Le Monnier’s first label predates *Cervus tarandus*. Moreover, the presently accepted genus *Rangifer* is later still. Zoological nomenclature influenced later atlas labeling, not the original invention.

### 9. Forty-six or forty-seven stars?

Modern literature alternates between:

- 46 stars plus one nebula;
- 47 stars;
- 45 stars in a reconstructed Bode hull.

These totals count different things. A definitive answer requires a line-by-line critical transcription of Bode’s catalogue entries and an explicit rule for double stars, nebular entries, and stars merely under the artwork.

### 10. Which was its brightest star?

There is no single historically invariant answer:

- 2 UMi in Le Monnier 1743/Bode 1782;
- HD 19275 in the reconstructed Bode 1801 body;
- 49 Cas as the modern namesake Rangifer.

### 11. Did Delporte transfer 2 UMi from Ursa Minor to Cepheus?

Geometrically, yes: the official boundary places the star in Cepheus. Nomenclaturally, no: Flamsteed designations are permanent historical identifiers, so it remains “2 Ursae Minoris.”

This is not an error or contradiction.

### 12. Was Tarandus “abolished by decree”?

No separate suppression decree was found. It was omitted in 1922 and made impossible as a territorial constellation by the 1930 division.

### 13. Did the constellation contain NGC 1184?

Bode catalogued the nebulous object in the Reindeer, and modern coordinate comparison supports the identification with NGC 1184. However, “NGC 1184 was discovered in Tarandus” is anachronistic if it suggests an official constellation at Herschel’s 1787 observation; Herschel used his own positional catalogue framework.

### 14. Tarandus versus Custos Messium

The two small figures adjoined and in some later renderings competed for stars. Modern captions occasionally assign 47 Cas or nearby stars to one when the chart being discussed places them in the other. The solution is atlas-specific, not a single timeless border.

### 15. “Western sky”

Tarandus belongs to the **Western constellation tradition**, but from mid-northern latitudes it is a northern circumpolar figure, not specifically a constellation located in the western quarter of the nightly sky.

### 16. Hoaxes and popular errors

The dedicated search located no historical Tarandus hoax. Recurring modern errors include:

- attributing it to Bode rather than Le Monnier;
- dating its printed appearance to 1736;
- saying it appears in Flamsteed’s original 1729 atlas;
- claiming Hevelius drew it;
- treating *Urania’s Mirror* as the work of a known anonymous woman without mentioning Bloxam;
- claiming it has an official IAU abbreviation;
- presenting arbitrary modern stick figures as Bode’s exact star list;
- connecting it with Santa’s reindeer;
- asserting documented Sámi inspiration without a transmission source.

## Open questions

1. **Date of conception:** did Le Monnier sketch the Reindeer during the 1736–37 expedition, or only while preparing the 1742-comet chart?

2. **Authorial explanation:** does an unindexed Le Monnier letter, Academy memorandum, or manuscript explain the choice directly?

3. **Spelling:** was *Reene* Le Monnier’s spelling, an engraver’s mistake, or a production error?

4. **Sámi contact:** did Le Monnier, Celsius, Outhier, or Maupertuis record a conversation about Sarvvis?

5. **Critical star list:** Bode’s catalogue requires a fresh diplomatic transcription to settle 46 versus 47 and match every entry with Gaia/HIP/HD identifiers.

6. **Changing outline:** precisely which stars moved between Tarandus, Custos Messium, Camelopardalis, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, and Ursa Minor in each edition of Fortin, Bode, Jamieson, and Burritt?

7. **Early reception:** did any globe between 1743 and Fortin’s 1776 atlas include the figure?

8. **Name history:** when does the exact compound **Tarandus vel Rangifer** first occur in print? It is later than Le Monnier’s French name, but its first verified edition remains uncertain.

9. **Sámi regional variants:** older ethnographic sources require renewed comparison without merging North, South, Inari, Skolt, and Kildin Sámi terminologies.

10. **Bode’s “nebula”:** confirmation from the printed line itself would remove dependence on modern secondary identifications of the entry with NGC 1184.
