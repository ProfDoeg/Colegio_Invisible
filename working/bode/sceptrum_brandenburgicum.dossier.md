# Sceptrum Brandenburgicum: Research Dossier

> **Status: abolished / not adopted by the IAU.**  
> *Sceptrum Brandenburgicum*, the Brandenburg Sceptre, was an early-modern political constellation introduced by Gottfried Kirch in 1688 and revived by Johann Elert Bode in 1782 and 1801. It was not included among the 88 constellations standardized by the International Astronomical Union in 1922. Eugène Delporte therefore drew no boundaries for it in 1930. Every part of Bode’s figure lies today in **Eridanus**.  
>  
> The constellation has no official IAU abbreviation or genitive. The historically regular Latin genitive is **Sceptri Brandenburgici**. Modern unofficial abbreviations such as **ScB** have no IAU standing. Its principal surviving name is **Sceptrum**, formally approved by the IAU in 2017 for **53 Eridani A**.

## Method and evidentiary labels

The following labels distinguish unlike kinds of evidence:

- **Documented fact** — directly attested in a contemporary publication, catalogue, atlas, institutional record, or official modern document.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference proposed in historical scholarship from contextual evidence.
- **Tradition** — a transmitted cultural identification or interpretation, without implying that it describes physical history.
- **Disputed claim** — incompatible identifications or chronologies occur in the sources.
- **Unverified claim** — repeated in secondary literature but not traceable here to a primary source.
- **Legend/myth** — a narrative explicitly presented as traditional or mythic.
- **Modern invention** — a post-antique constellation, name, or interpretation deliberately constructed in modern times.

Because the Sceptre was invented in 1688, it has **no independent Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, Indian, or classical mythological history**. Older traditions discussed below belong to the same stars as parts of Eridanus or other asterisms, not to a pre-existing “Brandenburg Sceptre.”

### Pronunciation and etymology

**Documented linguistic fact.** Latin *sceptrum* comes through Greek **σκῆπτρον** (*skēptron*), a staff or sceptre, from the verb **σκήπτω**, “to prop, lean, support.” In Greek epic a sceptre signifies delegated authority, kingship, judicial speech, or heraldic office. Kirch’s figure, however, is not a classical sceptre from Greek mythology: it is the emblem of Brandenburg electoral sovereignty.

“Brandenburg” is the territorial and dynastic designation of the Margraviate/Electorate of Brandenburg. Calling the object a sceptre evokes both ordinary princely rule and Brandenburg’s special imperial office: the elector of Brandenburg was hereditary **Arch-Chamberlain of the Holy Roman Empire**, an office represented ceremonially by a sceptre.

### Official status, abbreviation, and genitive

**Documented fact.**

- Not one of the 88 IAU constellations.
- No official IAU abbreviation.
- No official IAU genitive.
- Historical genitive: **Sceptri Brandenburgici**.
- “ScB,” found on modern astrological or hobbyist sites, is an unofficial back-formation.
- Modern location: wholly within **Eridanus** (*Eri*, genitive *Eridani*).

It is more accurate to say that the IAU **did not adopt** Sceptrum in 1922 than that the Union passed a separate motion “abolishing” it. The 1922 General Assembly at Rome standardized a list of 88 constellations and their abbreviations; Delporte’s 1930 publication fixed their rectilinear boundaries. Since Sceptrum was absent from the list, no independent territory survived.

### Position

Kirch and Bode placed the Sceptre:

- southwest of Orion;
- west of Lepus;
- inside or across the conspicuous northern bend of Eridanus;
- approximately around right ascension 4h30m–4h45m and declination −5° to −20° in modern coordinates, depending on whether one means Kirch’s original core or Bode’s enlarged illustrated figure.

Bode’s 1801 *Uranographia* puts it on **Plate XVIII**, among Eridanus, Lepus, Orion, Columba, Caela Sculptoris, Harpa Georgii, Officina Typographica, and related southern/equatorial figures. The atlas depicts an upright, elaborately ornamented sceptre enclosed by the River’s bend.

### The “830 stars in 109 groups” lead

**Unverified / probably conflated.** The figure-specific brief’s phrase “in the Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be traced to Bode’s description of Sceptrum, to the *Uranographia* title matter, or to a recognized catalogue of the constellation. Bode’s companion catalogue instead advertises **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters**, and the atlas’s constellation totals are variously counted depending on whether subordinate and obsolete figures are included. No reliable source consulted assigns 830 stars in 109 groups to the Brandenburg Sceptre.

## Stars and objects

### The problem of defining the membership

There was no universally accepted boundary and no stable membership list. Three different things are often conflated:

1. **Kirch’s original compact constellation** in the 1688 *Acta Eruditorum* chart;
2. **Bode’s expanded 1782/1801 drawing and catalogue**, incorporating more telescopic stars;
3. Later popular reconstructions that turn nearby Eridanus stars into a modern line asterism.

Richard Hinckley Allen, drawing on nineteenth-century sources, described **four stars of fourth and fifth magnitude in an approximately north–south line**. Bode’s German description similarly says:

> “Drei Sterne vierter Größe und ein Stern fünfter Größe stehen in fast gerader Linie zwischen Orion, Eridanus und dem Haasen untereinander.”

“Three stars of the fourth magnitude and one star of the fifth stand one below another in an almost straight line between Orion, Eridanus and the Hare.”

Bode’s text gives the introduction as **1680**, but Kirch’s dated publication is 1688. The “1680” is therefore a documented Bode error, typographical error, or lapse rather than evidence for a lost 1680 chart.

### Bode’s lettered stars and their modern identities

Bode used lower-case Roman letters for stars assigned to his Sceptre. A commonly reconstructed cross-identification is:

| Bode designation in Sceptrum | Modern identification | Modern constellation/status | Approx. visual magnitude | Notes |
|---|---|---|---:|---|
| **e Sceptri** | HD 28979 | Eridanus | ~6 | Faint telescopic member in Bode’s expanded figure. |
| **f Sceptri** | 60 Eridani | Eridanus | ~5.0 | Flamsteed star; part of Chinese Jiǔliú. |
| **m Sceptri** | 46 Eridani | Eridanus | ~5.7 | Northern portion of the historical figure. |
| **n Sceptri** | 47 Eridani | Eridanus | ~5.2 | Northern portion. |
| **o Sceptri** | HD 29573 | Eridanus | ~6 | Faint member. |
| **p Sceptri** | **53 Eridani** = **l Eridani**, HR 1481, HD 29503 | Eridanus | 3.86 combined | Brightest; 53 Eri A is officially **Sceptrum**. |
| **q Sceptri** | HD 30238 | Eridanus | ~6 | Faint member. |
| **r Sceptri** | **54 Eridani** = **DM Eridani** | Eridanus | ~4.3 | Bright red semiregular variable. |
| **z Sceptri** | **58 Eridani** = **IX Eridani** | Eridanus | ~5.5 | Variable-star designation IX Eri. |

The identifications above reconstruct **Bode’s enlarged constellation**, not necessarily Kirch’s original four-star nucleus.

### 53 Eridani A: Sceptrum

**Documented fact.**

- Flamsteed: **53 Eridani**
- Bayer-style letter used in later Eridanus catalogues: **l Eridani** — lower-case Roman *l*, not Greek lambda.
- Bode: **p Sceptri Brandenburgici**
- HR 1481
- HD 29503
- HIP 21594
- SAO 149781
- WDS identifier: 04382−1418
- Combined apparent magnitude: about 3.86–3.87
- Primary spectral class: approximately K1 III/K2 IIIb
- Distance: about 110 light-years
- Nature: visual binary; the secondary is near seventh magnitude and the orbital period is roughly 77 years.

The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Sceptrum** specifically for component **53 Eridani A** on 30 June 2017. The official list gives HR 1481, 53 Eri A, coordinates 69.545104°, −14.304017°, and magnitude 4.02 for the named component rather than the brighter combined system.

**Etymology.** The proper name is a deliberate survival from the obsolete constellation, not an Arabic or ancient name. It appears in nineteenth-century star-name usage and in Giuseppe Piazzi’s catalogue tradition before its modern IAU standardization.

### 54 Eridani / DM Eridani

**Documented fact.**

- Flamsteed: **54 Eridani**
- Bode: **r Sceptri**
- Variable name: **DM Eridani**
- Magnitude: approximately 4.28–4.36
- Spectral type: around M3/4 III
- Type: semiregular variable, SRb
- Distance: roughly 400 light-years, subject to catalogue revision.

Benjamin Apthorp Gould reported its variability in *Uranometria Argentina* (1879). It received the variable-star designation DM Eridani in 1973. It is generally the second-brightest star in Bode’s Sceptre.

### 58 Eridani / IX Eridani

**Documented fact.** Bode’s **z Sceptri** is usually identified with 58 Eri, later **IX Eridani**. It was not the origin of the name Sceptrum.

### 46, 47, 53, and 54 Eridani

Allen’s “four stars” and Bode’s prose have encouraged the identification of the naked-eye core with **46, 47, 53, and 54 Eridani**. This is geometrically plausible and agrees with the surviving Bode cross-identifications, although different reproductions emphasize different subsets.

### The erroneous “64–60–59–58–54 Eri line”

**Disputed modern reconstruction.** A recent World Asterisms Project handbook describes a five-star line through **64, 60, 59, 58, and 54 Eridani**. That line is largely the ancient Chinese **Jiǔliú** sequence and does not agree cleanly with the usual Bode-letter table, which includes 46, 47, 53, and several HD stars but not 59 or 64 Eri. It is safest to regard the five-star version as a modern simplified asterism or a conflation with Jiǔliú, not as an established transcription of Kirch’s chart.

### Bayer and Flamsteed boundaries

The phrase “Bayer letters” needs qualification:

- Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* (1603) predates Sceptrum by 85 years and therefore contains **no Sceptrum constellation or Sceptrum Bayer letters**.
- Bode assigned lower-case letters within his own constellation. These are sometimes loosely called “Bayer designations,” but they are Bode-era catalogue lettering, not Bayer’s original 1603 nomenclature.
- Flamsteed’s numbering belongs to **Eridanus**, showing where these stars lay in the standard English catalogue tradition.
- With the extinction of the Sceptre, the Eridanus designations prevailed.

### Internal asterisms

No historically named sub-asterism inside Sceptrum was found. The Sceptre itself is better described as an asterism elevated by Kirch and Bode to constellation status.

### Deep-sky objects

**Negative finding.** No Messier object, NGC object, nebula, cluster, galaxy, or planetary nebula is historically named for *Sceptrum Brandenburgicum*. No object bears a standard “Sceptrum Nebula,” “Brandenburg Sceptre Cluster,” or equivalent name.

Eridanus as a whole contains such objects as NGC 1535, NGC 1407, NGC 1084, and IC 2118, but assigning all Eridanus objects retrospectively to the Sceptre would be misleading. The Sceptre had no official boundary, and these objects are not documented as parts of Kirch’s little figure.

### Novae and supernovae

**Negative finding.** No historical nova or supernova is recorded under a Sceptrum constellation designation. Modern transients in the same sky region are catalogued as objects in Eridanus or by survey coordinates. No securely documented naked-eye nova was found in the historical four-star figure.

### Meteor showers

**Negative finding.** There is no officially recognized Sceptrid or Brandenburg-Sceptrid meteor shower. The name did not survive in meteor nomenclature as Quadrans Muralis did in the Quadrantids.

### Astrology

No ancient astrological doctrine can attach to Sceptrum as such, because it was created long after the classical astrological corpus.

A modern astrological website assigns it approximately 4°–8° tropical Gemini and gives it royal or authoritative symbolism. This is a **modern invention**, derived by projecting the historical stars onto a precessing tropical zodiac. It is not found in Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*, Firmicus Maternus, Manilius, or the medieval Arabic astrologers.

None of the principal Behenian fixed stars is in the Sceptre. No standard Arabic or Indian lunar mansion is centered on its chief stars. Modern interpretive statements that “Sceptrum gives authority” are analogies from the object’s symbolism, not historical doctrine.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before 1600: stars without a Brandenburg Sceptre

**Documented fact.** The stars existed in catalogues and sky traditions only as anonymous or numbered stars of the River region or as members of non-Western asterisms. There is no Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, or Indian constellation corresponding to a Brandenburg sceptre.

### Classical antiquity: Eridanus

The surrounding figure was **Eridanus**, the celestial River. Ptolemy catalogued 34 stars in Eridanus in the *Almagest* (Book VII–VIII star catalogue), but exact one-to-one identifications of his fainter northern Eridanus stars with modern Flamsteed stars are sometimes disputed because of observational error, precession, manuscript corruption, and the density of possible candidates.

The Sceptre’s stars were not a separate Ptolemaic group. Consequently:

- there are no Sceptrum stars “lost” by Ptolemy to another constellation;
- Kirch carved his image from the visual territory of Eridanus and the starless-looking gap west of Lepus;
- modern claims of an ancient Sceptre ancestry confuse physical overlap with identity.

### 964: al-Ṣūfī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita* (*Book of the Fixed Stars*, 964) presents Ptolemy’s River as **ṣūrat al-nahr**, “the figure of the River.” Al-Ṣūfī also records indigenous Arabian star lore distinct from Ptolemy’s figure, including ostrich imagery farther down the River and **kursī al-jawzāʾ al-muqaddam**, the “front throne of al-Jawzāʾ,” for β, γ, δ Eri together with τ Ori.

**Negative finding.** No al-Ṣūfī name specifically corresponding to 53 Eri or Kirch’s Sceptre could be established. The better-known Bedouin “ostriches,” “eggs,” and “young ostriches” belong to broader or more southerly Eridanus/Piscis Austrinus lore and should not be transferred automatically to 53–54 Eri.

### 1515: Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s 1515 northern and southern celestial maps, prepared with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel, contain the classical Eridanus tradition but no Sceptre. The relevant stars remain within or beside the River. This is **documented absence**, expected because the invention lay 173 years in the future.

### 1603: Bayer

Bayer’s *Uranometria* depicts Eridanus and Lepus without Sceptrum. Bayer did not invent, name, or letter the Sceptre. Later references to “Bayer letters of Sceptrum” mean that Bode imitated the Bayer lettering system.

### 1627: Julius Schiller

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Augsburg, replaced Eridanus with:

> **Transitus Israel nempe per Mare Rubrum**  
> “The passage of Israel through the Red Sea.”

The same stars therefore formed part of the Red Sea and the Israelite crossing, not a saint holding a sceptre. Schiller’s atlas precedes Kirch, so it cannot contain Sceptrum itself. Andreas Cellarius reproduced Schiller’s Christianized system in *Harmonia Macrocosmica* (1660/1661), but the system never displaced classical nomenclature.

### 1688: Kirch’s invention

**First secure astronomical attestation.** Gottfried Kirch published **“Godofredi Kirchii Pomum Imperiale, et Sceptrum Brandenburgicum”** in the Leipzig journal *Acta Eruditorum* for 1688, p. 452. The bibliographical record is independently confirmed by the Gottfried Kirch correspondence edition and the Bremen handbook of early-modern calendar makers.

Kirch introduced two political constellations together:

- **Pomum Imperiale**, the Imperial Orb, honoring Emperor Leopold I;
- **Sceptrum Brandenburgicum**, honoring Brandenburg’s ruling authority.

The notice included a small chart. It placed the Sceptre in the bend of Eridanus west of Lepus.

### 1688: the honorand problem

Two historical interpretations compete.

1. **Frederick William, the Great Elector (1620–1688).**  
   A scholarly cartographic history calls it the sceptre of “Friedrich Wilhelm elector of Brandenburg.” Frederick William died on 29 April 1688 Gregorian/19 April Old Style. Joachim Feller’s separate funeral poem, *Sceptrum Brandenburgicum post Aquilam inter astra relatum*, was written for ceremonies commemorating him in September 1688. This establishes that “the Brandenburg Sceptre raised among the stars” was immediately intelligible as Great-Elector funerary imagery.

2. **Frederick III, the reigning elector from 1688, later Frederick I in Prussia.**  
   Ian Ridpath and John Barentine argue that the astronomical constellation more likely courted the new ruler. Kirch subsequently prepared calendars for Frederick III and in 1700 received appointment as astronomer to the newly established Brandenburg Society of Sciences.

**Conclusion: disputed.** The constellation unquestionably honored Brandenburg’s electoral house. The evidence does not justify converting that into “the province of Prussia.” Whether Kirch’s primary personal target was the dead Great Elector, the new Elector Frederick III, or the dynasty represented by both remains unresolved without a complete diplomatic transcription of Kirch’s notice and associated correspondence.

### 1688: Joachim Feller’s poem

Joachim Feller, Leipzig professor, librarian, and rector, issued the Latin occasional poem:

> *Sceptrum Brandenburgicum post Aquilam inter astra relatum*  
> “The Brandenburg Sceptre, carried among the stars behind the Eagle.”

Its long title explicitly commemorates Frederick William, Margrave and Elector of Brandenburg, hereditary Arch-Chamberlain of the Holy Roman Empire and ruler in Prussia, removed from earthly life in 1688. The surviving catalogue describes 16 leaves and Leipzig printing by Christoph Günther.

**Documented fact:** the poem exists and uses the same celestial-political metaphor in the same year.

**Open question:** whether Feller directly responded to Kirch’s newly published constellation, Kirch borrowed language from the funerary program, or both participated in a coordinated Leipzig commemoration has not been established from the catalogues alone.

### 1690–1770s: near-disappearance

The constellation was not adopted by Hevelius in the posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* (1690), nor did it enter the principal Flamsteed atlas tradition. It consequently spent most of the eighteenth century outside mainstream observational use.

It should not be listed as a Hevelius invention. Nor should it be confused with:

- Royer’s French royal **Sceptrum** of 1679 in the later Lacerta/Andromeda region;
- Hevelius’s Scutum Sobiescianum;
- Bode’s Honores Friderici.

### 1782: Bode’s revival

**Documented fact.** Johann Elert Bode restored the figure in the 1782 edition of his popular atlas *Vorstellung der Gestirne*. He labeled it **Die/Das Brandenburgische Scepter**.

Bode described a four-star nucleus: three fourth-magnitude stars and one fifth-magnitude star in an almost straight line between Orion, Eridanus, and Lepus. He credited Kirch but mistakenly dated the introduction to **1680**.

The revival occurred roughly 94 years after Kirch’s publication. “Ignored for nearly a century” is therefore substantially correct, although absolute absence from every intervening globe or manuscript cannot be proved.

### 1798: Van Keulen planisphere

A Gerard Hulst van Keulen planisphere is reported to show **Scepter** in this region. This is plausible evidence of diffusion through the Bode-derived atlas tradition, but the exact title and impression require further bibliographical checking: the form *Planisphaerium Coeleste* is inconsistently spelled in secondary sources.

### 1801: *Uranographia*

Bode’s monumental *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio*, published at Berlin at the author’s expense, depicts Sceptrum on Plate XVIII under Kirch’s Latin name **Sceptrum Brandenburgicum**.

The atlas contains 20 engraved plates and is accompanied by the bilingual German/French *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne / Description et connoissance générale des constellations*, cataloguing 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters.

Bode enlarged the visually simple four-star stick with faint catalogue stars and wrapped it in a grand ornamental sceptre. The image is political court iconography imposed upon a sparse region of Eridanus, not an attempt to reproduce a naturally obvious sceptre.

### 1810: William Croswell

William Croswell’s *Mercator Map of the Starry Heavens* reportedly labels **Sceptrum Brandenburgium, the Brandenburg Sceptre**. This is documented in modern atlas surveys, although the original sheet was not directly transcribed here.

### 1814: Piazzi

Giuseppe Piazzi’s Palermo catalogue tradition uses **Sceptrum** as a stellar name or constellation-derived designation associated with 53 Eri. This is the principal bridge between Bode’s extinct figure and the modern proper name of the star.

### 1818–1820: Bode’s later listing

Bode’s supplements continued to list **Brandenburgische Scepter**, demonstrating that he did not immediately withdraw the figure after *Uranographia*.

### 1822: Jamieson

Alexander Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* shows the ornate figure as **Sceptrum Brandenburgium** on the plate containing Orion, Lepus, Eridanus, and the Georgian Harp. Jamieson’s image is strongly indebted to Bode.

### 1830s–1870s: Burritt and American educational atlases

Elijah Hinsdale Burritt’s *Atlas Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens* included the upright Sceptre. Some impressions contain the typographical form **“Sceptrum Brakdenburgium”** or similarly corrupted lettering, an evident engraving or printing error rather than an alternative etymology.

Burritt helped prolong the image in American popular astronomy after professional catalogue makers had ceased to need it.

### c. 1850: movable planispheres

Aspin/Griffith/Wyld movable planisphere traditions reportedly include **Sceptrum Brandenburgium**. This shows late iconographic persistence in commercial rather than cutting-edge professional cartography.

### 1879: Gould and 54 Eri

Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* reports the variability of 54 Eri. It treats the star in Eridanus, not as an enduring Sceptrum constellation member. This illustrates the scientific nomenclature’s return to Eridanus even while decorative atlases could retain the Sceptre.

### 1899: Allen

Richard Hinckley Allen’s *Star-Names and Their Meanings* gives a compact historical notice:

- charted by Kirch in 1688;
- later rescued by Bode;
- four fourth- and fifth-magnitude stars;
- a straight north–south line below the River’s first bend;
- west of Lepus;
- Chinese **Kew Yew**, now transcribed Jiǔliú, in the same general region.

Allen also distinguished it from Royer’s other royal Sceptre.

### 1911: Olcott

William Tyler Olcott’s *Star Lore of All Ages* repeats the four-star description and notes Burritt’s upright depiction. By this stage the figure was antiquarian lore rather than a working scientific constellation.

### 1916–1925: Oosthoek encyclopaedia

The Dutch *Oosthoek* called it a constellation placed west of Orion by Kirch “about 1690,” already obsolete. The approximate date is secondary compression; 1688 is secure.

### 1922: IAU standardization

At its first General Assembly in Rome, the IAU standardized 88 constellations and abbreviations. Sceptrum was omitted. This is the operative point at which it ceased to have any possibility of official independent status.

### 1930: Delporte boundaries

Eugène Delporte’s *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* supplied boundaries, principally along hour circles and declination parallels for epoch B1875.0, for the 88 accepted constellations. Since Sceptrum was not among them, the entire Bode figure fell within the official boundary of **Eridanus**.

### 2017: partial restoration as a star name

On 30 June 2017, the IAU WGSN approved **Sceptrum** for 53 Eridani A. This did not revive the constellation. It canonized a historical residue of it as a proper stellar name.

### Present

Today Sceptrum Brandenburgicum survives in:

- historical atlases and facsimiles;
- obsolete-constellation catalogues;
- planetarium software and user-defined asterism lists;
- the official name Sceptrum for 53 Eri A;
- occasional modern astrology;
- historical discussions of “political constellations” and Prussian scientific patronage.

## Invention and reception

### Gottfried Kirch

Gottfried Kirch (1639–1710) was born at Guben and worked as teacher, calendar maker, observational astronomer, and instrument maker. He studied or trained in circles associated with Erhard Weigel and Johannes Hevelius.

His astronomical work included:

- systematic telescopic comet searches;
- the first comet discovery made with a telescope, C/1680 V1;
- discovery or early observation of M11 and M5;
- discovery of the variability of χ Cygni;
- ephemerides, calendars, weather records, and correspondence networks.

Kirch’s invented constellations were political:

- electoral Saxon swords;
- Pomum Imperiale for Emperor Leopold I;
- Sceptrum Brandenburgicum for Brandenburg rule;
- Corona Hungarica in later calendar material.

They were not successful as permanent constellations.

### Maria Margaretha Kirch and the Berlin Academy

Maria Margaretha Kirch, née Winkelmann (1670–1720), married Gottfried in **1692**, four years after the Sceptre’s publication. She therefore did not co-invent the 1688 constellation.

She was nevertheless central to the later Kirch astronomical household. Institutional and correspondence records document her work in:

- observations of planets, comets, aurorae, sunspots, and weather;
- ephemerides and calendar calculations;
- collaboration with Gottfried and their children;
- discovery of the comet of 1702.

In 1700 Frederick III appointed Gottfried Kirch astronomer to the newly founded Brandenburg Society of Sciences in Berlin. Maria worked with him but was not elected as a member. After his death she unsuccessfully sought to continue his official position.

The phrase “Kirch and Maria Margaretha Kirch, the Berlin Academy’s first astronomers” therefore requires correction:

- **Gottfried** was formally appointed the Society’s first astronomer.
- **Maria** was a practicing astronomer and indispensable collaborator, but the institution denied her equivalent official membership or succession.
- Their joint Berlin work belongs to the constellation’s patronage afterlife, not its original 1688 invention.

### Patronage and possible reward

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Some writers connect Kirch’s flattering Sceptre with his appointment by Frederick III in 1700. The sequence is suggestive, but no document found states that the constellation caused the appointment. Kirch had abundant independent qualifications: comet work, ephemerides, calendar production, and an international reputation.

Calling the constellation “shameless flattery” is an interpretive judgment in modern scholarship, not a phrase from Kirch. In seventeenth-century science, dedicating discoveries and celestial figures to rulers was a normal strategy for obtaining patronage.

### Bode and the “Prussian sky”

Bode’s revival belongs to a broader late-eighteenth-century proliferation of dynastic and commemorative figures. In the same atlas culture appear:

- **Honores Friderici / Friedrichs Ehre**, honoring Frederick the Great;
- Cor Caroli;
- Robur Carolinum;
- Scutum Sobiescianum;
- Taurus Poniatovii;
- Harpa Georgii;
- Telescopium Herschelii.

Sceptrum Brandenburgicum and Honores Friderici form a recognizable **Prussian courtly layer** in Bode’s sky, but they are distinct:

- Sceptrum: near Eridanus and Lepus; Kirch, 1688; Brandenburg dynasty.
- Honores Friderici: near Andromeda/Lacerta; Bode, 1787; Frederick the Great.

Confusing them is a recurrent error, worsened by the existence of Royer’s unrelated French Sceptre in the Honores Friderici/Lacerta region.

## Myths and legends by culture

## The Brandenburg Sceptre itself

**Modern invention; no classical myth.** No Greek or Roman source mentions Sceptrum Brandenburgicum. It has no catasterism in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus;
- the scholia to Aratus or Germanicus;
- Germanicus’ *Aratea*;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias;
- late-antique mythographers.

Accordingly, nobody in classical myth “placed the Brandenburg Sceptre in the sky.” Gottfried Kirch did so in 1688 as a political honor.

### Greek sceptres are not antecedents

Greek literature contains many divine and royal sceptres — Zeus’s sceptre, Agamemnon’s inherited staff in *Iliad* 2, heralds’ staffs, and symbols of judicial authority — but no evidence links any of them to Kirch’s stars. Treating Zeus’s or Agamemnon’s sceptre as the “myth” of this constellation is a modern associative invention.

## Eridanus myths occupying the same sky

These myths belong to Eridanus, not Sceptrum, but they formed the inherited Western interpretation displaced locally by Kirch.

### Phaethon and the celestial River

**Classical myth.** Phaethon, son of Helios, obtained permission to drive the solar chariot, lost control, scorched the earth, and was killed by Zeus’s thunderbolt. He fell into the River Eridanus.

Early literary components appear in archaic and classical Greek poetry; the fullest surviving Latin telling is Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1.747–2.400. Hyginus summarizes the catastrophe in *Fabulae* 152A and relates the celestial River in *Poetica Astronomica* 2.32. Nonnus retells Phaethon material in the *Dionysiaca*, Books 38–40.

In catasteristic tradition the River was identified with the stream receiving Phaethon. Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* 37 and the Epitome discuss Eridanus and its relation to the solar accident.

### Cycnus mourning Phaethon

**Classical myth.** Cycnus mourned Phaethon beside the River and was changed into a swan. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 2.367–380, gives the best-known account. Hyginus, *Fabulae* 154, preserves variants involving different figures named Cycnus. This story connects Eridanus to Cygnus, but not to the Brandenburg Sceptre.

### The Heliades and amber

**Classical myth.** Phaethon’s sisters, the Heliades, mourned him and became poplar trees; their tears hardened into amber in or beside Eridanus. See Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 2.340–366; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 154; and Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 4.596–626 for the eerie amber-bearing region.

### Eridanus as a terrestrial river

**Ancient learned tradition, disputed geography.** Greek and Roman writers variously associated Eridanus with:

- the Po in northern Italy;
- a mythical far-western or northern river;
- the Nile;
- Oceanus;
- a celestial river without exact terrestrial identity.

Herodotus, *Histories* 3.115, expresses skepticism about stories of a northern river Eridanus from which amber came. Strabo, *Geography* 5.1.9, discusses Eridanus/Po traditions. Virgil invokes Eridanus in *Georgics* 1.482 and *Aeneid* 6.659.

None of these texts isolates Kirch’s four stars as a sceptre.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian

**Negative finding.** No recognized Babylonian constellation has been securely equated with the compact group 46–54 Eri that became Sceptrum. Mesopotamian star lists contain river, god, and animal figures, but a claim that the Brandenburg Sceptre descends from one would require a star-by-star identification not found in the consulted scholarship.

### Egyptian

**Negative finding.** No pharaonic Egyptian sceptre constellation can be securely placed at 53–54 Eri. Egyptian royal iconography contains *was*-sceptres and crooks, but iconographic resemblance is not astronomical continuity.

### Arabic and Persian

Al-Ṣūfī’s documented constellation name is **ṣūrat al-nahr**, “the figure of the River.” The Persian astronomer was transmitting and correcting Ptolemy while also recording Arabic vernacular asterisms.

The better-attested Bedouin imagery around Eridanus includes:

- **kursī al-jawzāʾ al-muqaddam**, the front throne of al-Jawzāʾ, at the head of Eridanus and Orion’s foot;
- **al-ẓalīm**, a male ostrich, for a far-southern star;
- **al-riʾāl / firākh al-naʿām**, young ostriches, in the southern-river/Southern-Fish region;
- eggs and nesting-place imagery reflected in later star-name discussions.

**Negative finding.** None was demonstrated to be a specific historical Arabic name for 53 Eri. “Sceptrum” is Latin and early modern.

### Chinese: Jiǔliú

The relevant indigenous Chinese asterism is **九斿 / 九旒, Jiǔliú**, commonly translated “Nine Pennons,” “Nine Banners,” or the tassels/streamers of an imperial military flag.

Modern star identifications include:

- μ Eri — Jiǔliú 2;
- ω Eri — Jiǔliú 3;
- 63 Eri — Jiǔliú 4;
- 64 Eri — Jiǔliú 5;
- 60 Eri — Jiǔliú 6;
- 58 Eri — Jiǔliú 7;
- 54 Eri — Jiǔliú 8.

Later “added stars” include:

- 45 Eri;
- 62 Eri;
- **53 Eri — 九斿增四, Jiǔliú zēng sì**, “fourth added star”;
- 59 Eri;
- HD 29613.

Thus the Sceptre overlaps significantly with Jiǔliú, especially at 54 and 58 Eri, while 53 Eri belongs to the later supplementary structure.

Allen’s spelling **Kew Yew** and translation “nine scallops of a pennon” reflect nineteenth-century romanization and interpretation. Jiǔliú is not a Chinese translation of Kirch’s Sceptre; it is an independent asterism whose military-regalia symbolism happens to be strikingly adjacent.

### Indian

**Negative finding.** No standard nakṣatra is centered on 53 or 54 Eri. The group lies south of the ecliptic and outside the defining-star sequence of the 27/28 lunar mansions. Broader Indian identifications of Eridanus are inconsistent and do not establish an indigenous sceptre in these stars.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

**Negative finding.** No secure Jewish astronomical or Kabbalistic source was found that names the 53–54 Eri group as a sceptre. General efforts to correlate constellations with tribes of Israel, Hebrew letters, biblical objects, or the Tree of Life are later symbolic systems and do not supply an attested pre-Kirch identity.

Schiller’s Red Sea is Christian biblical uranography, not evidence of an older Hebrew constellation.

### Christianized sky

As noted, Julius Schiller made Eridanus the Red Sea crossed by Israel. Because his atlas was published in 1627, it mapped the future Sceptre stars within **Transitus Israel nempe per Mare Rubrum**. Cellarius later reproduced Schiller’s Christian heavens.

No saint or isolated Christian sceptre replaced Kirch’s constellation specifically.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

**Negative finding.** No premodern alchemical or Hermetic text was found treating Sceptrum Brandenburgicum as a celestial symbol. A sceptre can be used generically for royal power, sulfuric kingship, dominion, or the alchemical king, but applying those themes to Kirch’s stars without a source is modern speculation.

## Boundary history

### Ptolemy

The region belonged to or adjoined Eridanus. Ptolemy used descriptive stellar positions rather than polygonal boundaries. The stars were not enclosed by a legal celestial border.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer retained Eridanus as a long pictorial river. There was still no empty bounded patch called Sceptrum. Later observers could assign stars near or within the River’s illustrated body differently because figures overlapped and boundaries were conventional.

### Schiller, 1627

The same Bayer-based stars became part of the Red Sea crossing scene. This was iconographic replacement, not a geometrical boundary revision.

### Kirch, 1688

Kirch extracted several stars from the visual territory between the River, Orion, and Lepus and drew an independent sceptre. Since no international boundary system existed, “taking stars from Eridanus” meant relabeling them, not transferring legally defined sky area.

### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius did not consolidate the Sceptre. His failure to adopt it substantially limited its diffusion.

### Flamsteed, 1725/1729

The stars were catalogued under Eridanus, furnishing the Flamsteed numbers by which the principal members remain known.

### Bode, 1782 and 1801

Bode revived Kirch’s group, lettered additional faint stars, and bent Eridanus’s pictorial course around or alongside the sceptre. Sceptrum consequently occupied space “inside” what other atlases simply treated as the River.

### Nineteenth century

Decorative atlases could preserve Sceptrum, while positional catalogues increasingly returned its stars to Eridanus. No universally accepted boundary was ever drawn.

### IAU/Delporte

The 1922 list recognized Eridanus and rejected Sceptrum by omission. Delporte’s 1930 boundary placed all of the contested sky inside Eridanus. Sceptrum lost no territory to Lepus in the final allocation; the principal reconstructed members are all Eri stars.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas

**Documented absence.** The Farnese Atlas, a Roman marble celestial globe generally dated to the second century CE and preserving Greco-Roman constellation imagery, shows the ancient River tradition but cannot contain Sceptrum. No sceptre west of Lepus can be identified as Kirch’s figure.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

Manuscripts of al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* show paired views of **al-Nahr**, the River, often one as seen on a globe and one as seen in the sky. They contain no Brandenburg object. The relevant value is evidence for the pre-Kirch River iconography.

### Dürer, 1515

Dürer’s engraved maps retain Eridanus. Again, no independent sceptre appears.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer shows Eridanus flowing from near Orion toward the south. The future Sceptre space is part of the River landscape.

### Schiller, 1627

The River becomes the Red Sea and Israelite crossing. Schiller produced both globe-view and earth-view versions; the reversal of orientation must be considered when comparing star placements.

### Cellarius, 1660/1661

Cellarius’s *Harmonia Macrocosmica* reproduces Schiller’s Christian constellation system in hemisphere plates, preserving the Red Sea interpretation but not the future Brandenburg Sceptre.

### Kirch, 1688

Kirch’s *Acta Eruditorum* notice contains the first astronomical image. The small figure is less elaborate than Bode’s later royal ornament and is essential for determining the original membership.

### Feller, Leipzig, 1688

Feller’s funerary poem is textual iconography rather than an atlas: the Brandenburg sceptre is “carried among the stars behind the Eagle.” It connects celestial elevation, the Brandenburg eagle, dynastic succession, and the Great Elector’s death.

### Bode, 1782

Bode’s popular atlas restores the Sceptre as a conspicuous upright implement labeled in German.

### Bode, 1801

Plate XVIII of *Uranographia* gives the canonical image: a tall ceremonial sceptre with ornamental head and shaft, surrounded by Eridanus’s coils. The plate was engraved for Bode’s large, scientifically dense but artistically traditional atlas.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s plate shows **Sceptrum Brandenburgium** with Orion, Lepus, Eridanus, and Harpa Georgii. Its style follows Bode but is adapted for an English popular atlas.

### Burritt, United States, nineteenth century

Burritt shows the Sceptre standing upright. Repeated editions and engraving variants helped preserve it in popular American star lore.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/25

**Negative finding.** Although the card set is closely related to Jamieson and includes many obsolete figures, no source consulted established a dedicated or clearly labeled Brandenburg Sceptre on the relevant card. Claims that it is certainly present should be verified against a high-resolution original card rather than inferred from Jamieson.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, calendars

- **Coins:** Brandenburg and Prussian coins frequently display sceptres and eagles, but no coin was found that demonstrably portrays Kirch’s constellation rather than ordinary regalia.
- **Ceilings and portals:** no securely identified architectural Sceptrum Brandenburgicum was found.
- **Calendars:** Kirch’s occupation as a calendar maker and the later Academy calendar monopoly are well documented, but a specific calendar illustration of this constellation requires further investigation.
- **Funerary print:** Feller’s 1688 poem is the strongest surviving non-atlas cultural artifact tied directly to the phrase.

## People, events, and works

### Frederick William, the Great Elector

Frederick William transformed Brandenburg-Prussia’s military and administrative power. His death in 1688 supplied the immediate context for Feller’s *Sceptrum Brandenburgicum post Aquilam inter astra relatum*. Whether Kirch’s chart was specifically funerary remains disputed, but Great-Elector commemoration is securely attached to the phrase in 1688.

### Frederick III / Frederick I in Prussia

Frederick III succeeded in 1688 and crowned himself Frederick I, King in Prussia, in 1701. He founded or chartered the Brandenburg Society of Sciences and appointed Kirch as astronomer in 1700. He is therefore a plausible immediate patron represented by the continuing Brandenburg sceptre.

### Leibniz and the Brandenburg Society

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz drafted the Society’s institutional plans and became its president. The Society’s calendar privilege helped fund it. Sceptrum belongs culturally to the same conjunction of court prestige, learned institutions, and astronomical patronage, although Leibniz was not its inventor.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode rescued the figure from obscurity, first in a popular German atlas and then in the most lavish late pictorial star atlas. Without Bode, Kirch’s single-page invention might have remained a bibliographical curiosity.

### Explorers and navigation

**Negative finding.** No explorer or navigator is documented as steering by “Sceptrum Brandenburgicum.” The stars are relatively faint and lack the navigational importance of Polaris, Sirius, Canopus, or Achernar. If used in navigation, they would have been identified through Eridanus or coordinate catalogues.

### Literary and artistic works

The principal work explicitly bearing the name is Joachim Feller’s Latin funeral poem of 1688. No major canonical poem, painting, opera, symphony, or building named for the constellation was found.

### Ships and missions

**Negative finding.** No well-documented naval vessel, spacecraft, astronomical mission, or observatory was found named *Sceptrum Brandenburgicum*. Modern businesses and fictional uses of the phrase do not establish an astronomical tradition.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was it invented in 1680 or 1688?

- **1688:** secured by Kirch’s publication in *Acta Eruditorum*, p. 452.
- **1680:** printed in Bode’s later German description.
- **Finding:** Bode’s 1680 is erroneous. It may have arisen from a dropped final digit, confusion with Kirch’s comet discovery, or a source-copying error. No 1680 Sceptrum publication was found.

### 2. Whom did it honor?

- **Documented minimum:** Brandenburg electoral/dynastic rule.
- **Great Elector:** supported by Feller’s 1688 funeral poem and some scholarly cartographic histories.
- **Frederick III:** supported as a patronage reconstruction by Ridpath and Barentine and by Kirch’s later employment.
- **“Province of Prussia”:** inaccurate simplification. Brandenburg was an electorate; Prussia was a distinct possession in dynastic union.
- **Finding:** dynasty certain; individual honorand disputed.

### 3. Was Kirch already a “Prussian Royal Society astronomer” in 1688?

No. The Brandenburg Society of Sciences was founded in 1700, and Kirch was then appointed its astronomer. Describing the 1688 inventor as already holding that office is anachronistic.

### 4. Did Maria Margaretha Kirch co-invent it?

No evidence. She married Kirch in 1692. Her later status as his astronomical collaborator is documented, but attaching her to the 1688 invention is chronologically unsupported.

### 5. Four stars, five stars, or nine?

- Bode’s prose: four conspicuous stars.
- Allen: four.
- Bode’s lettered catalogue reconstruction: nine stars.
- Some modern asterism lists: five, including 64–60–59–58–54 Eri.
- **Finding:** four is the visible nucleus; nine is Bode’s expanded catalogue figure; the five-star Jiǔliú-like chain is a questionable modern reconstruction.

### 6. Is 53 Eri really the brightest?

Yes, at combined magnitude about 3.87. Some sources quote component A at 4.02, which can make comparisons appear inconsistent. The discrepancy is between component and combined light, not a different star.

### 7. Is “p Sceptri” rho Sceptri?

No. Bode’s lower-case Roman **p** is sometimes visually or typographically transformed into Greek **ρ** (“rho”) in modern summaries. This is a likely source of the form “Rho Sceptri.” Bode’s letter series was not necessarily a Greek Bayer alphabet.

### 8. Brandenburgicum or Brandenburgium?

**Brandenburgicum** is Kirch’s and Bode’s principal Latin form and agrees with neuter *sceptrum*. **Brandenburgium** appears in later atlases, particularly Jamieson-derived English usage. It is a historical variant, not the original form.

### 9. Sceptrum Brandenburgicum versus Sceptrum et Manus Iustitiae

They are different constellations:

- Kirch’s Brandenburg Sceptre: Eridanus/Lepus region, 1688.
- Royer’s French royal Sceptre: Lacerta/Andromeda region, 1679.

Modern sources sometimes call Royer’s figure *Sceptrum et Manus Iustitiae*, but recent source criticism argues that Royer’s chart and catalogue simply use *Sceptrum*, while his introduction explains the Sceptre and Hand of Justice symbolism. The expanded name may be a twentieth-century scholarly construction.

### 10. Sceptrum versus Honores Friderici

Also distinct. Bode’s Honores Friderici occupied a northern region near Andromeda and Lacerta and honored Frederick the Great. Confusing it with Sceptrum collapses two separate Prussian commemorative constellations.

### 11. Did the IAU “abolish it in 1922”?

Substantively yes, procedurally imprecise. The IAU approved the canonical 88 and their abbreviations. It did not need to vote separately on every discarded atlas figure. Delporte’s 1930 boundaries completed the territorial standardization.

### 12. Did the IAU revive it in 2017?

No. The IAU approved **Sceptrum** as a star name for 53 Eri A. The constellation remains obsolete.

### 13. Astrological antiquity

Claims that the Sceptre has ancient fixed-star effects are unsupported. Any horoscope interpretation under that name is modern and post-Kirch.

### 14. Hoaxes

No specific historical hoax involving the constellation was located. The main problems are cumulative copying errors, conflation with Royer’s sceptre, incorrect institutional titles, and retroactive assignment of ancient lore.

## Open questions

1. A new diplomatic transcription and translation of Kirch’s complete one-page 1688 notice is needed to settle its exact dedication language and original star list.
2. Kirch’s original engraving should be astrometrically overlaid on a precessed modern chart to distinguish its four-star core from Bode’s later enlargement.
3. The relation between Kirch’s publication and Joachim Feller’s September 1688 funeral poem remains unclear.
4. Bode’s source for the erroneous date **1680** has not been identified.
5. The earliest occurrence of the variant **Sceptrum Brandenburgium** needs bibliographical tracing.
6. Piazzi’s exact catalogue entry should be transcribed from the original edition to determine whether *Sceptrum* functioned as a proper name, constellation label, or abbreviated positional description.
7. The claimed 1798 Van Keulen planisphere and c. 1850 movable planisphere deserve direct examination.
8. A high-resolution comparison of Jamieson, Burritt, and *Urania’s Mirror* could establish whether the last contains an unlabeled portion of the figure.
9. The modern five-star sequence 64–60–59–58–54 Eri should be traced to its first publication; it appears to conflate Sceptrum with Chinese Jiǔliú.
10. No origin was found for the “830 stars in 109 groups” phrase in the supplied brief.
11. No historical Hebrew, Kabbalistic, Hermetic, Indian, navigational, architectural, or musical use specific to the Sceptre could be documented.
