# Lacerta: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented fact** — directly attested in a primary text, atlas, catalogue, institutional database, or contemporary report.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern identification or historical conclusion based on comparison of catalogues, coordinates, and images.
- **Tradition** — a transmitted cultural interpretation whose historical existence is documented, although it is not a modern scientific identification.
- **Disputed claim** — conflicting sources or identifications exist.
- **Unverified claim** — repeated in secondary literature but not traceable here to adequate primary evidence.
- **Legend/myth** — traditional narrative, not an empirical historical assertion.
- **Modern invention** — a newly created name, image, or story rather than an inherited ancient tradition.

## Basic identification

### Status

**Kept.** Lacerta is one of the 88 constellations recognized by the International Astronomical Union.

- Official Latin nominative: **Lacerta**
- Official Latin genitive: **Lacertae**
- IAU abbreviation: **Lac**
- English: **the Lizard**
- Modern conventional German: **die Eidechse**
- Spanish: **el Lagarto** or **la Lagartija**, with *Lagarto* common in astronomical lists
- French: **le Lézard**
- Italian: **la Lucertola**
- Polish: **Jaszczurka**
- Modern Hebrew: **לטאה**, *leta’ah*, “lizard”
- Modern Persian: **سوسمار**, *sūsmār*, “lizard,” or translations using **مارمولک**, *mārmūlak*

These Persian and Hebrew expressions are modern translations, not recovered ancient constellation names.

The IAU’s 1922 General Assembly established a standard list and abbreviations; it did **not** yet create the exact modern borders. Eugène Delporte prepared the boundaries at the IAU’s request after proposals presented in 1925. They were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* in 1930. The lines were defined along right ascension and declination for epoch B1875.0. [IAU history and list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/), [Delporte bibliographical record](https://books.google.com/books/about/D%C3%A9limitation_scientifique_des_constella.html?id=v3XvAAAAMAAJ)

Thus the often encountered formula “the IAU created the boundaries at Rome in 1922” is **incorrect**. Rome standardized the constellation system; Delporte’s borders came later.

### Position and extent

Lacerta is a small northern constellation in a Milky Way field between:

- Cygnus to the west;
- Cepheus to the north;
- Cassiopeia and Andromeda to the east;
- Pegasus to the south.

Its official area is about **201 square degrees**, ranking approximately 68th among the 88 constellations. Its brightest star is only magnitude 3.76, explaining why this conspicuous gap in the inherited Greco-Roman constellation system remained available for seventeenth-century invention.

### Meaning and etymology

**Lacerta** is ordinary Latin for a lizard. The genitive is *lacertae*. It is unrelated to Latin *lacertus*, “upper arm” or “muscular limb,” although the resemblance has occasionally encouraged fanciful etymology.

Hevelius’s full expression was:

> **Lacerta sive Stellio** — “The Lizard, or Stellion.”

Latin *stellio* denoted a spotted lizard or gecko-like reptile. Ancient and medieval explanations connected it with *stella*, “star,” because of star-like spots. A sixteenth-century lexicon describes the animal as *parva lacerta, atris stellata corpora guttis*, approximately “a little lizard whose body is starred with black spots.” [Faber and Gesner, 1571](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/faber_gessner1571/1033)

Hevelius’s introductory description reportedly characterized the creature as a small animal of many colors, seemingly decorated with little stars. This makes the spotted-lizard interpretation substantially better grounded than the claim that *Stellio* meant a newt. Zoological terminology was not stable, however, so an exact modern species identification—often given as the starred agama, *Stellagama stellio*, formerly *Laudakia stellio*—is a **scholarly reconstruction**, not a taxonomic statement made by Hevelius. [Hevelius atlas guide](https://meteo-maarssen.nl/lib_hevelius_1690.html), [discussion of the ambiguity](https://www2.arnes.si/~ucotar/astro/Sky%26Telescope/2025/aas_st_202510.pdf)

### What stars make the visual figure?

There is no single official IAU stick figure. The IAU defines Lacerta as an area, not a required pattern. Common modern outlines connect a narrow zigzag through some combination of:

- α Lacertae;
- β Lacertae;
- 1 Lacertae;
- 2 Lacertae;
- 4 Lacertae;
- 5 Lacertae;
- sometimes 11 Lacertae.

The northern five-star zigzag resembles a much fainter Cassiopeian W or M. Hence the informal asterism or nickname **“Little Cassiopeia.”** The expression is widespread in twentieth- and twenty-first-century observing literature, but this investigation did not locate a secure nineteenth-century first use. It should therefore be treated as a **modern informal tradition**, not an ancient name. [Modern discussion and historical image](https://astrohum.org/newsletters/newsletter_2023_d.pdf)

### Bayer and Flamsteed systems

Lacerta did not exist when Johann Bayer published the *Uranometria* in 1603. Its Greek-letter designations are later “Bayer-style” assignments rather than labels printed by Bayer himself.

Only two Greek letters are conventionally used:

- **α Lacertae = 7 Lacertae**
- **β Lacertae = 3 Lacertae**

The established Flamsteed sequence is:

1, 2, 3 (= β), 4, 5, 6, 7 (= α), 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 and 16 Lacertae.

The numbers do not appear as such in the official 1725 *Historia Coelestis Britannica*. They entered common use through Joseph-Jérôme de Lalande’s later numbered edition of Flamsteed’s catalogue. “Flamsteed number” is therefore conventional shorthand, not proof that Flamsteed personally printed each numeral. [Digitized Flamsteed catalogue analysis](https://pbarbier.com/flamsteed/flamsteed.html)

### Boundary history and transfers

#### Ptolemy and al-Ṣūfī

**Documented absence:** Lacerta was not one of Ptolemy’s 48 constellations and consequently does not appear in al-Ṣūfī’s constellation sequence. The region lay among the outskirts of Cepheus, Cygnus, Pegasus and Andromeda, with many of its faint stars simply uncatalogued or treated as unformed stars associated with those figures.

#### Bayer, 1603

Bayer had no Lacerta. Stars later incorporated into the Lizard remained anonymous background points or lay visually among the adjacent figures. Since Bayer’s maps were pictorial rather than bordered territorial divisions, it is anachronistic to say that Lacerta “took a fixed area” from Bayer’s constellations.

#### Royer, 1679

Augustin Royer placed **Sceptrum et Manus Iustitiae**, “the Sceptre and Hand of Justice,” in this general region in honor of Louis XIV. It drew upon stars now divided between Lacerta and western Andromeda. Because Royer’s and Hevelius’s constructions were pictorial and lacked Delporte-type borders, statements that one constellation contained exactly the same stars as the other are too strong. There was substantial overlap, not identity.

#### Hevelius, prepared by 1687; published 1690

Hevelius laid the long, narrow animal into the faint space between Cygnus and Andromeda/Cepheus. His catalogue contains **10 Lacerta entries**, not 16. Modern machine-readable comparison finds no Ptolemaic, Tycho, Ulugh Beg, Riccioli, or Landgrave-of-Hesse predecessors assigned to those ten entries, underscoring that it was a new European grouping. [Verbunt and van Gent’s catalogue study](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2010/08/aa14003-10.pdf), [digital Hevelius catalogue](https://pbarbier.com/hevelius/hevelius.html)

A particularly important complication is that stars around the Lizard’s head overlap the area in which Hevelius drew the elaborate turban or headdress of Cepheus. Later compilers could therefore assign nearby points differently. Modern claims that α, β, and 9 Lacertae were all Hevelius’s original “head” stars require star-by-star checking; at least α and β belong to the later expanded Lacerta scheme, not automatically to the ten-star Hevelian catalogue.

#### Flamsteed and Lalande

Flamsteed accepted Lacerta as one of the newer northern constellations attributed to Hevelius, and the later numbered catalogue expanded its recognizable inventory to 16 stars. The constellation thus gained stars beyond Hevelius’s original ten, principally from the previously unformed or loosely allocated space around Cepheus, Andromeda and Cygnus.

#### Bode

Bode retained Lacerta but also placed his royal memorial **Honores Friderici** immediately to its east/southeast in the former chain and arm region of Andromeda. Consequently, Bode did not simply rename all Lacerta as Frederick’s Honors. His plate displays both as distinct neighboring figures.

#### Delporte, 1930

Delporte turned the traditional figure into a closed territorial polygon. Its official neighbors became Andromeda, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, Cygnus and Pegasus. Stars inside that polygon are now “in Lacerta” whether or not Hevelius ever drew them on the reptile.

The obsolete Sceptre’s stars now fall mainly in Lacerta and Andromeda. The stars of *Honores Friderici* fall principally in Andromeda, with its western fringe adjacent to Lacerta. Lacerta itself was not abolished and therefore did not “lose all its stars” to another constellation.

### Stellio

**Documented modern name:** The IAU Working Group on Star Names approved **Stellio** for α Lacertae on 29 August 2024. It derives from Hevelius’s alternative constellation title, not from an ancient Arabic star name. Accordingly, older books that say Lacerta has “no named star” were accurate when written but are now out of date. [IAU Working Group on Star Names](https://iau.org/WG280/WG280/Home.aspx)

**Contested zoological gloss:** Calling the star “the newt” is possible only under an expansive early-modern use of reptile/amphibian vocabulary. “Spotted lizard” or “stellion” is philologically and historically safer.

### Chinese-derived names

Modern Chinese mappings assign Lacerta’s stars among several indigenous asterisms:

- **Téngshé**, 螣蛇, “Flying Serpent”;
- **Chǔ**, 杵, “Pestle”;
- **Chēfǔ**, 車府, “Carriage Yard” or “Repository for Chariots.”

In one common mapping:

- 1 Lacertae = 杵一, **Chǔyī**, First Star of the Pestle;
- 15 Lacertae, 11 Lacertae and 2 Lacertae are the first three stars of Chēfǔ;
- α Lacertae, β Lacertae, 4 Lacertae and 9 Lacertae participate in Téngshé.

These are modern equivalences between Western and Chinese catalogues, not evidence that Chinese astronomers recognized a European “Lizard.” [Lacerta in Chinese astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacerta_in_Chinese_astronomy), [Chinese star-name table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Chinese_star_names)

The IAU name **Tengshe** has been applied to V424 Lacertae, near the Andromeda border, in recognition of the old Chinese asterism. This differs from the later Chinese convention in which α Lacertae may be numbered “Téngshé One.” The discrepancy reflects changing historical reconstructions of the asterism’s leading star, not two identical proper names. [Ridpath’s summary](https://ianridpath.com/startales/lacerta.html), [Tengshe historical discussion](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Tengshe)

### Important variable and peculiar stars

#### BL Lacertae

**Documented fact:** Cuno Hoffmeister identified the object as a variable star in 1929. Because it looked stellar, it received the ordinary variable-star designation **BL Lacertae**.

John L. Schmitt identified it in 1968 with the variable radio source VRO 42.22.01 and detected surrounding nebulosity. Its rapid optical and radio variation, polarization, strong nonthermal continuum and nearly featureless spectrum made it clear that it was not an ordinary Galactic variable. [Photometric-history paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/2281070a0), [1969 rapid-radio report](https://www.nature.com/articles/223598a0)

Oke and Gunn subsequently measured a host-galaxy redshift near **z = 0.07**, confirming its extragalactic character. BL Lacertae became the prototype of the **BL Lac objects**, a class of jet-dominated active galactic nuclei viewed approximately down the jet axis. BL Lac objects are now treated as one branch of the broader **blazar** category.

Thus a minor seventeenth-century constellation supplied the name for a major twentieth-century class of active galaxies.

#### EV Lacertae

EV Lac is a nearby M dwarf roughly 16–17 light-years away and a powerful flare star. Its energetic magnetic outbursts have sometimes generated misleading popular headlines implying that the star itself became naked-eye bright. What can briefly become very strong is its high-energy emission; ordinary visual naked-eye visibility is not established for every reported flare.

#### 10 Lacertae

10 Lacertae is an O9 V star and a long-used spectral standard. It is associated with the wider **Lacerta OB1** stellar association. Modern work has questioned whether all stars historically collected under “Lac OB1” constitute one simple, coeval physical association; membership and subgroup boundaries are therefore matters of ongoing stellar-kinematic reconstruction. [Study of Lacerta OB1](https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.0443)

#### 12 Lacertae / DD Lacertae

A well-studied multiperiodic β Cephei pulsator. Asteroseismic campaigns have identified several radial and non-radial modes and used them to constrain internal mixing and core overshooting. [Asteroseismic study](https://arxiv.org/abs/0903.5477)

#### Babcock’s Star

HD 215441, commonly called **Babcock’s Magnetic Star**, is an exceptionally magnetic chemically peculiar A star. The informal name honors Horace W. Babcock, who reported its remarkable field. It is not an IAU-approved traditional proper name.

#### SS Lacertae

SS Lac is an eclipsing binary in the field of NGC 7209. Its eclipses progressively weakened and disappeared as nodal precession changed the orbital orientation. It is a valuable demonstration that a star can cease to appear eclipsing without the binary system ceasing to exist.

### Novae

#### DI Lacertae — Nova Lacertae 1910

**Documented fact:** A classical nova erupted in 1910 and later received the variable designation DI Lacertae. It remains an old-nova cataclysmic binary. [Modern study of DI Lacertae](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0008455)

#### CP Lacertae — Nova Lacertae 1936

The brightest recorded Lacertan nova erupted in June 1936, reaching about magnitude 2.1. Discovery credit is complicated:

- contemporary notices report independent observations by several people;
- a *Nature* notice highlighted Dr. Nielsen aboard the *Strathaird* during the 19 June eclipse expedition;
- later accounts credit Leslie Peltier, E. Loreta, Włodzimierz Zonn, Kazuaki Gomi and sometimes Záviš Bochníček.

The safest formulation is **multiple independent discovery**, with priority dependent on exact observation and reporting times. [Contemporary *Nature* notice](https://www.nature.com/articles/1371064d0)

Claims that one named observer was unambiguously “the discoverer” are therefore **disputed or incomplete**. The nova faded by roughly three magnitudes in nine days and is classed as a fast nova. A supposed pre-eruption brightening remains doubtful because some early photographic measures may concern a nearby star.

#### DK Lacertae — Nova Lacertae 1950

DK Lac reached approximately fifth magnitude in 1950 and survives as an old nova. Together with DI and CP Lac, it accounts for the traditional statement that three conspicuous twentieth-century novae occurred in Lacerta.

### Supernovae

No securely recorded naked-eye Galactic supernova is known in Lacerta. Modern surveys have, of course, discovered supernovae in distant galaxies inside the IAU polygon, but none is historically foundational for the constellation.

A common source of confusion is **Cassiopeia A**, whose supernova remnant lies in Cassiopeia, not Lacerta, despite the neighboring boundaries.

### Multiple stars and small asterisms

- **Roe 47** is a telescopic multiple-star grouping in southern Lacerta. The “47” is a discoverer/catalogue designation, not a Flamsteed number.
- **8 Lacertae** is a visually attractive multiple system.
- **Little Cassiopeia** is the W-shaped naked-eye zigzag in the constellation’s northern/main outline.
- NGC 7243 contains the multiple star Struve 2890 in projection or membership depending on the component considered.

### Open clusters, nebulae and galaxies

Lacerta contains **no Messier object**.

Principal catalogued objects include:

- **NGC 7243 = Caldwell 16 = Collinder 448** — open cluster, V about 6.4; discovered by William Herschel on 26 September 1788.
- **NGC 7209 = Collinder 444** — open cluster, V about 7.7; discovered by William Herschel on 19 October 1787.
- **NGC 7245** — compact open cluster.
- **NGC 7296** — small open cluster.
- **IC 1434** and **IC 1442** — open clusters.
- **IC 5217** — compact planetary nebula.
- **NGC 7248, NGC 7250, NGC 7223, NGC 7265, NGC 7426, NGC 7330, NGC 7231, NGC 7273, NGC 7274, NGC 7379 and NGC 7197** — galaxies of varying faintness.

The statement sometimes encountered online that “IC 5217 is the BL Lacertae blazar” is **false**. IC 5217 is a planetary nebula; BL Lacertae is an unrelated active galaxy. The error appears to result from careless list formatting. [Catalogue-style object list](https://theskylive.com/sky/constellations/lacerta-deepsky-objects), [observing list distinguishing the objects](https://www.weasner.com/etx/ref_guides/lacerta.html)

NGC 7209’s line of sight may contain overlapping stellar populations. An older proposal that two discrete clusters are superposed was not supported by all later photometric work, so this remains a historical interpretation rather than settled fact. [NGC 7209 summary and references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_7209)

### Objects carrying the Lacerta name

- **BL Lacertae** and the class **BL Lac objects**.
- **Lacerta OB1**, a loose young stellar association.
- **Lacerta I**, also **Andromeda XXXI**, a dwarf spheroidal satellite of M31 discovered in Pan-STARRS1 data. Its name is positional: it was found toward Lacerta, not because it belongs to the Milky Way constellation physically. [Discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.5301)
- Numerous variable stars, catalogued as “X Lacertae,” use the constellation’s Latin genitive under the standard variable-star system.

### Meteor showers

The claim that Lacerta has “no associated meteor showers” is obsolete if interpreted as a catalogue statement.

The IAU Meteor Data Center includes or has included several weak candidate streams with Lacertan radiants, notably:

- **328 ALA, alpha-Lacertids**, radiant roughly RA 343°, Dec +50°; initially reported from radar work by Brown and collaborators;
- **350 MAL, May Lacertids**, on the working list;
- **863 TLR, 12-Lacertids**, on the working list.

These are minor, often radar-derived streams, not prominent annual visual showers. The IAU MDC explicitly warns that database inclusion does not itself certify a shower’s physical reality, uniqueness, or correct orbit. They should therefore be labeled **reported/candidate meteor showers**, not equivalents of the Perseids or Leonids. [IAU Meteor Data Center](https://iaumeteordatacenter.org/), [MDC working list](https://iaumdc.astro.sk/working), [NASA-hosted MDC table](https://fireballs.ndc.nasa.gov/cmor-radiants/iau-mdc/)

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity to the sixteenth century: no Western Lacerta

#### Mesopotamia and Egypt

**Finding: absence of evidence.** No Babylonian or Egyptian constellation securely identified with the complete modern Lacerta figure was found in the standard transmission into Greek astronomy. The region is far north of the ecliptic and outside the zodiacal and decanal systems that dominate surviving Mesopotamian and Egyptian astral records.

It would be unsafe to treat generic serpent or lizard symbols in ancient religion as Lacerta without a coordinate-bearing astronomical source.

#### Greek astronomy, second century CE

Ptolemy’s *Almagest*, Books VII–VIII, lists 48 constellations. Lacerta is absent. Its present territory lay among the unformed spaces adjoining Andromeda, Cepheus, Cygnus and Pegasus.

There is consequently:

- no Greek constellation name;
- no Ptolemaic star description for a lizard figure;
- no Greek catasterism of this constellation;
- no fixed-star astrological nature assigned to α or β Lacertae in Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos*.

#### Farnese Atlas, probably second century CE Roman copy of a Hellenistic model

The Farnese globe preserves the inherited classical constellation figures but no Lacerta. Its surviving northern figures include Cepheus, Cygnus, Cassiopeia, Pegasus and Andromeda around the empty region. [Detailed inventory of the Farnese figures](https://www.phys.lsu.edu/farnese/JHAFarneseProofs.pdf)

#### al-Ṣūfī, 964

Al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib al-Thābita*, “Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars,” revises Ptolemy’s 48 constellations and records indigenous Arabic star names and asterisms. It has no *Ṣūrat al-Lacerta*. Any individual stars from the modern polygon would have been treated as faint background or as unformed stars near the adjoining Ptolemaic constellations.

No distinct Bedouin “lizard” using Hevelius’s stars was recovered. This is an important negative finding: later Arabic names for the Western constellation are translations, not al-Ṣūfī’s designation. [Al-Ṣūfī manuscript, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_18412/?sp=3&st=gallery), [constellation table](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_constellations.htm)

#### Dürer, 1515

Albrecht Dürer’s printed celestial maps show the classical northern and southern constellation systems. No Lacerta appears because the constellation had not yet been invented.

#### Bayer, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* likewise lacks Lacerta. Claims that it has “Bayer stars” should be understood as later Greek-letter nomenclature, not evidence that Bayer drew the animal.

#### Julius Schiller, 1627

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* reworked Bayer’s existing constellation system into Christian images. Because Lacerta had not yet been introduced, there is no exact “saint replacing Lacerta.”

Its future stars lay across the fringes of Schiller’s replacements for neighboring figures:

- Cygnus became the Cross of Christ with St Helena;
- Andromeda and Cepheus were transformed within Schiller’s New Testament/Christian historical scheme.

A claim that one named saint specifically replaced Lacerta is therefore anachronistic. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/item/65059218/), [ETH facsimile and study](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-8720)

### 1679: Royer’s royal Sceptre

**Documented fact:** Augustin Royer’s celestial charts introduced **Sceptrum et Manus Iustitiae** for Louis XIV. The phrase means “Sceptre and Hand of Justice,” the hand-shaped French coronation emblem and royal sceptre.

The figure occupied stars now in Lacerta and western Andromeda. Its cumbersome title was shortened or altered by later cartographers to such forms as:

- *Sceptrum*;
- *Sceptrum Imperiale*;
- Italian *Scettro*;
- occasionally labels confused with *Stellio* in later summaries.

It did not achieve durable adoption. [Historical summary and source trail](https://www.ianridpath.com/startales/honoresfriderici.html#sceptrum)

**Priority dispute:** Some modern summaries date Royer’s constellation to 1670, but Royer’s atlas is dated 1679. “1670” appears to be a propagated error. No adequate 1670 primary edition was located.

### 1687–1690: Hevelius’s invention

Johannes Hevelius died in January 1687. His completed astronomical work was issued posthumously in Gdańsk/Danzig in 1690 through the efforts of his widow and collaborator Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius.

The publication comprises:

- *Prodromus Astronomiae*;
- *Catalogus Stellarum Fixarum*;
- *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia*.

Lacerta appears as one of nine new constellations in the catalogue:

> **Lacerta sive Stellio**

The original catalogue contains **ten** stars under this heading. [Machine-readable historical analysis](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/pdf/2010/08/aa14003-10.pdf)

Hevelius’s plate M shows a long-tailed, four-legged lizard pressed between Cygnus, Andromeda and Cepheus. The creature’s posture visually exploits the elongated chain of faint stars. [Hevelius plate M](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AJohannes_Hevelius_-_Prodromus_Astronomia_-_Volume_III_%22Firmamentum_Sobiescianum%2C_sive_uranographia%22_-_Tavola_M_-_Lacerta_sive_stellio.jpg)

The often quoted date **1687** is defensible as the year of Hevelius’s completed work and death, and some impressions or title material bear it. **1690**, however, is the secure publication year of the posthumous atlas and catalogue. Statements that Lacerta “first appeared publicly in 1687” should therefore be qualified.

#### Was Lacerta copied from Royer?

**Disputed/inconclusive.** Royer’s royal emblem preceded Hevelius’s publication and occupied overlapping space. But Hevelius did not merely redraw a sceptre as a lizard, and no explicit statement by him acknowledging or rejecting Royer was recovered. Independent reuse of an attractive blank field is possible; direct rivalry is also plausible. The evidence supports precedence and overlap, not a demonstrated act of copying.

### Early eighteenth century: acceptance

Flamsteed incorporated Lacerta among the newer northern constellations. His treatment helped normalize it for practical European astronomy. Lalande’s numbered presentation produced the familiar sequence of 1–16 Lacertae.

Johann Gabriel Doppelmayr’s *Atlas Coelestis* of 1742 labels it **Lacerta Stellio** and shows a running lizard, evidence that Hevelius’s alternative name still circulated. [Historical atlas survey](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/), [World Asterisms historical handbook](https://rasc.rocks/sites/default/files/World%20Asterisms%20Project%20Handbook%20V%202025.6.pdf)

### 1782–1787: Bode before the great *Uranographia*

Bode reproduced Hevelius’s constellation in his popular charts and atlases. In 1787 he also devised **Honores Friderici**, “Frederick’s Honors” or “The Honors of Frederick,” to commemorate Frederick II of Prussia after the king’s death in 1786.

The figure comprised the crown, sword, pen or sceptre-like insignia of Frederick, drawn mainly from stars belonging historically to Andromeda’s chain and arm. It was not a complete replacement for Lacerta. The two occupied adjacent, partly competitive cartographic territory.

### 1801: Lacerta in Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode’s *Uranographia sive Astrorum Descriptio* was published in Berlin in 1801. It consists of large copper-engraved charts and was accompanied by the bilingual German-French *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne / Description et connoissance générale des constellations*.

The atlas depicts Lacerta on **plate IV**, with Perseus, Cassiopeia, Triangulum, Andromeda and Honores Friderici. [Plate index](https://www.sunflower-astronomy.com/Books/Bode_Uranographia/Bode_Uranographia.html), [institutional digital record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035)

Bode’s labels are:

- Latin: **Lacerta**
- German descriptive name: **die Eidechse**
- French parallel text: **le Lézard**

Bode did not use *Stellio* as the principal label on the plate. He retained the lizard as a distinct Hevelian constellation while giving Frederick’s memorial a separate figure nearby.

The accompanying catalogue advertised **17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae and clusters**, not 830. The user-supplied description “830 stars in 109 groups” appears to describe a modern collection, database chain, or subset—not Bode’s own complete astronomical tally. I found no primary Bode statement that Lacerta contained 830 stars or that the *Uranographia* contained exactly 109 constellations. The Marseille copy describes 102 constellation figures across the atlas; other counts differ depending on whether paired or subordinate figures are counted separately. [Bode text-volume record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1036)

### Nineteenth-century reception

#### Alexander Jamieson, 1822

Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* included Lacerta as an established northern constellation. His reptile is more naturalistic than Flamsteed’s sometimes dog-like rendering.

#### *Urania’s Mirror*, 1824/1825

Card 14 depicts **Lacerta, Cygnus, Lyra, Vulpecula and Anser**. The cards use pinholes so stars appear illuminated when held before a light.

The plates were closely copied from Jamieson. The author, long anonymous, was identified in 1994 by Peter Hingley as the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam. The old marketing attribution to “a lady” was false. [Royal Astronomical Society](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror), [card list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urania%27s_Mirror)

#### Elijah H. Burritt, 1833–1835 onward

Burritt’s *Geography of the Heavens* and accompanying atlas brought Lacerta into American school astronomy. The first atlas appeared in 1833; the widely digitized “new edition” is dated 1835. Its popular success helped carry the traditional pictorial constellations deep into the nineteenth century. [American Museum of Natural History copy](https://digitalcollections.amnh.org/archive/Celestial-map-from-Burritt-s-Atlas-designed-to-illustrate-the-geography-of-the-heavens-2URM1T14S658.html)

#### Argelander, 1843

Friedrich Wilhelm August Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* retained Lacerta but emphasized accurate naked-eye stellar mapping over elaborate allegorical art.

#### Heis, 1872

Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus*, based substantially on Argelander, retained the constellation. Its practical northern constellation system helped prepare the cartographic consensus from which the IAU later worked. Heis’s reptile has been praised in later descriptive literature as anatomically more convincing, but this is an aesthetic judgment, not an astronomical fact.

### Twentieth century to the present

- **1922:** IAU adopts the conventional 88-constellation list and three-letter abbreviations.
- **1925:** Delporte presents his boundary proposal.
- **1928:** boundaries approved at Leiden.
- **1930:** Delporte publishes the definitive lines.
- **1929:** Hoffmeister announces BL Lac as a variable.
- **1936:** CP Lac becomes a conspicuous naked-eye nova.
- **1968:** Schmitt identifies BL Lac with a variable radio source.
- **1970s:** BL Lacertae becomes the prototype of a recognized AGN class.
- **2013:** Lacerta I/Andromeda XXXI announced as an M31 satellite.
- **2024:** IAU WGSN approves **Stellio** for α Lacertae.

## Invention and reception

### Inventor

**Documented attribution:** Johannes Hevelius.

### Date

- Design or completed work: by 1687.
- Secure posthumous publication: 1690.

### Medium

- Star catalogue: *Catalogus Stellarum Fixarum*.
- Atlas: *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia*.
- Plate: northern chart traditionally identified as plate M.

### Purpose

Hevelius did not state that the figure honored a king, patron, voyage, machine or instrument. Unlike Scutum Sobiescianum, it was apparently a morphological and mnemonic invention: a small spotted animal fitted to a thin chain of faint stars.

The constellation belonged to Hevelius’s broader program of filling poorly occupied northern spaces with figures that observers could use for positional reference.

### Rival figures

#### Sceptrum et Manus Iustitiae

- Inventor: Augustin Royer.
- Date: 1679.
- Honoree: Louis XIV.
- Fate: obsolete.
- Present location of stars: Lacerta and western Andromeda.

#### Honores Friderici

- Inventor: Johann Elert Bode.
- Date: 1787.
- Honoree: Frederick II “the Great” of Prussia.
- Fate: obsolete; excluded from the IAU 88.
- Present location: overwhelmingly Andromeda, close to Lacerta.
- Important qualification: it was a separate figure, not merely Bode’s new name for Lacerta.

Lacerta survived because it was compact, nonpolitical, memorable, and adopted by Flamsteed and major eighteenth- and nineteenth-century practical atlases. The French and Prussian royal memorials were tied to particular courts and never achieved comparable international durability.

## Myths and legends

### Classical Greek and Roman mythology

**Documented absence:** Lacerta is a modern constellation and has no ancient Greek or Roman catasterism.

It does not occur as a constellation in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica*;
- Hyginus’ *Fabulae*;
- Aratus’ *Phaenomena*;
- the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus’ Aratean poem and scholia;
- Manilius’ *Astronomica*;
- Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* or *Fasti*;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus’ *Bibliotheca*;
- Pausanias’ *Description of Greece*;
- Nonnus’ *Dionysiaca*.

There is therefore no ancient answer to “who placed Lacerta in the sky and why.”

### Ancient lizard stories improperly attached to Lacerta

Classical literature contains lizards and transformations involving small reptiles, but linking these to Hevelius’s constellation is a **modern invention** unless a source explicitly makes the identification.

One especially relevant lexical tradition concerns the *stellio*. Ancient natural history and later bestiaries described it as spotted, poisonous, deceitful, or capable of shedding its skin. Medieval moralizers consequently used it as an emblem of envy or fraud. These traditions help explain the word and possible visual character of Hevelius’s animal; they are not catasterisms.

Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 5.446–461 tells how a rude boy mocked the goddess Ceres/Demeter while she drank and was transformed into a spotted lizard, traditionally called *stellio*. This is the most obvious classical lizard-transformation available to later readers. However:

- Ovid does not place the boy in the sky;
- Hevelius does not cite Ovid as the reason for Lacerta;
- no early atlas commentary discovered here joins the two.

Calling Lacerta “the boy transformed by Ceres” is therefore a **modern mythographic graft**, not an ancient constellation myth.

### Modern invented stories

Astronomy clubs and educational writers sometimes invite readers to invent heroic stories for the Lizard precisely because it lacks one. Such tales are legitimate modern folklore but must not be presented as Greek mythology.

### “Lacerta Files”

The “Lacerta Files” or “Lacerta Interview” is an internet-era reptilian-alien narrative circulated from about 1999–2000. Its supposed reptilian witness is named Lacerta. The original translator himself described it as a fictional or hoax text. There is no evidentiary connection to the constellation beyond the Latin reptile-name. It belongs to modern UFO folklore, not astronomical tradition. [Historical skeptical analysis](https://www.skeptic.org.uk/2021/11/the-lacerta-files-and-its-reptilian-hybrids-show-the-persistence-of-appealing-myths/)

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Chinese

The strongest demonstrable pre-Hevelian figure in the same sky is **Téngshé**, the Flying Serpent.

The *Jin Shu* astronomical treatise describes a 22-star Téngshé north of the lunar mansion Shì, “Encampment” or “House,” representing a celestial snake and chief of aquatic reptiles. Its stars stretch through modern Lacerta, Cygnus, Cepheus, Cassiopeia and Andromeda.

A Téngshé figure appears on Chinese star maps including the Suzhou planisphere, carved in 1247 from an earlier design. Northern Lacerta lies near its center. [Suzhou-map discussion](https://astrohum.org/newsletters/newsletter_2023_d.pdf)

This is a real cultural antecedent in the **same stars**, but not a historical ancestor of Hevelius’s constellation: no evidence shows that Hevelius knew the Chinese figure. The resemblance between a European lizard and a Chinese flying reptile is best classified as **independent convergence**.

Other mapped Chinese asterisms crossing the modern polygon include:

- Chǔ, the Pestle;
- Chēfǔ, the Chariot Yard;
- marginal stars of neighboring establishments within the Black Tortoise of the North.

### Arabic and Persian

Al-Ṣūfī retained Ptolemy’s constellation framework and supplemented it with indigenous Arabic asterisms. No separate Arabic Lacerta or *Stellio* was found.

Stars of the present area lay among:

- the unformed stars of Cepheus;
- the outer chain/limbs of Andromeda;
- the margins of Cygnus and Pegasus;
- Bedouin star groups extending across those figures.

Because most Lacertan stars are faint, they did not generate major individual Arabic proper names comparable with Deneb, Alderamin or Markab. Modern Arabic forms such as **السحلية**, *al-suḥliyya*, “the lizard,” are translations of the IAU constellation.

Persian astronomical books drawing on Arabic-Ptolemaic astronomy likewise did not preserve a pre-Hevelian Persian lizard here. Modern Persian names are translated international nomenclature.

### Hebrew and Jewish traditions

No securely attested ancient Hebrew constellation corresponding to Lacerta was found.

Biblical **leta’ah** and related reptile terms name terrestrial animals, not this star group. Medieval Jewish astronomical texts generally transmitted Arabic-Ptolemaic constellations; because Lacerta did not exist in that system, it has no established medieval Hebrew equivalent.

Claims assigning Lacerta to:

- a tribe of Israel;
- a Hebrew letter;
- a sefirah;
- a creature in Ezekiel’s vision;

could not be traced to a premodern coordinate-bearing source. They should be classified as **modern esoteric correspondences** unless a specific manuscript is produced.

### Indian astronomy

Lacerta lies far north of the ecliptic. The classical Indian nakṣatras are lunar stations arranged along the Moon’s path, so none properly “crosses Lacerta.”

No traditional Sanskrit nakṣatra or classical Indian constellation equivalent covering the Hevelian figure was identified. Modern Indian-language names for Lacerta are translations of the Western constellation.

### Christianized sky

Schiller’s 1627 atlas predates Lacerta. Therefore no saint or biblical object replaced it specifically.

It is misleading to search Schiller’s table for “the saint corresponding to Lacerta”: the stars were still distributed among Bayer’s neighboring constellations, and Schiller Christianized those older figures.

### Astrological, Hermetic and alchemical uses

#### Ptolemy

Ptolemy could not assign a planetary nature to Lacerta because it did not exist. None of its principal stars appears among the prominent fixed stars discussed under a Lacertan heading in *Tetrabiblos* I.9.

#### Behenian stars

None of the traditional fifteen Behenian fixed stars lies in Lacerta. Lists placing “a Behenian star of Lacerta” are erroneous or modern expansions.

#### Lunar mansions

Arabic, Indian and Western lunar mansions follow the ecliptic. Lacerta, at approximately +35° to +57° declination, is well outside them. No lunar mansion properly crosses the constellation.

#### Later astrology

Post-Hevelian astrologers sometimes assign Lacerta qualities derived from the terrestrial lizard—wariness, concealment, adaptability, regeneration or venom. These are **modern symbolic inventions**, not inherited Hellenistic doctrine.

A widely quoted astrological claim that Lacerta gives “a sharp intellect and a retiring nature” appears in modern compilations descended from nineteenth-century constellation lore, but no ancient textual basis was located.

#### Hermetic and alchemical literature

No canonical Hermetic or early alchemical text refers to Lacerta as a constellation. Symbolic lizards and salamanders occur in emblematic and alchemical art, especially in relation to fire, transformation and regeneration, but identifying one with Hevelius’s northern constellation without an explicit caption is unjustified.

## Iconography

### Ancient monuments and manuscripts

#### Farnese Atlas

No Lacerta. The relevant visual evidence is the **blank interval** among surrounding classical figures.

#### al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

No Lacerta figure. Manuscripts contain only the 48 Ptolemaic constellations, drawn twice—globe-view and sky-view. Faint stars later enclosed by Lacerta appear, if at all, with adjacent constellations.

#### Dürer, 1515

No Lacerta. The map documents the pre-Hevelian northern sky immediately before the great early-modern expansion.

#### Schiller, 1627

No separate Lacerta. His Christian imagery covers its future space only incidentally through neighboring plates.

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

The foundational image is plate M of the *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*. It depicts an elongated spotted reptile with:

- head toward the Cepheus/Andromeda side;
- four splayed limbs;
- long tapering tail toward Cygnus;
- stars set along or upon the body.

Hevelius printed the celestial figures in globe-view orientation, as though seen from outside the celestial sphere. This reverses east and west relative to ordinary naked-eye sky maps.

### Doppelmayr, Nuremberg, 1742

Doppelmayr shows the figure as **Lacerta Stellio**, demonstrating continued reception of both Hevelian names.

### Flamsteed atlas

Flamsteed accepted Lacerta, but later critics remarked that his animal could look more like a greyhound than a lizard. This is an iconographic judgment rather than a naming dispute.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s plate IV presents an anatomically decorative lizard among:

- Cygnus;
- Cepheus;
- Cassiopeia;
- Andromeda;
- Pegasus;
- Honores Friderici.

Its proximity to Frederick’s insignia makes the plate especially valuable for understanding how Bode simultaneously preserved Hevelius and promoted his own royal constellation.

The Marseille institutional copy consists of large double-page copper engravings, approximately 54 × 66 cm, with the explanatory volume printed in parallel German and French. [Bode atlas record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035)

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson naturalized the animal into a more recognizably reptilian form. His plates directly influenced *Urania’s Mirror*.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/1825

Card 14 combines Lacerta with Cygnus, Lyra, Vulpecula and Anser. Pinholes mark the stars. The cards are culturally important as domestic and educational objects rather than precision catalogues.

A practical hazard accompanied the intended candle-lit display: surviving accounts note the danger of scorching or burning the cards.

### Burritt, United States, 1833/1835

Burritt’s school atlas used hand-colored mythological and animal figures. Lacerta’s inclusion shows that by the 1830s it was thoroughly established in transatlantic teaching.

### Argelander and Heis

Their charts mark the nineteenth-century transition from sumptuous pictorial uranography to measured stellar atlases. Lacerta remains, but the illustrative animal becomes secondary to positions, magnitudes and constellation lines.

### Coins, cathedral portals, calendars and ceilings

No ancient coin, classical temple, medieval cathedral portal, astrological calendar or Renaissance ceiling can securely depict **Lacerta as this constellation**, because the constellation did not yet exist.

Spotted lizards on such objects should not be identified as Lacerta without an accompanying star pattern or label. No well-documented monumental ceiling program specifically featuring the Hevelian Lacerta was located.

## People, events and works

### Johannes and Elisabeth Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius designed the constellation and catalogued its stars. Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius completed and published his posthumous astronomical works in 1690. Attribution solely to Johannes is conventional but should not erase Elisabeth’s editorial and observational role.

### Robert Hooke and Edmond Halley

Their famous dispute with Hevelius concerned naked-eye versus telescopic sighting instruments, not Lacerta itself. Halley visited Gdańsk in 1679 and confirmed the quality of Hevelius’s observations. The episode is relevant to the reliability and production context of the catalogue in which Lacerta appeared. [Hevelius catalogue history](https://pbarbier.com/hevelius/hevelius.html)

### William Herschel

Herschel discovered the constellation’s two best-known open clusters:

- NGC 7209 in 1787;
- NGC 7243 in 1788.

### Cuno Hoffmeister

Hoffmeister’s 1929 classification of BL Lac as a variable star unintentionally supplied the designation for an entire extragalactic class.

### John L. Schmitt

Schmitt’s 1968 radio identification transformed BL Lac from an obscure variable into an astrophysical puzzle.

### J. B. Oke and James Gunn

Their spectroscopy of the surrounding host galaxy supplied the redshift that securely established BL Lac’s extragalactic nature.

### Nova observers of 1936

The nearly simultaneous discoveries of CP Lac by observers in several countries illustrate the difficulty of assigning a single discoverer to sudden naked-eye phenomena. The solar-eclipse expedition aboard *Strathaird* became part of the event’s contemporary narrative.

### Explorers and navigation

No major documented exploration voyage is known to have depended specifically on Lacerta. Its stars are too faint for it to rank with Polaris, Ursa Major, Crux, Canopus or the bright stars of Orion in historical navigation.

Claims that “ancient sailors steered by Lacerta” are chronologically impossible under that name and observationally implausible. Chinese or other mariners may have used broader northern star fields containing its stars, but no specific Lacertan navigation tradition was recovered.

### USS *Lacerta* (AKA-29)

The United States Navy attack cargo ship **USS Lacerta** was explicitly named for the constellation.

- laid down: 5 July 1944;
- launched: 10 November 1944;
- commissioned: 19 December 1944;
- served at Okinawa and in the movement of occupation forces;
- decommissioned: 25 March 1946;
- received one battle star;
- sold for scrapping in 1966.

It should not be confused with USS *Lagarto*, a submarine whose Spanish name also means a kind of lizard. [USS *Lacerta* history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Lacerta)

### Literature, visual art, music and buildings

No major canonical ancient poem or pre-Hevelian painting names the constellation.

Modern uses occur primarily in:

- astronomical poetry and star-themed art;
- science fiction;
- music projects using constellation names;
- commercial observatory and telescope-product branding;
- the online “Lacerta Files” fiction.

No historically important building securely named for the constellation, as opposed to the animal or zoological genus, was verified.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. 1687 or 1690?

- **1687:** year of Hevelius’s death and of completed or dated material.
- **1690:** secure posthumous publication of the *Prodromus*, catalogue and *Firmamentum*.

Best practice: “devised by Hevelius by 1687; published posthumously in 1690.”

### 2. Was it first called Stellio?

Many summaries state that Hevelius “initially named it Stellio and later changed it to Lacerta.” The primary heading **Lacerta sive Stellio** does not establish a chronological sequence; it presents alternatives simultaneously. “Initially named Stellio” is therefore **unsupported simplification**.

### 3. What animal is *stellio*?

- spotted lizard or stellion: strongly supported;
- starred agama, *Stellagama stellio*: plausible modern zoological identification;
- gecko: used in some historical dictionaries;
- newt: reflects unstable old terminology but is less secure in Hevelius’s context.

No exact species can be proved from the atlas image alone.

### 4. Royer’s date

Royer’s constellation is properly associated with his **1679** atlas. The date 1670, widely copied online, was not substantiated by a corresponding primary edition.

### 5. Royer versus Hevelius

Royer had chronological precedence in occupying part of the field, but the degree of direct influence is unknown. The two figures overlap without being identical.

### 6. Did Bode rename Lacerta?

No. Bode preserved Lacerta and placed **Honores Friderici** beside it. Statements that Bode “renamed Lacerta Frederick’s Glory” conflate adjacent constellations.

### 7. Was Honores Friderici created in 1787 or 1801?

Bode proposed it in 1787; the grand *Uranographia* depiction followed in 1801.

### 8. How many stars did Hevelius give Lacerta?

The critical catalogue analysis gives **10**. The familiar **16** are the later Flamsteed-numbered sequence. Counts such as “23 stars” in nineteenth-century literature usually refer to later atlas inventories or fainter plotted points, not the original Hevelian catalogue heading.

### 9. “830 stars in 109 groups”

No primary evidence was found that Bode described Lacerta this way. Bode’s companion catalogue advertises 17,240 entries, while modern institutional descriptions count roughly 102 constellation figures in the atlas. “830 stars in 109 groups” most likely belongs to a modern curated Bode-image collection or metadata chain. Without the collection record, it cannot be treated as a property of the constellation or the 1801 catalogue.

### 10. Little Cassiopeia

The shape is real and the nickname widespread, but its inventor and first printing remain unidentified. It is not a historical alternative official name.

### 11. Does Lacerta have a named star?

Old sources: no.  
Current answer: yes—α Lacertae was formally named **Stellio** in 2024, and V424 Lacertae bears **Tengshe**.

### 12. Chinese Flying Serpent equals Lacerta

Only partly. Téngshé is much larger and crosses several Western constellations. Northern Lacerta lies centrally within it, but the two are not coextensive.

### 13. Téngshé’s principal star

Different historical reconstructions identify different leading stars. Later mappings use α Lacertae as Téngshé One, while the IAU name Tengshe was assigned to V424 Lacertae based on an earlier reconstructed configuration. This is a genuine identification problem, not necessarily an error.

### 14. “Lacerta is an ancient Chinese lizard”

False formulation. The Chinese figure is a flying serpent, documented independently. Hevelius’s lizard is a seventeenth-century European invention.

### 15. CP Lacertae discovery priority

Multiple independent observers saw or reported the nova within a short interval. Sources differ because they privilege first sighting, first report, local publication or national observing records. A collective discovery formulation is most defensible.

### 16. CP Lac’s alleged pre-eruption brightening

Published analyses have questioned whether some early photographic measurements actually recorded the nova rather than a neighboring star. The proposed precursor brightening is therefore weak evidence, not established behavior.

### 17. IC 5217 and BL Lac

They are different objects. Calling IC 5217 “the prototypical blazar” is a catalogue-copying error.

### 18. Are there Lacertid meteor showers?

Yes, in the IAU MDC working/database sense. No, if “associated shower” is meant to signify a strong, securely established annual visual display. The apparent contradiction comes from different thresholds of recognition.

### 19. Lacerta Files

A modern internet hoax/fiction using the reptile name. It provides no historical information about the constellation.

### 20. Astrological antiquity

Any “ancient astrological meaning of Lacerta” is false as stated: the constellation did not exist in antiquity. Later symbolic interpretations are modern.

## Open questions

1. **Exact first use of “Little Cassiopeia.”** The nickname is common, but a securely dated first printed occurrence remains to be found.

2. **Hevelius’s precise zoological model.** His wording supports a star-spotted lizard, but whether he had a particular Mediterranean species, bestiary image or emblem in mind is not demonstrable.

3. **Royer’s influence on Hevelius.** Comparative chronology proves overlap and priority, not direct borrowing.

4. **Original membership of the Lizard’s head.** Star-by-star comparison among Hevelius’s plate, catalogue coordinates, Flamsteed’s revisions and later Bayer-style letters would sharpen which points were original and which were later transferred from the surroundings of Cepheus.

5. **Bode’s exact German-French Lacerta paragraph.** The digitized companion volume is available, but its page-level OCR was not exposed through the consulted catalogue interface. A diplomatic transcription from the scanned page would be desirable.

6. **“830 stars in 109 groups.”** This could not be traced to Bode’s original text. The modern Bode collection or chain record from which it comes needs to be identified before the statement can be interpreted.

7. **Téngshé reconstruction.** Chinese charts and texts vary, and modern identifications of all 22 stars are not unanimous. A full solution requires comparison of the *Jin Shu*, Tang catalogues, the Suzhou planisphere and positional reconstructions.

8. **Arabic unformed stars.** A complete manuscript-level collation of al-Ṣūfī’s unformed stars around Cepheus, Cygnus, Pegasus and Andromeda may reveal individual modern Lacertan identifications, but it would still not produce an Arabic Lacerta constellation.

9. **Early vernacular translations.** Exact first printed dates for German *Eidechse*, French *Lézard*, Italian *Lucertola* and Spanish *Lagarto* remain to be established from national astronomical manuals.
