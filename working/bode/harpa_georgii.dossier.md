# Harpa Georgii: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

> **Status: abolished / not adopted by the IAU.** Harpa Georgii is not one of the 88 constellations standardized at the International Astronomical Union’s Rome General Assembly of 1922. It consequently has no official IAU abbreviation, genitive, boundary, or assigned astronomical objects. When Eugène Delporte’s boundaries were approved in 1928 and published in 1930, the former harp’s principal stars fell in **Taurus** and **Eridanus**. Some broad nineteenth-century drawings approached the modern boundary of **Cetus**, but no securely identified principal star of Hell’s original figure is now in Cetus.

**Classification:** documented modern invention.

**Inventor:** Maximilian Hell, S.J. (1720–1792), Hungarian-born Jesuit astronomer and director of the Imperial and Royal Observatory at Vienna.

**First securely documented publication:** Vienna, 1789, in Hell’s *Monumenta, aere perenniora, inter astra ponenda*, issued with the *Ephemerides Astronomicae* for 1790.

**Original name:** *Psalterium Georgianum*—“the Georgian/Georgean Psaltery,” meaning George III’s psaltery.

**Bode’s name:** *Harpa Georgii*—“George’s Harp,” in Johann Elert Bode’s *Uranographia* and its accompanying *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, Berlin, 1801.

**Location:** immediately south of Taurus, north of the upper reaches of Eridanus, east of the head or mouth of Cetus and west of Orion. Hell described it as *infra Taurum Zodiacalem, et inter Orionem, atque Cetum, supra Eridanum*.

**Brightest principal star:** 10 Tauri, visual magnitude about 4.29. Hell labeled it γ (*Gamma Psalterii Georgiani*); Bode labeled it E in his own non-Bayer sequence.

**Number of stars:** Hell expressly said that he formed the figure from **twenty-one fixed stars** previously “not yet arranged into any constellation.” Later cartographers redrew it and disagreed over which stars constituted the instrument.

**Nature of the figure:** not simply a generic harp. Hell specified a **ten-stringed Davidic psaltery**, whose strings signified the Ten Commandments and whose sacred use signified George III’s Christian piety. The common statement that Hell chose a harp because William Herschel was a musician is a plausible modern association, but it is not the reason Hell himself gives.

The Latin *psalterium* derives from Greek ψαλτήριον, *psaltērion*, a plucked string instrument, from ψάλλειν, “to pluck.” Hell also supplied the Hebrew identification *Nablon*, representing נֵבֶל, *nēḇel*, a biblical stringed instrument. The exact construction of the ancient *nevel* remains debated; “harp,” “lyre,” and “psaltery” are conventional approximations rather than secure organological identifications.

Latin *harpa* is medieval rather than classical Latin and is related to Germanic words represented by Old High German *harpha* and Old English *hearpe*. Bode therefore changed both the grammatical construction and the kind of instrument: Hell’s adjectival *Psalterium Georgianum* became the possessive *Harpa Georgii*.

### Abbreviation and genitive

**Documented fact:** Harpa Georgii has no IAU abbreviation or official IAU genitive.

- *Harpae Georgii* would be the ordinary Latin genitive of *Harpa Georgii*, but it was never standardized by the IAU.
- *Psalterii Georgii* is the grammatically regular genitive of *Psalterium Georgii* and appears in retrospective databases.
- **PsG**, sometimes printed online, is a modern unofficial abbreviation. It must not be presented as an IAU designation.

### Why “abolished” requires qualification

The IAU did not pass a separate resolution reading “Harpa Georgii is abolished.” At Rome in 1922 it selected the canonical list and abbreviations from which the harp was absent. Delporte subsequently partitioned the entire celestial sphere among those accepted constellations. “Abolished” is therefore accurate shorthand for **excluded from the standardized list and deprived of any remaining territory**, not for a targeted condemnation of Hell’s figure.

The IAU’s own historical account states that its Commission on Notations and Units agreed upon the standard list at Rome in 1922; Delporte proposed boundaries in 1925, the IAU approved them at Leiden in 1928, and the results appeared in 1930. The frequent statement that the boundaries were fixed “in 1922” conflates the several stages.

### The brightest stars

#### 10 Tauri

**Documented identification:** Hell’s γ; Bode’s E; modern 10 Tau.

- Flamsteed designation: 10 Tauri.
- Bayer designation: none.
- Magnitude: approximately 4.29.
- Spectral type: usually catalogued near F9 IV–V.
- Distance: about 45 light-years from Gaia/Hipparcos-scale parallaxes.
- Proper name: none approved by the IAU.
- Traditional Arabic, Greek, or Latin name: none securely attested.
- Historical importance: generally the brightest star in Hell’s figure.

It is often claimed that ο² Eridani—40 Eridani—was the harp’s brightest star. That is an error discussed below: surviving charts do not extend the instrument sufficiently far east to contain ο¹ or ο² Eridani.

#### 32 Eridani

- Flamsteed designation: 32 Eridani.
- Bayer designation: none.
- Magnitude: approximately 4.45 combined.
- Nature: visual binary; the principal component is an evolved G-type giant and the companion an A-type star.
- Proper name: none approved by the IAU.

#### 17 Eridani

- Flamsteed designation: 17 Eridani.
- Magnitude: approximately 4.74.
- Spectral class: late B-type in modern catalogues.
- Proper name: none.

#### 24 Eridani

- Flamsteed designation: 24 Eridani.
- Magnitude: approximately 5.24.
- Proper name: none.

#### 35 Eridani

- Flamsteed designation: 35 Eridani.
- Magnitude: approximately 5.28.
- Proper name: none.

### A modern retrospective ten-star list

One widely copied astrological compilation gives ten stars under the heading *Psalterium Georgii*:

- 93 G. Eridani
- 17 Eridani
- 109 G. Eridani
- 22 Eridani
- 10 Tauri
- 24 Eridani
- 25 Eridani
- 32 Eridani
- 35 Eridani
- 164 G. Eridani

Here “G.” refers to Benjamin Gould’s catalogue numbering, not a Bayer letter. This list is useful as a reconstruction of a broad later footprint but is not identical to Hell’s twenty-one stars or Bode’s labeled set.

### Stars gained from or returned to neighboring figures

The history is not a simple transfer followed by restitution:

- **Ptolemaic tradition:** much of the space was populated by “unformed” stars outside the formal figures. Some could be catalogued with Eridanus or Cetus without being drawn as part of their bodies.
- **Bayer, 1603:** 10 Tauri and 32 Eridani appear among decorative reeds or flourishes along the bank of Eridanus.
- **Cellarius, 1661:** stars later used by Hell contribute visually to the lower edge of Cetus’s open mouth.
- **Hevelius, 1690, and Flamsteed, 1729:** the relevant stars are largely unformed or assigned to the surrounding catalogue regions; neither recognized a harp.
- **Hell, 1789:** deliberately gathered twenty-one stars between Taurus, Cetus, Orion, and Eridanus into a new figure.
- **Goldbach, 1799:** used ζ Eridani in the base of his upright harp.
- **Bode, 1801:** diverted the drawn course of Eridanus around the harp, apparently to avoid sharing δ or ζ Eridani.
- **Bode, 1805:** his revised *Vorstellung der Gestirne* allowed the harp’s base to overlap the river more ambiguously.
- **Jamieson, 1822, and *Urania’s Mirror*, 1824/25:** broadened the decorated base over δ and ε Eridani.
- **Delporte, 1930:** assigned the secure principal stars to Eridanus and Taurus. The ornamental historical footprint has no legal modern equivalent.

### Asterisms inside or overlapping the figure

No enduring Western naked-eye asterism is known to have developed within Harpa Georgii after the constellation disappeared.

The harp itself is now best treated as a **historical asterism**, not a constellation in the IAU territorial sense.

In Chinese uranography the same region crosses later “additional-star” extensions of two asterisms:

- **天苑, Tiānyuàn, “Celestial Meadows”**: associated with the lunar mansion 昴, *Mǎo*, the Hairy Head/Pleiades mansion. Later Chinese catalogues identify 17 and 22 Eridani as additional stars of Tiānyuàn.
- **天囷, Tiānjūn, “Circular Celestial Granary”**: in the 胃, *Wèi*, Stomach mansion. 10 Tauri and 24–25 Eridani appear as later additional stars of this system.

The word “additional” is important: these identifications do not establish that all of Hell’s faint stars belonged to the oldest Chinese core figures.

### Proper names and their absence

None of the five secure principal harp stars has a traditional proper name accepted by the IAU Working Group on Star Names. Consequently there is no defensible list of Arabic-derived names comparable to Aldebaran, Betelgeuse, or Rigel.

Claims that the constellation’s stars bore a collective Greek, Arabic, Persian, Babylonian, Egyptian, or Hebrew name have not been traced to primary catalogues. The surviving record points in the opposite direction: Hell chose the space precisely because it contained faint, weakly integrated or “unformed” stars.

### Deep-sky objects

**Finding:** no Messier, NGC, IC, nebula, cluster, or galaxy is known to bear the official or traditional name “Harpa Georgii,” “George’s Harp,” or “Psalterium Georgianum.”

Because the figure never possessed agreed geometric boundaries, it is methodologically unsafe to compile every modern deep-sky object lying inside one selected artist’s outline and call it an “object of Harpa Georgii.” Different versions disagree substantially about the base, ribbon, and eastward extent.

No Messier object is securely situated in the narrow core defined by 10 Tau, 17 Eri, 24 Eri, 32 Eri, and 35 Eri. Catalogued faint galaxies undoubtedly occur in the line of sight, but none has a historical connection to the figure.

### Variables, novae, and supernovae

No classical nova or historical supernova was recorded under Harpa Georgii’s name. No variable-star nomenclature was permanently based on it. By the time systematic variable-star designation became extensive, the figure was already falling out of scientific use.

Modern variable stars in the same sky are assigned Taurus or Eridanus designations. The IAU boundary project was explicitly instructed, where practicable, to preserve established variable-star constellation designations; it had no Harpa variables to preserve.

### Meteor showers

No established IAU meteor shower is named the Harpids, Harpa Georgiids, Psalteriids, or equivalent. Any radiant now falling in the historical drawing must be named through Taurus, Eridanus, Cetus, or another recognized neighboring constellation.

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity to the seventeenth century: no Harpa Georgii

**Documented absence:** there is no Harpa Georgii, George’s Harp, or Davidic Psaltery in Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Arabic, Persian, Jewish, Indian, or medieval Latin uranography.

This conclusion follows not merely from silence in modern lists but from the figure’s transparent dedication to an eighteenth-century British king and Hell’s declaration that he was forming it himself.

#### ca. second century CE — Ptolemy

Ptolemy’s *Almagest* knew Taurus, Cetus, Orion, and Eridanus, including “unformed” stars adjacent to them. It did not know a harp here. The relevant stars therefore had no shared classical figure and no catasterism.

#### tenth century — al-Ṣūfī

ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Images of the Fixed Stars* revised the Ptolemaic catalogue and pictured Taurus, Cetus, Orion, and Eridanus. It contains no George’s Harp and no Davidic psaltery in this location. Some of the faint stars were treated within the inherited Ptolemaic constellations or as stars outside their main figures.

#### 1603 — Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria*, plate Mm for Eridanus, placed stars later used by Hell within or near decorative riverbank vegetation. Barentine specifically identifies 10 Tauri and 32 Eridani among these flourishes. This is an artistic antecedent, not a harp.

#### 1661 — Cellarius

Andrea Cellarius’s *Harmonia Macrocosmica* used part of the future harp region in the vicinity of Cetus’s gaping mouth. Again, it did not depict a harp.

#### 1690 — Hevelius

Johannes Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum* did not introduce a figure here. Its absence matters because Hevelius did create several new constellations elsewhere and became a major source for later canonical additions.

#### 1729 — Flamsteed

Flamsteed’s *Atlas Coelestis* preceded Hell and therefore contains no Psalterium Georgianum. Hell’s references to Flamsteed’s plate numbers locate his new figure on the older charts; they do not claim Flamsteed as an inventor or adopter.

### 1781 — the date that caused later confusion

William Herschel discovered Uranus on 13 March 1781. He proposed *Georgium Sidus*, “George’s Star,” to honor George III, who became his patron.

**Disputed/error tradition:** Richard Hinckley Allen and numerous derivative websites dated Hell’s constellation to 1781. That date appears to have been inferred from Uranus’s discovery, not from a surviving 1781 chart by Hell. The securely documented constellation publication is 1789.

### 1789 — Hell’s invention at Vienna

Hell published *Monumenta, aere perenniora, inter astra ponenda*. Its Horatian title means approximately “monuments more lasting than bronze, to be placed among the stars.”

He introduced three memorial figures:

1. *Tubus Herschelii Major*, Herschel’s large telescope.
2. *Tubus Herschelii Minor*, Herschel’s smaller telescope.
3. *Psalterium Georgianum*, George III’s psaltery.

Two honored Herschel’s instruments; the third honored Herschel’s royal patron. A fourth overview chart related these figures to Uranus.

Hell’s account is explicit:

- the instrument was Davidic;
- it had ten strings;
- the strings signified the Mosaic Decalogue;
- Hebrew worshippers had used such an instrument in the Jerusalem Temple;
- George III’s “singular Christian piety” made it a fitting royal emblem;
- the constellation was meant to preserve that reputation for later astronomers;
- the psaltery was shown suspended from a band because Hell read Augustine as describing an instrument carried by the player.

Hell drew upon Jerome and Augustine for the distinction between psaltery and cithara. He wrote that the psaltery’s resonating cavity was above the strings, whereas the cithara’s was below.

**Historical context:** Hell was director of the Vienna Observatory, not George III’s court astronomer. Herschel, who benefited directly from George’s patronage, was the astronomical intermediary of the homage.

### 1790 — appearance in the astronomical ephemeris

The work circulated with the Vienna *Ephemerides Astronomicae* for 1790. Bibliographic descriptions consequently alternate between “1789” as the publication/composition year and “for 1790” as the ephemeris year. These are compatible, not rival invention dates.

### 1799 — Christian Goldbach

Christian Goldbach’s *Neuester Himmels-Atlas* abbreviated the label to *Harfe* and transformed Hell’s shallow, suspended psaltery into a more familiar upright medieval/modern harp. It included ζ Eridani at the base.

This demonstrates that the conversion from psaltery to upright harp preceded Bode’s *Uranographia*. Therefore “Bode invented the upright form” is too strong.

### 1801 — Bode’s *Uranographia*

Bode included it on the chart containing Taurus, Gemini, Orion, Canis Minor, and Harpa Georgii. The surviving institutional record describes the work as published at Berlin, *apud autorem*, in 1801.

Bode:

- changed the label to *Harpa Georgii*;
- drew an upright, triangular harp;
- omitted Hell’s suspension ribbon;
- angled the instrument to fill the available space;
- placed it below Taurus and above Eridanus;
- drew Eridanus around the harp’s base rather than clearly incorporating the river’s δ or ζ stars.

Bode’s accompanying German description reads, with modernization:

> *Diese Harfe hat der Abt Hell dem jetzigen König von Großbritannien Georg III. zu Ehren an den Himmel gebracht. Sie steht südlich unterhalb dem Stier, östlich vor dem Wallfisch, und ist aus Sternen vierter Größe zusammengesetzt, die sonst zum Eridanfluss gehörten.*

Translation:

> “Abbé Hell placed this harp in the sky in honor of the present King of Great Britain, George III. It stands south below the Bull, east in front of the Whale, and is composed of fourth-magnitude stars that otherwise belonged to the river Eridanus.”

Bode thus correctly credited Hell and George III. He did not claim to have invented the constellation.

### 1805 — Bode’s revised popular atlas

In the 1805 edition of Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne*, *Georgs-Harfe* appears where it was absent from the 1782 edition. Here the base overlaps Eridanus more freely than in the 1801 *Uranographia*, apparently sharing ε Eridani or adjacent stars depending on how the ornament is read.

### 1818–1820 — Bode’s later handbook material

Bode continued to list *Georgs Harfe* in additions to his popular astronomical instruction. Its continued appearance reflects Bode’s personal constellation system, not universal acceptance.

### 1822 — Alexander Jamieson

Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas*, plate 24, restored the label *Psalterium Georgii* but did not restore Hell’s ancient psaltery design.

Instead, Jamieson created an ornate upright harp whose upper frame is a winged nude female figure. The base extends into Eridanus and appears to share δ and ε Eridani. Five core stars agree with Hell and Bode, but several edge identifications do not.

### 1824/1825 — *Urania’s Mirror*

Sidney Hall’s engraved plate 28 in *Urania’s Mirror* copied Jamieson’s treatment closely, including the winged figurehead. The plate is titled:

*Psalterium Georgii, Fluvius Eridanus, Cetus, Officina Sculptoris, Fornax Chemica, and Machina Electrica.*

The first issue is generally dated November 1824; the better-known second issue is 1825. The Library of Congress catalogues the plate as 1825.

### 1829 — Kornelius Reissig

The first major Russian celestial atlas by Kornelius Reissig included a simple triangular form under a translated or transliterated “George’s Harp” label. This documents limited continental reception beyond Bode’s immediate German audience.

### 1830s–1870s — decline

The figure survived in some educational and popular works but increasingly as an antiquarian curiosity.

William Henry Smyth condemned patronal and political constellations, writing that figures such as *Scutum Sobieskii*, *Honores Frederici*, *Taurus Poniatowski*, *Robur Caroli*, *Sceptrum Brandenburgicum*, and *Harpa Georgii* should be “swept away.” His rhetoric reflects a nineteenth-century movement toward neutral, stable nomenclature.

Elijah Burritt repeated Smyth’s colorful observation that Hell had “squeezed in his Harpa Georgii” and “filched from Eridanus about thirty or forty stars.” The number is an exaggeration compared with Hell’s own explicit twenty-one.

Some nineteenth-century sources continued to list it:

- English educational atlases influenced by Jamieson and Bode;
- French lists as *La Harpe de George*;
- German planispheres as *Georgs Harfe*;
- Flammarion-era enumerations of modern constellations;
- Adolf Stieler’s planisphere.

Argelander’s *Uranometria Nova* of 1843 and Eduard Heis’s *Atlas Coelestis Novus* of 1872 were influential in narrowing practical constellation usage. Harpa Georgii did not acquire a durable scientific role comparable with Hevelius’s or Lacaille’s accepted figures.

### 1899 — Richard Hinckley Allen

Allen’s *Star Names and Their Meanings* summarized the figure but introduced or perpetuated three important errors:

- invention in 1781 rather than documented publication in 1789;
- dedication to George II rather than George III;
- inclusion of ο² Eridani as its conspicuous star.

Because Allen was extraordinarily influential, these details passed into twentieth- and twenty-first-century web reference pages.

### 1922–1930 — exclusion and territorial extinction

- **1922, Rome:** the IAU adopted the standard constellation list and abbreviations. Harpa Georgii was absent.
- **1925, Cambridge:** Delporte presented a systematic boundary proposal.
- **1928, Leiden:** the IAU approved the completed boundaries.
- **1930:** Delporte published *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* and the associated atlas.

The principal harp stars became definitively Taurus and Eridanus stars. Harpa Georgii ceased to have even an informal territorial role in professional positional astronomy.

### Present

It survives as:

- a historical constellation;
- an atlas image in museum and library collections;
- an entry in histories of obsolete constellations;
- an optional observing reconstruction or historical asterism;
- a case study in royal patronage, constellation proliferation, and copied cataloguing errors.

It is not a modern scientific constellation.

## Invention, meaning, and reception

## Why George III?

**Documented fact:** Hell explicitly says the psaltery symbolizes George III’s religion and piety.

**Historical reconstruction:** George III’s connection to the astronomical episode ran through Herschel:

- Herschel discovered Uranus in 1781.
- Herschel proposed *Georgium Sidus* for the planet.
- George III granted Herschel patronage and enabled him to pursue astronomy professionally.
- Hell admired Herschel’s telescopic work and placed two Herschel telescopes in the sky alongside the royal psaltery.

Thus the three constellations form a coordinated commemorative ensemble: observer, instruments, discovery, and patron.

## Was the harp chosen because Herschel was a musician?

**Modern tradition / unverified motive:** Herschel had indeed been a professional musician and composer, so the association is attractive.

**Evidence against making it Hell’s primary motive:** Hell’s surviving explanation discusses David, the ten strings, the Decalogue, Temple worship, Jerome, Augustine, and George III’s piety. It does not say “I chose a harp because Herschel was a musician.”

The musical explanation should therefore be labeled a later interpretation, not a quotation of Hell’s intention.

## Was it taken from George III’s royal arms?

A modern Russian-language account suggests that the harp was selected because the royal arms contained the English lion, Scottish unicorn, and Irish harp; since Leo and Monoceros already existed, the harp remained available.

**Status: unverified modern speculation.** No such reasoning has been located in Hell’s text. Moreover, Hell depicts a Davidic ten-string psaltery, not specifically the Irish heraldic harp. The royal-arms parallel may have aided later reception but is not evidenced as the invention’s cause.

## “Harp of David”

**Documented:** Hell explicitly wrote *sub figura … Psalterii Davidici, decem chordarum*.

**Not documented:** that ancient Jewish or Christian astronomers recognized a constellation called David’s Harp in these stars.

The correct formulation is: **Hell created a new eighteenth-century constellation and dressed it in biblical-Davidic iconography.**

## Myths and legends

## No classical catasterism

Harpa Georgii has no Greek or Roman catasterism. It does not occur in:

- Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* or its Epitome;
- Hyginus’s *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*;
- Aratus or the scholia to Aratus;
- Germanicus’s *Aratea* or its scholia;
- Ovid;
- Manilius;
- Nonnus;
- Pseudo-Apollodorus;
- Pausanias;
- the classical mythographers.

No god, hero, king, or Muse placed George’s Harp in the sky. Maximilian Hell did so in 1789.

### Relationship to Lyra

**Popular confusion:** because Lyra is an ancient stringed-instrument constellation, modern retellings sometimes transfer the myths of Hermes, Apollo, Orpheus, and the lyre to Harpa Georgii.

**Finding:** there is no historical basis for that transfer. Lyra is far away in the northern sky and has a separate ancient tradition. Harpa Georgii was neither a second Lyra nor a variant of it.

### Relationship to Eridanus myths

Some of the same sky belonged visually or catalogually to Eridanus, whose classical traditions included:

- the river into which Phaethon fell after Zeus struck him down;
- Oceanus;
- the Nile;
- the Po/Padus;
- occasionally other celebrated terrestrial rivers.

These myths belong to **Eridanus**, not to Harpa Georgii. Bode’s text merely says the new harp’s stars formerly belonged to the river.

### Biblical tradition: David’s instrument

Hell’s religious iconography draws principally on:

- Psalm 32:2 in the Vulgate numbering, corresponding to Psalm 33:2 in many modern Bibles: praise on a ten-stringed psaltery;
- Jerome’s discussions of psaltery and cithara;
- Augustine’s descriptions of their differently situated resonating bodies;
- the broad biblical tradition of David as psalmist and musician.

**Tradition:** medieval and early modern Christian art regularly portrayed David playing a harp, lyre, psaltery, or other locally familiar string instrument.

**Historical limitation:** biblical Hebrew does not provide a precise modern engineering diagram of the *nevel*. Hell’s pictured instrument is an eighteenth-century antiquarian reconstruction mediated through patristic Latin, not a demonstrably accurate Israelite instrument.

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Babylonian

No Babylonian constellation corresponding to Harpa Georgii has been identified. The region lies south of the zodiacal Bull but the harp’s faint principal stars are not known to have formed a named coherent Babylonian figure.

This does not mean Babylonian observers never recorded any of the stars; it means there is no demonstrated one-to-one antecedent for Hell’s group.

## Egyptian

No Egyptian precursor to George’s Harp has been traced. Claims based merely on Egyptian depictions of harps or David-like musicians are iconographic analogies, not stellar identifications.

## Greek and Latin

The stars lay among or outside Taurus, Cetus, Orion, and Eridanus. Greek Ἠριδανός and Latin *Eridanus* supplied the main inherited neighboring figure. No ancient *psaltērion* or *harpa* occupied this location.

## Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī inherited Ptolemy’s large figures and distinguished constellation stars from external or unformed stars. No Arabic “George’s Harp” exists.

No securely documented collective Bedouin asterism has been found that matches Hell’s five-star core. Some nearby stars could have participated in larger indigenous Arabic sky pictures, but equating all faint Eridanus/Taurus stars with a single Bedouin figure would require evidence not located in the consulted catalogues.

The frequently expected Arabic proper names are absent because the principal stars are comparatively faint and did not retain prominent individual names in the European-Arabic transmission.

## Persian

No classical Persian equivalent matching Hell’s exact group has been traced. Persian astronomical works using the Ptolemaic/Arabic system would normally treat these stars within Taurus, Cetus, or Eridanus, not as a harp.

## Hebrew and Jewish

Hell himself used the Hebrew term *Nablon*, from *nevel*, and invoked the Jerusalem Temple and Mosaic Decalogue.

That is evidence for **Hell’s Christian Hebraic symbolism**, not evidence for an ancient Hebrew constellation. No Jewish astronomical source has been found that maps David’s harp onto these stars.

## Kabbalistic, alchemical, and Hermetic interpretations

No pre-1789 Kabbalistic, alchemical, or Hermetic mapping of this star group as a harp has been traced.

Hell’s companion work on the naming and symbol of Uranus included playful learned discussion of astronomical symbolism and even platinum as a planetary metal. That context shows his comfort with emblematic thinking, but it does not establish a separate alchemical doctrine of Harpa Georgii.

Later occult writers could associate a harp with harmony, David, the Decalogue, or celestial music. No historically continuous Harpa Georgii esotericism has been demonstrated.

## Chinese

The region belongs to the White Tiger of the West and crosses two lunar-mansion systems:

- **胃宿, Wèi Xiù**, the Stomach mansion.
- **昴宿, Mǎo Xiù**, the Hairy Head/Pleiades mansion.

Later Chinese stellar concordances give:

- 10 Tauri: 天囷增十五, “fifteenth additional star of the Circular Celestial Granary.”
- 24 Eridani: 天囷增十四, “fourteenth additional star” of that granary.
- 25 Eridani: another additional Granary star, though numbering varies in secondary tables.
- 17 Eridani: 天苑增十四, an additional star of Celestial Meadows.
- 22 Eridani: 天苑增十六, another additional star of Celestial Meadows.

These Chinese figures are independent of Hell’s harp and should not be represented as translations of it.

## Indian

No nakshatra is centered on the harp’s principal stars. The region lies south of the ecliptic near the Taurus sector, but membership in a broad longitude band does not make the stars constituents of a lunar mansion. No Sanskrit Davidic or harp figure is attested here.

## Julius Schiller’s Christianized sky

Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum* of 1627 predates Harpa Georgii by 162 years. Therefore no saint or object in Schiller can literally “replace” Hell’s constellation.

Schiller reassigned the ancient zodiac and Ptolemaic constellations to Christian figures. The future harp stars would have remained embedded in whatever Christian figures replaced Taurus, Cetus, Orion, and Eridanus or among their unformed surroundings. No separate Davidic harp is documented at this location.

This is a useful negative result: Hell’s Davidic design was not borrowed from Schiller’s Christian atlas.

## Astrology

### Classical astrology

Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* contains no influence for Harpa Georgii. Its stars would inherit whatever qualities Ptolemy gave to neighboring Taurus or Eridanus stars only if a particular star can be securely matched.

### Behenian stars

No principal Harpa Georgii star belongs to the usual list of fifteen Behenian fixed stars. The constellation has no medieval magical seal, herb, or stone assignment in the standard Behenian corpus.

### Lunar mansions

The figure is not itself a lunar mansion. Its proximity to the Taurus sector means its stars lie near the longitudes of the Pleiades/Aldebaran mansion region, while Chinese classifications assign some to the Wèi and Mǎo systems. Longitude alone is not evidence that the entire harp belonged to Arabic *manāzil al-qamar* or Indian nakshatras.

### Modern constellation astrology

Vivian E. Robson’s *Fixed Stars and Constellations in Astrology* (1923) repeated the erroneous 1781/George II account and assigned Psalterium Georgianum a “harmonious and artistic nature” with joviality and kindness.

**Status: modern astrological invention.** No such influence appears in Hell, Bode, Ptolemy, or medieval astrological texts. Later websites have repeated Robson’s statement and assigned zodiacal spans and “orbs” to reconstructed harp stars, but these are twentieth- and twenty-first-century astrological conventions.

## Iconography

### No ancient iconography of Harpa Georgii

The following cannot depict Harpa Georgii because they predate its creation:

- the Farnese Atlas;
- Greco-Roman celestial globes;
- medieval Aratus manuscripts;
- al-Ṣūfī manuscripts;
- Dürer’s 1515 celestial maps;
- Bayer’s 1603 *Uranometria*;
- Schiller’s 1627 Christian sky;
- Hevelius’s 1690 atlas;
- Flamsteed’s 1729 atlas.

At most, they show the same stars as parts of adjacent figures or as unformed points.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer’s Eridanus plate shows 10 Tauri and 32 Eridani near decorative reeds on the riverbank. This visual detail later made it possible to carve the area out of the River, but it is not a proto-harp.

### Cellarius, Amsterdam, 1661

In the southern planisphere of *Harmonia Macrocosmica*, future harp stars lie around the lower part of Cetus’s mouth. The placement illustrates how pre-boundary atlases allowed different figures’ ornaments to occupy the same visual territory.

### Hell, Vienna, 1789

Hell’s original is the foundational image:

- a shallow, ten-stringed psaltery rather than a tall orchestral harp;
- a labeled suspension band;
- ten stars on or associated with the band;
- the body beneath Taurus;
- the entire group above Eridanus and before Cetus.

The suspension band follows Hell’s reading of Augustine: the psaltery had to be carried by the player, therefore it required a supporting strap.

### Goldbach, Weimar, 1799

Goldbach converted the psaltery into an upright harp, tilted eastward. His design used ζ Eridani at the base.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Bode’s copperplate presents an upright harp without Hell’s ribbon. It is visually integrated with the space under Taurus’s foreparts. The instrument is angled and ornamental but lacks Jamieson’s anthropomorphic figurehead.

The Marseille copy describes *Uranographia* as twenty engraved double-page plates, with Harpa Georgii on the plate grouping Taurus, Gemini, Orion, and Canis Minor. Different modern catalogues number the relevant image differently because the planispheres and double-page plates may be counted in different ways; “plate XII” and a higher sequential image number can both occur.

### Bode’s popular atlas, 1805

The 1805 *Vorstellung der Gestirne* gives *Georgs-Harfe* a somewhat different posture and a more ambiguous relationship to Eridanus’s stars.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s harp has a winged female bust or figurehead lifting the strings over an ornate soundbox. No explanatory myth identifies her. Descriptions calling her Urania, an angel, or a particular saint are interpretations unless supported by Jamieson’s text.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824/25

Sidney Hall copied Jamieson’s anthropomorphic harp and produced the best-known popular image after Bode. Punched star holes permitted the card to be held against light.

### Reissig, St Petersburg, 1829

Reissig returned to a simpler triangular harp. The figure’s appearance in this atlas demonstrates transmission through the Bode/Fortin/Jamieson atlas family.

### Mid-nineteenth-century planispheres

Jehoshaphat Aspin-associated movable planispheres and some German educational charts retained the Jamieson-derived figure. Other charts reduced it to a plain harp or omitted it entirely.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

Harps, Davidic musicians, and royal emblems occur abundantly on coins, ceilings, cathedral sculpture, manuscripts, and calendars. No securely documented example has been found in which such an object is explicitly identified as the constellation Harpa Georgii.

George III’s arms contained the Irish harp within the Hanoverian-British heraldic structure, but that does not establish a direct iconographic source for Hell’s psaltery.

## People, events, and works

### Maximilian Hell

Hell was a Jesuit astronomer, director of the Vienna Observatory, calculator and publisher of annual ephemerides, and a celebrated observer of the 1769 transit of Venus from Vardø. His astronomical reputation long suffered from nineteenth-century suspicions about alteration of his transit notebooks; later examination vindicated the underlying observations. That separate controversy should not be transferred to the Harpa publication, whose authorship and date are secure.

### William Herschel

Herschel connects the harp to:

- the discovery of Uranus in 1781;
- the proposed name *Georgium Sidus*;
- George III’s patronage;
- Hell’s two telescope constellations;
- the broader eighteenth-century culture of placing patrons and instruments in the sky.

Herschel did not invent Harpa Georgii.

### George III

The king was the explicit honorand. Hell praised his religion and piety, while later summaries often reduce the reason to “patron of Herschel.” Both are relevant, but the first is Hell’s stated symbolic rationale.

### Johann Elert Bode

Bode was the decisive popularizer. His monumental atlas gave Hell’s short-lived figure its best-known Latin name and image. Bode’s broader program combined classical constellations, recent southern figures, scientific instruments, animals, and political memorials.

The user-supplied phrase “Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)” could not be securely traced to Bode’s own title pages or catalogue in that form. It may describe a particular modern collection, database, visual exhibition, or linked series rather than the historical contents of *Uranographia*. Bode’s accompanying catalogue instead advertises 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters, while modern institutional descriptions count approximately 102 illustrated constellations depending on how subdivisions are handled.

### Navigators and explorers

No voyage or explorer is known to have steered by “Harpa Georgii” as a recognized navigational figure. Its stars are faint, the pattern short-lived, and the region lacks a distinguished navigational star. Mariners could of course observe Taurus, Orion, or neighboring stars, but attributing a voyage specifically to the harp would be unsupported.

### Discoverers of objects

No discovery of a planet, comet, nova, nebula, or galaxy was historically announced as occurring “in Harpa Georgii” in the consulted material. The group’s chief association with discovery is commemorative: it accompanied Hell’s telescope figures honoring Herschel and Uranus.

### Literature, painting, music, buildings, ships, and missions

No securely documented major poem, painting, musical composition, building, ship, spacecraft, or mission named specifically for Harpa Georgii has been traced.

The separate cultural histories of David’s harp, George III, Herschel, and Uranus are extensive, but treating every work about them as a Harpa Georgii work would manufacture an association absent from the sources.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. 1781 or 1789?

**Documented answer:** 1789.

Hell’s *Monumenta* and the Vienna *Ephemerides* provide direct dated evidence. “1781” is almost certainly confusion with Herschel’s discovery of Uranus.

**Spread of the error:** Richard Hinckley Allen’s influential 1899 summary; later encyclopedias, obsolete-constellation pages, astrology books, and translated websites.

## 2. George II or George III?

**Documented answer:** George III.

Hell wrote *Georgii III*; Bode likewise wrote *Georg III*. George II had died in 1760, before Uranus’s discovery and the constellation’s creation.

**Spread of the error:** Allen and Vivian Robson, followed by websites copying them.

## 3. Who renamed it Harpa Georgii—Bode or Lalande?

The common, well-documented atlas occurrence is Bode’s *Harpa Georgii* in 1801. Some modern compilations credit Jérôme Lalande with the renaming.

**Status: disputed attribution.** In the consulted material no primary Lalande chart or text was located that securely predates Bode with the exact name *Harpa Georgii*. Until such an edition and page are produced, “renamed by Lalande” should be treated as unverified; Bode is the first securely demonstrated user in this dossier.

## 4. Did Bode invent the harp?

No. Hell invented the constellation; Goldbach had already converted it to an upright harp by 1799; Bode popularized the name and a related upright design in 1801.

## 5. Was it a harp, psaltery, lyre, or lute?

Hell’s word and discussion specify a *psalterium*. His image resembles a shallow psaltery or zither-family instrument more than a modern pedal harp. Bode’s image is recognizably an upright harp. Russian “lute” and some casual English “lyre” translations are approximate and obscure the design change.

## 6. Was ο² Eridani part of it?

**Documented cartographic finding:** apparently not.

Allen stated that the inconspicuous constellation’s exception was 4½-magnitude ο² Eridani. Modern cartographic comparison finds that neither ο¹ nor ο² Eridani lies within the surviving harp drawings. Barentine calls the claim erroneous.

Possible causes include:

- confusion between Bode’s letter labels and Bayer Greek letters;
- expansion of the constellation’s supposed territory beyond its illustrated outline;
- mistaken identification of a bright Eridanus star in a crowded old engraving.

## 7. Was 10 Tauri or “14 Tauri” the principal star?

The secure identification is **10 Tauri**. A Spanish popular article calls the principal star 14 Tauri, but Hell’s γ and Bode’s E align with 10 Tau. “14” is best treated as a secondary-source error unless a distinct atlas tradition can be produced.

## 8. Twenty-one stars or thirty to forty?

Hell says twenty-one. Smyth’s “thirty or forty,” repeated by Burritt, is rhetorical exaggeration or a count including faint background stars caught inside a broad outline.

## 9. Did its stars all return to Eridanus?

No. Most of the secure later stars are in Eridanus, but 10 Tauri and some of Hell’s additional points are in Taurus; the original drawing also used stars now assigned to Cetus in some reconstructions. “All returned to Eridanus” is an oversimplification.

## 10. Was it abolished specifically in 1922?

It was excluded from the IAU list in 1922. Its former sky area was definitively partitioned only through the 1925–1930 boundary process. There is no evidence of a resolution directed individually against Harpa Georgii.

## 11. Why did it fail?

No single IAU statement gives a Harpa-specific reason. The best-supported reconstruction combines:

- faint and unmemorable principal stars;
- no ancient or widespread observational tradition;
- inconsistent outlines and memberships;
- competition with established Taurus, Cetus, and Eridanus;
- a politically flattering name tied to one monarch;
- nineteenth-century rejection of proliferating patronal constellations;
- no need for it in stable positional or variable-star nomenclature.

## 12. Was its disappearance censorship of George III?

No evidence supports this. Other patronal figures also disappeared, while the historically successful *Scutum Sobiescianum* survived in shortened form as Scutum. The operative issue was standardization and established usage, not a recorded anti-Georgian policy.

## 13. Hoaxes

No deliberate Harpa Georgii hoax was found. The principal problems are copying errors, mistranslation, and unattributed speculation rather than fabrication:

- 1781;
- George II;
- ο² Eridani;
- 14 Tauri;
- official abbreviation PsG;
- Lalande’s unverified priority;
- royal-arms and Herschel-the-musician motives presented as Hell’s own words.

## Open questions

1. **Complete twenty-one-star concordance.** Hell states the number, but a definitive modern table for every plotted dot requires astrometric reduction of a high-resolution original plate at the exact chart epoch.

2. **Lalande’s role.** Modern reference works credit Lalande with changing *Psalterium Georgianum* to *Harpa Georgii*, but an exact primary edition, page, and date remain to be demonstrated.

3. **First use of *Psalterium Georgii*.** Hell used *Psalterium Georgianum*; Jamieson used the genitival form by 1822. An earlier intermediary may exist.

4. **Goldbach’s sources.** Goldbach’s 1799 upright harp precedes Bode’s famous design. Whether Goldbach independently reinterpreted Hell’s psaltery or followed an unlocated intermediary chart deserves further study.

5. **Bode plate numbering.** Institutional catalogues count the large double-page plates and preliminary planispheres differently. A copy-specific collation would resolve “plate XII” versus higher sequential image references.

6. **Cetus membership.** Hell’s reconstruction includes 94, 95, κ¹, and κ² Ceti, while Bode’s narrower principal set lies overwhelmingly in Taurus and Eridanus. The discrepancy reflects genuinely different conceptions, not merely modern boundary movement.

7. **Chinese “additional stars.”** Exact numbering varies between modern concordances. It should be checked against Qing-period catalogues rather than harmonized from secondary web tables.

8. **“830 stars in 109 groups.”** This phrase could not be verified as a description of Bode’s 1801 publication. Identifying the specific “Bode collection” or database intended would permit a separate provenance audit.

9. **Last serious atlas adoption.** Claims that a particular 1849 atlas was the “last” to depict the harp are vulnerable to counterexamples from later retrospective charts, globes, and educational planispheres. “Last” should be used only within a defined corpus.
