# Pyxis: Research Dossier

## Basic identification

**Status — documented fact.** Pyxis is one of the **88 constellations retained by the International Astronomical Union**. It was not abolished. Its official boundaries are those devised by Eugène Delporte, approved by the IAU at Leiden in 1928, and published in *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* in 1930. The modern constellation is therefore a bounded area of sky, not merely Lacaille’s compass-shaped line figure. [IAU constellation history and official list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

### Etymology

**Documented linguistic fact.** Latin *pyxis* means a small box or casket and is borrowed from Greek πυξίς, originally a box—especially one made from boxwood—related to πύξος, “box tree.” It did not originally mean a magnetic compass. A ship’s compass was conventionally housed in a box or compass bowl, allowing early-modern Neo-Latin expressions such as *pyxis nautica* and *pyxis magnetica*. Consequently, the literal translation “nautical box” is philologically defensible, while “mariner’s compass” is the intended technical meaning. [Paris Observatory etymological dictionary](https://dictionary.obspm.fr/terms/pyxis/)

**Documented historical spelling.** Lacaille printed **Pixis Nautica**, with *i*, in the posthumous 1763 *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*. Later astronomers regularized this to the classical spelling **Pyxis**. The original *Pixis* is not a separate constellation and should not be silently treated as evidence for a different etymology. [Lacaille catalogue comparison](https://pbarbier.com/lacaille/lacaille.html)

### Position, area, and boundaries

Pyxis occupies approximately **220.8–221 square degrees**, ranking 65th among the 88 constellations. It borders:

- Hydra to the north;
- Puppis mainly to the west;
- Vela to the south;
- Antlia to the east.

Its approximate modern extent is right ascension 8h–9h30m and declination −17° to −37°, although the legal boundary is Delporte’s polygon rather than those rounded limits. The constellation can be seen in full from much of the Southern Hemisphere and in part from latitudes north to about 50° N. [IAU](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/); [modern chart and object data](https://in-the-sky.org/data/constellation.php?id=70)

**Boundary history — important distinction.**

1. **Ptolemy, second century CE:** there was no Pyxis. Four stars now identified as β, α, γ, and δ Pyxidis were catalogued as parts of the mast of Argo.
2. **Bayer, 1603:** there was no Pyxis. The region belonged iconographically to or lay against Bayer’s enlarged Argo Navis, whose outline derived partly from late-sixteenth-century Dutch southern mapping.
3. **Hevelius, 1690:** no Pyxis; Argo remained a single ship.
4. **Lacaille, observations 1751–52; publication 1756:** *la Boussole* appeared as an independent instrument near Argo.
5. **Lacaille, 1763:** *Pixis Nautica* received its own Greek letters. Argo itself continued in the catalogue, subdivided for practical stellar nomenclature into keel, stern, and sails.
6. **Bode, 1782 and 1801:** Pyxis was accepted; in 1801 Bode wrapped his new *Lochium Funis*—log and line—around it and treated the instruments as a composite navigational ensemble.
7. **Herschel and Baily, 1844–45:** Pyxis was proposed for replacement by **Malus**, the mast.
8. **Gould, 1879:** restored Lacaille’s Pyxis in *Uranometria Argentina*.
9. **IAU, 1922:** Pyxis was retained and assigned the abbreviation **Pyx**.
10. **Delporte, 1928/1930:** its exact polygonal boundary was fixed. Stars inside that polygon became legally Pyxidis stars regardless of older pictures.

No neighboring modern constellation “received Pyxis’s stars” in 1930 because Pyxis survived. The important transfers had occurred conceptually earlier: stars represented as Argo’s mast in the Ptolemaic tradition became the core of Lacaille’s independent compass. The modern boundaries also include numerous previously “unformed” stars that no pictorial compass had claimed.

## Stars and objects

### The line figure and Lacaille’s letters

The most recognizable modern asterism is the nearly straight chain:

**β Pyxidis — α Pyxidis — γ Pyxidis**, with δ Pyxidis nearby.

Lacaille assigned ten Greek letters, conventionally listed as:

**α, β, γ, δ, ε, ζ, η, θ, κ, λ Pyxidis.**

There is no surviving ι Pyxidis in this scheme. Historical summaries sometimes say Lacaille skipped both iota and kappa, but κ Pyxidis plainly exists; that wording is an error propagated through derivative sources. [Catalogue and star list](https://pbarbier.com/lacaille/lacaille.html); [Pyxis star list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_stars_in_Pyxis)

**Flamsteed numbers.** Pyxis has no standard Flamsteed-number sequence. Flamsteed’s original numbered catalogue did not establish a Pyxis constellation, which postdates him and lies in the far-southern zone poorly covered from Greenwich. Numbers occasionally encountered in later catalogues belong to other systems, not canonical “1 Pyxidis,” “2 Pyxidis,” and so forth.

### Proper names

**Official fact.** No star in Pyxis currently has an IAU-approved proper name. Alpha Pyxidis is officially **α Pyxidis**, not “Al Sumut.”

#### “Al Sumut”

**Disputed modern name.** Numerous websites and some popular star-name compilations call α Pyxidis **Al Sumut**, deriving it from Arabic السموت, *al-sumūt*, “the directions/ways/bearings.” The word is genuinely Arabic: *al-sumūt* is the plural of *samt*, and medieval Latin borrowing produced *azimut(h)*.

However:

- the name is not in the IAU catalogue of approved stellar proper names;
- it is not a traditional name reported for this star by al-Ṣūfī;
- it cannot be ancient for “the compass star,” since Pyxis is an eighteenth-century constellation;
- no early Arabic manuscript attestation tying السموت specifically to α Pyxidis was located.

The most cautious classification is **modern learned invention or untraced modern attribution**, probably coined from the semantic connection between azimuth and compass bearings. Repetition in modern databases is not evidence of medieval use. The popular claim appears in several language Wikipedias and derivative sites but not in the historical Arabic catalogue tradition.

### Ptolemy’s four mast stars

Ptolemy’s *Almagest* VII–VIII catalogue, Argo entries 27–30, describes:

| Ptolemy’s description | Modern identification |
|---|---|
| “The southernmost of the two in the middle of the mast” | β Pyxidis |
| “The northernmost of them” | α Pyxidis |
| “The preceding of the two by the tip of the mast” | γ Pyxidis |
| “The following of them” | δ Pyxidis |

The Greek includes ἱστός, *histos*, “mast.” These identifications are standard modern reconstructions, not names used by Ptolemy. [Greek and Toomer-based table](https://xing.fmi.uni-jena.de/mediawiki/index.php/Pyxis)

### Variable stars and novae

#### T Pyxidis

**Documented fact.** T Pyxidis is the constellation’s most historically important stellar object and the prototype of a recurrent-nova class. It is a very close cataclysmic binary in which a white dwarf accretes matter from a low-mass companion. The photometric/orbital period is about 1.8 hours.

Recorded eruptions occurred in:

- 1890;
- 1902;
- 1920;
- 1944;
- December 1966, often labeled “1967” because maximum and much of the observing campaign fell in January 1967;
- 14 April 2011.

This explains the apparently conflicting lists “1966” and “1967”: they refer to the same eruption, dated either from discovery or maximum.

The 2011 eruption was detected visually by Mike Linnolt at magnitude 13.0 on 14 April and then confirmed as it brightened. It eventually approached sixth magnitude. [AAVSO notice](https://www.aavso.org/index.php/tags/recurrent-novae); [2011 eruption study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.0065)

Hubble observations mapped a clumpy, disk-like or ring-like distribution of ejecta illuminated by the 2011 outburst. NASA reported a light-echo distance near 15,600 light-years, although published distance and white-dwarf-mass estimates have varied substantially with method. [NASA/Hubble](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/hubble-maps-3-d-structure-of-ejected-material-around-erupting-star/)

**Disputed scientific interpretation.** T Pyx was once discussed as a possible future Type Ia supernova progenitor because its white dwarf might gain mass. More recent mass-budget work argues that its eruptions expel substantially more material than is accreted, making that path unlikely. This is a scientific dispute about long-term evolution, not evidence that it poses a terrestrial threat. [Recent mass-budget paper](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae3aa2)

**Popular-error warning.** Claims that T Pyxidis is about to become a dangerous nearby supernova arose from speculative mass and distance assumptions. The recurrence is a surface thermonuclear nova, not repeated destruction of the star, and neither a date nor an imminent supernova is established.

Other variables include α Pyxidis, κ Pyxidis, θ Pyxidis, and numerous fainter survey variables. No historical naked-eye “guest star” can securely be associated with the modern Pyxis area before modern boundaries existed.

### Exoplanet systems

Among systems assigned to Pyxis are HD 73256/CS Pyxidis, Gliese 317, HD 73267, and HD 77338. The number of confirmed planets changes as catalogues are revised, so “Pyxis has exactly three/four planetary systems” is time-dependent rather than a durable historical fact.

#### NGC 2613

William Herschel described it as faint, extended, brighter in the middle, and resolvable. Its nearly edge-on disk is now known to contain gas, dust, an obscured active nucleus, and evidence of interaction with a small companion. [Historical and modern data](https://www.deepskycorner.ch/obj/ngc2613.en.php)

SN 2023dtc, classified as Type Ib, was reported in NGC 2613 in March 2023. This is an extragalactic supernova whose apparent sky position lies within Pyxis, not an explosion of a constellation star. [NGC 2613](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NGC_2613)

#### NGC 2818 and NGC 2818A

The object historically called NGC 2818 is a bipolar planetary nebula projected against an open cluster, now often distinguished as NGC 2818A. Older radial-velocity work argued that the nebula and cluster were unrelated; photometric and later astrometric studies have revived the case for membership. The issue is a genuine observational controversy, not merely a naming confusion. [2008 analysis](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/386/1/324/978142); [2024 morphology study](https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/stae1013); [recent association study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19287)

#### The Pyxis globular cluster

**Documented fact.** The remote Galactic-halo globular cluster commonly called **Pyxis** or the **Pyxis globular cluster** was recognized in 1995 after Ronald Weinberger found it while searching survey material for planetary nebula candidates. Follow-up by Irwin, Demers, and Kunkel established it as a low-luminosity outer-halo globular cluster rather than a planetary nebula or dwarf galaxy.

**Scientific reconstruction.** Its unusual position, age, chemistry, and orbit prompted suggestions that it was stripped from the Large Magellanic Cloud or another accreted dwarf galaxy. Proper-motion studies have tested associations with the Magellanic system and the ATLAS stream; no single parent has been decisively demonstrated. [Discovery/history summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyxis_globular_cluster); [proper-motion study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.08598); [spectroscopy](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0005215)

### Meteor showers

The common statement “Pyxis has no meteor showers” is now too absolute.

The IAU Meteor Data Center has carried candidate or working-list showers including:

- **January α Pyxidids**, code APY, shower 310;
- **θ Pyxidids**, including a December θ-Pyxidid solution, with nomenclature varying between datasets.

These are weak, survey-derived streams and should not be confused with an established, conspicuous annual shower comparable to the Perseids. Some may remain on working rather than established lists. Older popular guides saying “none” reflect the absence of a recognized major shower, not necessarily the modern MDC database. [IAU MDC](https://www.iaumeteordatacenter.org/); [published shower list including January α Pyxidids and December θ Pyxidids](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220007671/downloads/moorhead_mets22_corrected_page_numbers.pdf)

## Chronology of attestation

### Antiquity: stars present, Pyxis absent

**Before the second century CE — no demonstrated Pyxis.** No Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek, or Roman source names a compass constellation in this region. A magnetic mariner’s compass would be anachronistic aboard Argo and within Greco-Roman astronomy.

**c. AD 127–150 — documented fact.** Ptolemy catalogued four of the principal modern Pyxis stars as Argo’s mast in the *Almagest*. This is the earliest secure textual attestation of a recognizable figure in precisely these stars, but that figure was a mast belonging to Argo, not Pyxis.

### Islamic and medieval transmission

**Tenth century — documented transmission.** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī’s *Kitāb Ṣuwar al-Kawākib* transmitted Ptolemy’s Argo and its stellar descriptions. Pyxis did not exist as an Arabic constellation. Arabic manuscripts depict or catalogue the ship and its mast according to the Ptolemaic tradition.

No secure Bedouin asterism specifically embracing α–δ Pyxidis was found. Their far-southern declination and low altitude from much of the Arabic-speaking world complicate naked-eye vernacular traditions. This is an **absence-of-evidence finding**, not proof that no local seafaring community ever named them.

Hebrew and Persian astronomical catalogues that translated or adapted Ptolemy likewise transmitted Argo rather than a compass constellation. No ancient or medieval Hebrew *Pyxis*, Kabbalistic compass mapping, or Persian proper name for α Pyxidis could be traced to a primary premodern source.

### 1515: Dürer

Albrecht Dürer’s printed celestial maps of 1515 include the classical constellation system and Argo in the southern hemisphere. They do not show Pyxis. Any of the relevant stars belong to the inherited Argo/mast region.

### 1603: Bayer

Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* depicts Argo Navis but no compass. Bayer’s Greek-letter system for Argo was later inherited across Carina, Puppis, and Vela. Pyxis’s separate Lacaille letters demonstrate that it was not treated simply as a fourth fragment of Bayer’s Argo.

### 1627: Schiller

Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, produced with assistance from Bayer and engraved at Augsburg, replaced pagan Argo with **Noah’s Ark**, *Arca Patriarchae Noachi*. Since Pyxis had not yet been invented, Schiller did not Christianize “Pyxis” separately. The relevant mast-region stars were absorbed into his biblical Ark. [Library of Congress facsimile](https://www.loc.gov/resource/rbc0001.2017rosen1317/); [Linda Hall description and plate](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/12-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum/)

### 1690: Hevelius

Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum sive Uranographia* retained Argo. There is no Pyxis and no separate compass.

### 1751–52 / 1756: Lacaille’s invention

**Documented fact.** Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope between 6 August 1751 and 18 July 1752. His planisphere and catalogue paper appeared in the *Mémoires de l’Académie royale des sciences* volume nominally for 1752, actually published in Paris in 1756.

On that planisphere the constellation is **la Boussole**. It is one of Lacaille’s fourteen new southern figures and one of thirteen representing scientific, artistic, or navigational instruments; Mensa, the Table Mountain, is the geographical exception. [Original-planisphere record](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/planisphere-containing-celestial-constellations.html); [Gallica-derived image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lacaille%E2%80%99s_southern_planisphere_of_1756.png)

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Lacaille left no surviving mythic foundation story and no detailed explanation of why he chose a compass specifically. Its location by Argo makes a navigational association obvious, but phrases such as “Lacaille placed Argo’s own compass on its mast” are later interpretation.

**Iconographic fact.** His compass is drawn as an isolated instrument adjacent to a still-recognizable ship. This weighs against calling it formally a fourth subdivision of Argo.

### 1763: *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*

Lacaille’s expanded catalogue and chart were published posthumously under the Latin name **Pixis Nautica**. The constellation received its own letter sequence, separate from Argo’s inherited Bayer series. [Catalogue history](https://pbarbier.com/lacaille/lacaille.html); [1763 Argo/Pixis chart](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lacaille%27s_Argo_Navis.jpg)

### 1776 and 1795: Fortin’s “Flamsteed” atlas

Jean-Nicolas Fortin’s reduced French revision of Flamsteed’s atlas included Lacaille’s southern planisphere and **la Boussole**. This is not evidence that Flamsteed himself invented or catalogued Pyxis: Flamsteed died in 1719, decades before Lacaille’s voyage. Calling Pyxis “in Flamsteed” without specifying the Fortin revision is bibliographically misleading. [BnF record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb407179978); [1776 atlas description](https://mediatheques.montpellier3m.fr/BIBNUMERIQUE/doc/CAMO/1138589/atlas-celeste-de-flamsteed-approuve-par-l-academie-royale-des-sciences-et-publie-sous-le-privilege-d)

The 1795 third edition, revised by Lalande and Méchain, continued the enlarged southern repertoire. [1795 bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Atlas_c%C3%A9leste_de_Flamst%C3%A9ed_publi%C3%A9_en.html?id=Lu960QEACAAJ)

### 1782: Bode’s first reception

Bode included the constellation in *Vorstellung der Gestirne auf XXXIV Kupfertafeln*, his German enlargement of the Fortin–Flamsteed atlas. German labels include forms such as **See Compass/See-Compass**, while associated catalogue glosses and later editions vary. [1782 atlas record](https://bcul.lib.uni.lodz.pl/dlibra/publication/105973/edition/95502)

### 1801: Bode’s *Uranographia*

**Documented fact.** In Bode’s monumental Berlin *Uranographia*, Pyxis appears on Plate XVIII beside Argo, Antlia, and the new **Lochium Funis**. Bode’s companion catalogue, *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne*, grouped Pyxis and Lochium Funis together.

The atlas contains more than 17,200 catalogued objects and over one hundred constellation figures. Claims that this edition comprised “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be reconciled with Bode’s title, catalogue, or library descriptions. That figure may refer to a modern selection, database visualization, or a different “Bode collection,” but it is not the documented object count of the 1801 *Uranographia*. [Digital *Uranographia* record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035); [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148)

#### Bode’s Lochium Funis

*Lochium Funis* represented the chip log and knotted line used to measure a vessel’s speed: the log indicated speed while the compass indicated direction. Bode drew the line curling around the compass and enclosed both in a common constellation boundary. This makes it more accurate to say he **augmented Pyxis with a companion instrument** than that he abolished Pyxis.

*Lochium* is awkward Neo-Latin based on “log”; *funis* is ordinary Latin for rope or line. The figure was not adopted by later standard catalogues and is now abolished. Its stars lie mainly in modern Pyxis and nearby Puppis/Hydra according to their Delporte positions. [Barentine’s study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299642367_Lochium_Funis)

### 1822–24: Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

Alexander Jamieson’s *A Celestial Atlas* of 1822 included **Pyxis Nautica**. The constellation then appeared on Card 32 of *Urania’s Mirror* (first published 1824), together with Hydra, Corvus, Crater, Sextans, Felis, Lupus, Centaurus, Antlia, Argo, and other southern figures.

The cards were advertised as “by a lady,” but research by Peter Hingley identified their designer as the Reverend Richard Rouse Bloxam. The designs closely copy Jamieson. [Royal Astronomical Society](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror); [card list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urania%27s_Mirror)

Elijah Burritt’s *Atlas, Designed to Illustrate the Geography of the Heavens* continued the popular educational atlas tradition and retained the nautical compass in its southern mapping.

### 1844–45: the Malus controversy

**Documented fact.** John Herschel proposed restoring Argo’s classical mast under the Latin name **Malus** and replacing Pyxis. Francis Baily adopted the proposal in the British Association catalogue. A surviving letter from Baily to Herschel dated 15 February 1844 concerns “the arrangement of Malus.” [Royal Society catalogue and manuscript link](https://catalogues.royalsociety.org/CalmView/Record.aspx?id=HS%2F3%2F274&src=CalmView.Catalog)

Some nineteenth-century catalogues accordingly used α Mali rather than α Pyxidis. The U.S. Naval Observatory catalogue, for example, listed modern α Pyxidis as α Mali.

**Dispute’s substance.** Herschel was not merely correcting a Latin word. He was choosing between:

- Lacaille’s independent modern instrument, and
- restoration of the Ptolemaic ship’s mast.

The latter was historically conservative but failed to become universal.

### 1879: Gould

Benjamin Apthorp Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* restored Lacaille’s Pyxis, helping secure it in southern positional astronomy. Malus survived in some British and American school atlases into the early twentieth century but not in the final international system.

### 1887–1910: persistence of Malus

Richard A. Proctor’s 1887 atlas recognized Malus through the British Association catalogue. Winslow Upton used it in 1896. Early editions of Arthur P. Norton’s *Star Atlas* also used Malus; later editions acknowledged its replacement by Pyxis. This was not an IAU abolition followed by revival: it was nineteenth-century catalogue competition before international standardization.

### 1922–1930: IAU settlement

At Rome in 1922, the IAU standardized three-letter abbreviations, retaining Pyxis as **Pyx**. Delporte’s boundaries were approved at Leiden in 1928 and published in 1930. Thus:

- **Pyxis survived**;
- **Malus** disappeared;
- **Lochium Funis** disappeared;
- Pyxis became an official area rather than a negotiable illustration.

## Myths and legends

### The compass itself

**Modern invention, not classical myth.** No Greek or Roman catasterism explains Pyxis. It does not appear in Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi*, the *Epitome*, Hyginus’ *Poetica Astronomica* or *Fabulae*, the scholia to Aratus or Germanicus, Ovid, Manilius, Nonnus, Pseudo-Apollodorus, or Pausanias. These authors could discuss Argo but not an eighteenth-century magnetic compass constellation.

The often-repeated statement “Pyxis represents the compass of the Argo” is a later associative story. Lacaille placed a mariner’s compass beside the ancient ship, but the Argonauts’ vessel possesses no magnetic compass in ancient literature.

### Argo’s catasterism

**Ancient myth associated with the earlier mast, not with Pyxis as such.** Argo was the ship built for Jason’s expedition to Colchis. Ancient accounts variously credit its construction to Argus under Athena’s guidance and say Athena inserted a speaking timber from Dodona.

- Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1.18–19, 1.111–15, gives Athena a role in its construction.
- Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9.16, says Argus built the ship at Athena’s direction.
- Hyginus, *Fabulae* 14, catalogues the Argonauts and ship tradition.
- Apollodorus and Apollonius represent Hellenistic prose and poetry traditions whose materials are older than their surviving texts.

**Catasterism variants.** Eratosthenes’ *Catasterismi* and the later *Epitome* say that Athena placed the ship among the stars because it was the first ship or because of its role in the heroic expedition. Only part of the ship appears, a convention sometimes explained as commemorating its stern-first sailing or its dangerous passage through the Symplegades.

Hyginus, *Poetica Astronomica* 2.37, transmits closely related explanations: Argo was placed among the stars for its unprecedented construction and divinely aided voyage; it appears stern-first, with portions missing.

Aratus, *Phaenomena* 342–52, describes Argo sailing stern-first and only partially visible. Germanicus’ Latin *Aratea* and its scholia elaborate the same astronomical image. These are myths of **Argo Navis**. They apply to Pyxis only insofar as α–δ Pyxidis were once interpreted as its mast.

### The “only anachronism aboard Argo”

**Modern humorous tradition.** The magnetic compass in Lacaille’s vicinity of Argo is commonly called “the only anachronism aboard the Argonauts’ ship.” The observation is materially correct—Greek heroic-age seamen did not use a magnetic ship’s compass—but its formulation is modern popular astronomy, traceable at least to Richard Hinckley Allen’s *Star-Names and Their Meanings* (1899), which calls Pyxis’s association with the Ship “of course … an anachronism.” [Allen, Pyxis](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Topics/astronomy/_Texts/secondary/ALLSTA/Pyxis%2A.html)

It should not be elevated into an ancient myth, nor does it prove Lacaille intended the instrument literally to stand on Argo’s deck or mast.

## Other traditions in the same stars

### Babylonian and Egyptian

**No secure equivalent found.** Pyxis lies far enough south that direct identifications in the standard Babylonian *MUL.APIN* constellation inventory are doubtful. No established Babylonian compass, box, mast, or ship asterism specifically matching α–δ Pyxidis was located.

Likewise, no securely identified Egyptian decan or pharaonic constellation can be equated specifically with the modern Pyxis line. Proposed one-to-one identifications of Egyptian boats with Argo are generally speculative and do not demonstrate a Pyxis antecedent.

### Greek and Roman

The same stars were Argo’s **mast**, as documented by Ptolemy. They did not form a separate *Pyxis*. Latin catalogue terminology uses *malus* for mast; hence Herschel’s nineteenth-century revival.

### Arabic and al-Ṣūfī

Al-Ṣūfī transmitted these stars within **al-Safīna**, the Ship, following the Ptolemaic figure. No evidence was found that he called them a magnetic compass, and “Al Sumut” is not securely traceable to his catalogue.

No documented independent Bedouin figure confined to the four mast stars was found. Claims that “the Arabs called α Pyxidis Al Sumut” should therefore be labeled **unverified** unless an Arabic manuscript, date, and catalogue entry are supplied.

### Persian

Persian Islamic astronomy generally inherited the Arabic-Ptolemaic Ship. Modern Persian قطب‌نما is a translation of the modern constellation concept, not evidence of a pre-Lacaille Persian asterism.

### Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

No ancient Hebrew constellation equivalent to Pyxis has been demonstrated. Biblical-astronomical and Kabbalistic diagrams sometimes map tribes, Hebrew letters, zodiacal signs, or sacred objects onto the sky, but no primary pre-eighteenth-century source was located that assigns α–δ Pyxidis to a compass or special Kabbalistic figure.

Schiller’s Noah’s Ark is Christian biblical iconography, not evidence of Jewish or Kabbalistic continuity.

### Chinese

The exact historical identification is more complicated than popular summaries imply.

Modern Chinese calls Pyxis **羅盤座/罗盘座**, “Compass Constellation.” Traditional Chinese uranography divided the region among asterisms in the southern Vermilion Bird.

Modern comparison tables assign:

- β Pyxidis = 天狗四, fourth star of **Tiāngǒu**, Celestial Dog;
- α Pyxidis = 天狗五;
- γ Pyxidis = 天狗六;
- δ Pyxidis = 天狗七;
- θ Pyxidis = 天廟一, first star of **Tiānmiào**, Celestial Temple.

Tiāngǒu belongs under the lunar-mansion region Guǐ, “Ghost,” and Tianmiao under Zhang, “Extended Net.” [Chinese comparison table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyxis_in_Chinese_astronomy)

**Identification dispute.** Other popular accounts say α, β, and γ Pyxidis belonged to Tianmiao with Antlia stars. Chinese-language summaries note that Tianmiao’s precise stellar membership is uncertain and that it was omitted from some Qing official catalogues. Therefore the neat modern assignments should be treated as **scholarly reconstructions from historical charts**, not indisputable ancient HIP-number identifications. [Chinese discussion](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%BD%97%E7%9B%98%E5%BA%A7)

### Indian astronomy and lunar mansions

No classical Indian nakṣatra is centered on the Pyxis line. The lunar mansions follow the ecliptic, whereas Pyxis is far south of it. Later Indian adaptations of Western constellations translate the mariner’s compass, but this is modern reception rather than an indigenous antecedent.

### Astrology

Because Pyxis is modern and far from the ecliptic:

- it has no sign in classical Greek zodiacal astrology;
- Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* gives no “nature of Pyxis”;
- its stars are not part of the classical Behenian fixed-star list;
- no Arabic, Indian, or European lunar mansion is based on the Pyxis figure.

Modern tropical astrologers sometimes project α–κ Pyxidis onto zodiacal longitude and assign themes of navigation, guidance, or moral direction. These are **modern inventions by analogy with the compass emblem**, not inherited ancient doctrine.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

No primary alchemical or Hermetic text predating Lacaille was found that associates these stars with a compass box. Modern esoteric interpretations—“inner compass,” “true north,” initiation, navigation of the soul—are symbolic elaborations of the eighteenth-century image. They should be described as modern occult reception, not ancient Hermetic star lore.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas

The Roman marble Farnese Atlas, usually dated to the second century CE and probably copying Hellenistic constellation imagery, includes the classical southern ship tradition where preserved, not a compass constellation. Its value for identifying individual Pyxis stars is limited: it carries sculpted figures, not a precise modern coordinate grid. There is no Pyxis object.

### Al-Ṣūfī manuscripts

Illustrated manuscripts of al-Ṣūfī’s *Book of the Fixed Stars* depict each constellation both as seen on a celestial globe and as seen in the sky. Argo is a ship; the modern Pyxis stars occupy the inherited mast region. The manuscripts provide no compass image.

### Dürer, Nuremberg, 1515

Dürer’s printed hemispheres show Argo in the Ptolemaic system. The relevant stars remain within or near its mast. No separate box or compass appears.

### Bayer, Augsburg, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* enlarged and artistically regularized Argo using southern observations available through Dutch voyages. Pyxis is absent.

### Schiller, Augsburg, 1627

Plate 40 turns Argo into Noah’s Ark. The modern Pyxis area is absorbed into that biblical composition. [Linda Hall plate](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/12-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum/)

### Hevelius, Gdańsk, 1690

Hevelius depicts classical Argo according to his globe-oriented convention. No compass.

### Lacaille, Paris, 1756

The first known Pyxis image is a small magnetic mariner’s compass labeled **la Boussole** on Lacaille’s southern planisphere, based on Cape observations. It is adjacent to, rather than convincingly installed aboard, Argo.

### Lacaille, Paris, 1763

The *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* chart labels **Pixis Nautica**. The compass is visually separate from the ship. This image is central evidence against the assertion that Lacaille made Pyxis a fourth formal division of Argo.

### Fortin, Paris, 1776; Lalande–Méchain edition, 1795

Fortin’s reduced *Atlas céleste de Flamstéed* incorporated Lacaille’s southern planisphere. It shows *la Boussole* as a marine compass. The attribution belongs to Fortin’s revised French atlas, not to Flamsteed’s 1729 original.

### Bode, Berlin, 1782

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* accepts the compass, with German labels. Its treatment helped disseminate Lacaille’s southern constellations in German-speaking Europe.

### Bode, Berlin, 1801

Plate XVIII of the *Uranographia* shows **Pyxis Nautica** surrounded by **Lochium Funis**. The log line snakes around the compass, producing one of Bode’s most ingenious composite machine figures. The pairing conveys direction plus speed. Pyxis itself survived; Bode’s addition did not.

### Jamieson, London, 1822

Jamieson’s *Celestial Atlas* depicts **Pyxis Nautica**, retaining the pictorial scientific-instrument tradition.

### *Urania’s Mirror*, London, 1824

Card 32 reproduces Pyxis Nautica among Hydra, Antlia, Argo, and neighboring southern figures. Pinholes mark selected stars. Its image descends closely from Jamieson. [RAS history](https://www.ras.ac.uk/library/treasures-of-the-ras/2428-treasures-of-the-ras-urania-s-mirror)

### Later nineteenth-century atlases

Burritt retained the compass. Proctor and some British catalogues substituted Malus. Argelander’s and Heis’s increasingly scientific atlases deemphasized elaborate imagery, marking the transition from constellation picture to stellar framework. Gould’s *Uranometria Argentina* restored the Pyxis name for positional astronomy.

### Coins, ceilings, portals, and calendars

No ancient coin, cathedral portal, medieval calendar, or Renaissance ceiling can securely depict Pyxis, because it had not yet been invented. A compass, casket, Noah’s Ark, or ship represented in such art is not Pyxis without a demonstrable astronomical label and star arrangement. No securely documented monumental premodern Pyxis iconography was found.

## People, events, and works

### Lacaille and navigation

Lacaille’s Cape expedition was ordered and supported in the context of French geodesy, navigation, and astronomical measurement. He observed nearly 10,000 southern stars and published positions for a smaller principal selection before the full posthumous catalogue appeared. The compass therefore belongs to a programme of Enlightenment instruments rather than a patron-honoring court constellation.

### Explorers and steering

**Negative finding.** No explorer could have said they “steered by Pyxis” before 1756, because the constellation did not exist. Its stars are relatively faint and are not standard oceanic pole-finding stars. Mariners navigated by brighter bodies, altitude observations, and the magnetic compass itself.

Claims that the Argonauts, Phoenicians, Polynesians, Arab pilots, or early Portuguese navigators used “Pyxis” are anachronistic unless they mean only that some of its physical stars were visible.

### Bode

Johann Elert Bode’s 1801 atlas was the culmination of the illustrated European star-atlas tradition, with 20 large plates and a companion catalogue of 17,240 stars, double stars, nebulae, and clusters. Pyxis was retained and expanded iconographically by Lochium Funis. [Bode digital record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035)

### Herschel, Baily, and Gould

John Herschel’s southern observations made him especially attentive to restoring a coherent Argo. Francis Baily implemented his Malus proposal in a catalogue; Benjamin Gould later rejected the substitution. Their disagreement exemplifies nineteenth-century tension between classical iconographic restoration and stable modern nomenclature.

### T Pyxidis observers

Henrietta Swan Leavitt and Edward Pickering’s early work on photographic variables helped establish T Pyx as a recurrent nova after archival eruptions were connected. Amateur and professional observers supplied indispensable light curves, particularly through the AAVSO. The 2011 eruption triggered intensive optical, radio, ultraviolet, and X-ray campaigns. [AAVSO historical account](https://www.aavso.org/sites/default/files/vsots/0402.pdf); [NASA technical report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20140017628)

### Pyxis as a modern project name

“Pyxis” has been reused for technological projects because of its navigational connotations. One current astronomical example is **Pyxis**, a ground-based demonstrator for formation-flying optical interferometry and a proposed future small-satellite system. This is a modern namesake, not an extension of constellation lore. [Project paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.07211)

Numerous businesses, software libraries, ships, fictional works, and musical projects use “Pyxis,” often intending the Greek “box” rather than the constellation. Without explicit authorial evidence they should not automatically be listed as constellation namesakes.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was Pyxis part of Argo Navis?

**Answer: historically connected, but not straightforwardly a fourth subdivision.**

Evidence for connection:

- Ptolemy described α–δ Pyxidis as Argo’s mast;
- Lacaille placed his compass next to Argo;
- later atlas makers visually associated it with the ship;
- Herschel’s Malus proposal deliberately restored the mast.

Evidence against formal membership:

- Lacaille’s 1756 and 1763 pictures show an isolated instrument;
- Pyxis received an independent Greek-letter sequence;
- Carina, Puppis, and Vela retained a single inherited Argo letter series;
- Lacaille continued to catalogue Argo separately.

Therefore “Pyxis was once part of Argo” is a defensible shorthand about stellar and iconographic ancestry, but “Lacaille divided Argo into four constellations, including Pyxis” is misleading. His recognized practical divisions of Argo were keel, stern, and sails; Pyxis was one of his new instrument constellations.

### 2. Did Lacaille put the compass on Argo’s mast?

**Unverified popular reconstruction.** Lacaille’s planisphere places the compass in the mast region, but his surviving text gives no such explanatory sentence. It is safer to say he reused or overlaid stars formerly associated with the mast and pictured a separate compass near the ship.

### 3. The magnetic-compass anachronism

**Documented historical observation, modern framing.** The Argonauts could not have used an early-modern magnetic compass. But Pyxis was not an ancient accessory described in the *Argonautica*; it was an eighteenth-century neighboring emblem. Calling it “aboard” Argo exaggerates the atlas geometry.

### 4. *Pixis* or *Pyxis*?

*Pixis Nautica* is Lacaille’s printed spelling. *Pyxis* is the classical and IAU-normalized spelling. Treating *Pixis* as a different Latin word or a printer’s creation that named a second constellation is erroneous.

### 5. “Al Sumut”

The Arabic etymology is plausible as a phrase meaning directions or bearings, but the star-name attribution lacks a traced medieval source. It is neither approved by the IAU nor demonstrated in al-Ṣūfī. Until an early citation is produced, it belongs under **unverified modern name**, not “traditional Arabic proper name.”

### 6. “No meteor showers”

This was a reasonable older summary for major established showers. Modern survey catalogues include weak candidate January α Pyxidids and θ/December θ Pyxidids. The corrected formulation is: **no prominent established visual shower is traditionally associated with Pyxis, but candidate MDC streams bear Pyxidid names.**

### 7. NGC 2818 versus NGC 2818A

Catalogue usage has conflated the planetary nebula, the projected cluster, and their combined appearance. The further question—whether they are physically associated—has alternated as radial-velocity, photometric, and astrometric evidence changed. Both nomenclature and membership require the author’s definition to be checked.

### 8. Pyxis globular cluster ancestry

Possible association with the Large Magellanic Cloud or a disrupted satellite is a research hypothesis, not established genealogy. Orbital work has constrained but not ended the debate.

### 9. T Pyxidis as a “threat”

Claims of a forthcoming dangerous supernova are sensational extrapolations. Its nova eruptions are real; its eventual stellar fate and mass evolution remain research questions. No source establishes an imminent destructive event.

### 10. Bode’s object and constellation counts

Bode’s companion catalogue explicitly advertises 17,240 objects. Library descriptions differ on whether the plates contain 99, 102, or “more than 100” constellations, depending on whether combined figures and planispheres are counted. The supplied phrase “830 stars in 109 groups” could not be matched to the 1801 original and should not be repeated as a fact about the *Uranographia* without identification of the modern “collection on chain” dataset.

### 11. Lochium Funis as part of Pyxis

Bode enclosed the two in one boundary and catalogued their stars together, so describing it as an extension of Pyxis is supported. But its title and picture identify a distinct instrument; a separate dossier is justified. It did not replace Pyxis, and its disappearance did not abolish Pyxis.

### 12. Chinese membership

Online accounts conflict between Tiāngǒu and Tianmiao assignments. Exact equivalences derive from reconstruction of charts whose scale, copying history, and southern-star coverage vary. Modern HIP identifications should not be mistaken for labels printed beside the stars in the oldest sources.

## Open questions

1. What is the earliest printed occurrence of **“Al Sumut”** specifically attached to α Pyxidis? No Arabic manuscript or early European star catalogue was located that supports it.

2. Did Lacaille leave an unpublished letter, observing notebook, or Academy memorandum explicitly explaining why he selected a compass rather than retaining the mast? His published catalogue supplies the name but not a foundation narrative.

3. Can the exact membership of Tianmiao and Tiāngǒu in the Pyxis–Antlia region be resolved from the earliest surviving Chinese charts rather than Qing or modern reconstructions?

4. Which precise Bode catalogue entries were enclosed within the common Pyxis–Lochium Funis boundary, and how do all of them distribute among modern Pyxis, Puppis, Hydra, and Antlia? This requires a coordinate-by-coordinate reduction of the 1801 catalogue.

5. What source underlies the phrase **“Bode collection on chain (830 stars in 109 groups)”**? It does not describe the full *Uranographia* and may belong to a modern linked-data collection.

6. When did the joking formula “the only anachronism aboard Argo” first appear verbatim? Allen documented the underlying observation in 1899, but an earlier exact wording may exist.

7. Will Gaia-era astrometry and abundance studies identify a convincing parent system for the Pyxis globular cluster?

8. Will the January α Pyxidids and θ Pyxidid solutions achieve established IAU status, merge with another stream, or be removed as weak/duplicate detections?
