# Carina: Research Dossier

## Status and evidentiary key

**IAU STATUS — DOCUMENTED FACT:** **Carina is one of the 88 constellations retained by the International Astronomical Union.** Its official abbreviation is **Car** and its Latin genitive is **Carinae**. Its modern boundaries were approved by the IAU at Leiden in 1928 and published by Eugène Delporte in 1930. The abolished constellation is not Carina but its parent, **Argo Navis**. The former ship’s stars now lie principally in **Carina, Puppis, and Vela**; stars in Lacaille’s separately invented compass, **Pyxis**, occupy part of the same historical sky but Pyxis was not originally a fourth anatomical division of Argo. [IAU constellation history and official list](https://iauarchive.eso.org/public/themes/constellations/)

Labels used below:

- **Documented fact:** directly supported by a primary text, catalogue, observation, institutional record, or securely dated object.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** a modern conclusion drawn from comparative, philological, astronomical, or iconographic evidence.
- **Tradition:** an identifiable inherited interpretation whose historical truth is not implied.
- **Disputed:** evidence or scholarly opinion is divided.
- **Legend/myth:** a narrative presented by its transmitting culture as story, cult history, or sacred history.
- **Modern invention:** a recent name, interpretation, or asterism rather than an ancient inheritance.
- **Unverified:** repeated in secondary or popular literature but not traced here to a reliable primary witness.

## Basic identification

### Name, meaning, and grammar

**Carina** is ordinary Latin for a ship’s **keel**, and by metonymy can mean the hull or ship itself. It is a first-declension feminine noun:

- nominative: **Carina**
- genitive: **Carinae**
- IAU abbreviation: **Car**
- English: **the Keel**
- Spanish: **la Quilla**; older or Gallicizing astronomical writing sometimes has **la Carena**
- French: **la Carène**
- Italian: **la Carena**
- German: **der Schiffskiel**, commonly shortened in constellation lists to **Kiel des Schiffes**
- Portuguese: **a Quilha**
- Dutch: **Kiel**
- Modern Greek: usually **Τρόπις**, “keel,” while the ancient parent ship is **Ἀργώ**
- Modern Arabic: **القاعدة** or **الجؤجؤ** may be used for the keel/bottom of the ship; the classical constellation inherited through Ptolemy is **السفينة**, *al-Safīna*, “the Ship”
- Modern Hebrew astronomical usage: **שדרית**, “keel,” occurs as a translation; no securely attested ancient Hebrew constellation corresponding specifically to modern Carina was found.

**Documented fact:** Carina was not an autonomous Greek constellation. Its stars belonged to **Ἀργώ**, Argo, or **Ναῦς Ἀργώ**, the Ship Argo. Roman authors used **Argo**, **Navis**, **Navis Argo**, **Argolica Navis**, **Argolica Puppis**, **Argoa Puppis**, **Iasonia carina**, and **Pagasaea carina**. In these poetic phrases *carina* usually means Jason’s vessel or hull, not a separately bounded constellation.

### The etymology of *Argo*

**Tradition, ancient:** Hyginus records two explanations in *Astronomica* 2.37:

1. the ship was called Argo because of its speed, Greek **ἀργός** in the sense “swift”;
2. it was named from **Argus/Argos**, its builder or inventor.

The same alternatives circulated through the mythographic tradition. “Bright, gleaming,” another sense of Greek *argos*, is philologically possible but is not securely established as the historical naming motive.

**Disputed:** attempts to derive Argo from Sanskrit *argha*, Noah’s Ark, an Egyptian funerary bark, or a universal prehistoric “solar ship” belong mainly to nineteenth-century comparative mythology. They may identify useful analogies, but no surviving transmission chain proves that Greek Argo was borrowed from those names or figures.

### Position, area, and boundaries

**Documented fact:** Modern Carina covers about **494 square degrees**, making it the 34th-largest IAU constellation. It lies wholly in the southern celestial hemisphere, largely across the rich southern Milky Way. It borders:

- Centaurus
- Chamaeleon
- Musca
- Pictor
- Puppis
- Vela
- Volans

Its nominal extremes are approximately RA 6h to 11h15m and declination −50¾° to −75°, although the official polygon is more complicated than a rectangle.

**Visibility:** the whole constellation is best seen from southern latitudes; only its northern portion rises from approximately 20° N. Canopus can be glimpsed farther north than much of the constellation because it lies at declination about −52°42′.

### What constitutes the figure?

There is no single historically permanent “stick figure.”

- **Ptolemy, second century CE:** catalogued **45 stars** under Argo, with descriptions locating them in the stern, steering-oar, little shield, mast apparatus, deck, and other ship details. He did not recognize a freestanding keel.
- **Aratus, third century BCE:** described a partly dim or starless prow-to-mast region and a brilliant hull, moving stern-first.
- **Bayer, 1603:** pictured the visible afterpart of an ornate vessel and attached Greek letters to Argo stars.
- **Lacaille, 1750s–1763:** tabulated the old ship under component headings corresponding to hull/keel, stern, and sails, while adding Pyxis nearby.
- **Bode, 1801:** still engraved one monumental Argo Navis, with subdivisions functioning primarily as catalogue compartments rather than three unrelated pictures.
- **Delporte, 1930:** replaced anatomy with exact equatorial boundaries. Every point, whether or not occupied by a traditional ship star, now belongs to one IAU constellation.

### Boundary changes and transferred stars

**Documented fact:** Ptolemy’s constellations were catalogued figures, not exhaustive tiled territories. His “outside” stars could later be absorbed into neighboring figures. Bayer and Hevelius similarly drew overlapping artistic figures without modern borders.

The most consequential reallocations were:

- **Crux:** Ptolemy treated the stars of the Southern Cross as part of Centaurus, not Argo. It later became independent.
- **Columba:** introduced beside Argo in the early-modern period, taking sky northwest of Puppis rather than stars from Carina proper.
- **Robur Carolinum:** Edmond Halley’s “Charles’s Oak” occupied stars now in Carina and Vela. Its brightest component was β Carinae. The figure was rejected and its stars returned to the ship divisions.
- **Pyxis:** Lacaille created the Mariner’s Compass in a previously poorly filled area adjoining Argo. Its stars do not inherit Argo’s Bayer sequence. Claims that Pyxis is simply “the fourth piece” of Argo are a convenient modern visualization, not Lacaille’s original anatomical scheme.
- **Volans, Pictor, Musca, Chamaeleon:** early-modern southern figures fixed the southern and western environment subsequently formalized by Delporte.
- **Carina–Vela:** the False Cross straddles the modern border because the old ship figure had no such internal territorial frontier.

### Bayer letters and Flamsteed numbers

Carina contains, among others:

**α, β, ε, η, θ, ι, υ, ω**, and numerous lower-case Roman-letter designations inherited from catalogues of southern stars.

It lacks γ and δ because those prominent Argo letters now lie in Vela; ζ is in Puppis. The sequence therefore remains a fossil of the undivided ship.

**Important correction — documented with qualification:** It is frequently said that Lacaille simply “kept Bayer’s letters.” Bayer did letter Argo in 1603, but Lacaille substantially regularized and reassigned the lettering across the entire ship in his southern catalogue. The modern distribution is best understood as the inherited **Argo sequence as standardized through Lacaille**, not an untouched transplant of every Bayer identification.

**Flamsteed numbers:** Carina has no normal sequence of Flamsteed numbers. Flamsteed’s catalogue was based on stars observable from England and did not number the deep southern sky. Numbers sometimes encountered beside Carina stars come from other catalogues—Lacaille, Gould, Cape catalogues, HD, HR, HIP—not from a genuine “Flamsteed Carina” series.

### Canopus

**Documented fact:** Canopus is α Carinae, an A-type bright giant/supergiant roughly 300 light-years away and the second-brightest stellar point in the night sky after Sirius.

**Ancient name:** Ptolemy’s catalogue gives Κάνωβος; Hipparchus has Κάνωπος. Latin forms include **Canopus** and **Canobus**.

**Myth/tradition:** Canopus was Menelaus’s helmsman, who died in Egypt and was buried near the western mouth of the Nile. Later accounts commonly say a snake killed him. The Egyptian port of Canopus was then etiologically said to bear his name. This is a Greek explanatory legend, not secure Egyptian history.

**Disputed etymology:** Ancient and early-modern writers also reported that the Egyptian place-name predated Menelaus. Proposed Egyptian/Coptic derivations such as **Kahi Nub**, “golden earth,” were popularized by early-modern and nineteenth-century writers but are not accepted without difficulty by modern Egyptologists. The Canopus Decree preserves a native place-name closer to **Karob/Karōb**, weakening simplistic derivations from either the Greek pilot or *Kahi Nub*. Michel Malaise’s study concludes that the traditional Greek pilot-etymology is inadequate and that an Egyptian origin should be sought. [Malaise’s discussion](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ephe_0000-0002_1992_num_105_101_14724)

**Arabic tradition:** Canopus is pre-eminently **Suhayl** (سهيل). Arabic authors applied qualified *Suhayl* names to several bright southern stars, so “Suhail” does not always mean Canopus without context. Al-Sufi reports that Canopus could be called simply *Suhayl*, while neighboring Argo stars bore expressions such as *Suhayl al-Wazn*, “Suhayl of the weight,” *Suhayl Ḥaḍār*, and *Suhayl al-Muḥlif*. The common modern translation “the glorious one” is traditional but the word’s deeper etymology remains debated.

### Miaplacidus

**Documented fact:** β Carinae was α of Halley’s obsolete **Robur Carolinum**.

**Documented textual history:** Richard Hinckley Allen found **Maia placidus** in Elijah Burritt’s 1835 atlas and **Miaplacidus** in the 1856 *Geography of the Heavens*.

**Disputed reconstruction:** William Higgins suggested an Arabic–Latin hybrid, Arabic **miyāh**, “waters,” plus Latin **placidus**, “calm”: “calm waters.” No Arabic star-name of precisely this form has been recovered. It may instead be a cartographic corruption whose exemplar is lost. The IAU standardized the spelling **Miaplacidus** in 2016, but standardization does not authenticate the conjectural etymology. [Allen’s historical discussion](https://www.microscopemuseum.eu/bookstelescopes/R_Allen_1899_Star_names_and_their_meanings.pdf)

### Avior

**Modern invention, documented:** **Avior** is not Greco-Roman or Arabic. In the late 1930s the Royal Air Force asked HM Nautical Almanac Office for unique names for all Air Almanac navigation stars. ε Carinae and α Pavonis lacked convenient names; the office supplied **Avior** and **Peacock**. Suggested Latin connections to *avis*, “bird,” *aviator*, or *avius*, “out-of-the-way,” are retrospective possibilities, not a securely documented naming rationale. [Nautical Almanac Office name history](https://www.obliquity.com/skyeye/misc/naoname.html)

### Aspidiske, Turais, and Scutulum

**Documented:** **Aspidiske** transliterates Greek ἀσπιδίσκη, “little shield.” Ptolemy’s ship included a small shield-like stern ornament, and the name ultimately arose from that anatomical description.

**Tradition:** **Scutulum**, Latin “little shield,” translates the same idea.

**Disputed identification:** **Turais/Tureis** derives through Arabic descriptions involving a “small shield,” but older catalogues applied related forms inconsistently to stars in Argo. Modern standardization assigns **Aspidiske** to ι Carinae; historical Turais is not a reliable one-to-one identifier without edition and context.

## Asterisms

### Diamond Cross

**Modern southern-sky asterism:** β Carinae (Miaplacidus), θ Carinae, υ Carinae, and ω Carinae form an elongated diamond or cross entirely within Carina. It is fainter and less cruciform than Crux.

### False Cross

**Traditional navigational asterism of uncertain first printed attestation:** ε Carinae (Avior), ι Carinae (Aspidiske), δ Velorum (Alsephina), and κ Velorum (Markeb). It is larger than Crux and can mislead inexperienced observers seeking the south celestial pole. The name is not classical and its precise first coinage remains open. [False Cross identification](https://www.constellation-guide.com/false-cross/)

### Southern Pleiades

**Modern popular name:** **IC 2602**, centered visually near θ Carinae. It was observed and catalogued by Lacaille at the Cape in 1751–52. The comparison with the northern Pleiades is descriptive, not a genetic continuation of Greek Pleiades myth.

## Eta Carinae and its eruptions

### Identification and early record

The star now called η Carinae had several historical designations, including **η Argus**. Edmond Halley catalogued it in 1679 at about fourth magnitude. Earlier proposed identifications in sixteenth- and early-seventeenth-century charts are uncertain because positions and magnitudes are insufficiently precise.

**Documented:** William Burchell’s 1827 observations provide the first definite record of its variability. John Herschel monitored it at Feldhausen near Cape Town between 1834 and 1838. [Revised historical light curve](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/415/3/2009/1043616)

### Great Eruption

**Documented observational history:** The star brightened sharply late in 1837/1838. It underwent additional peaks, reaching approximately magnitude −1 in March 1843—briefly rivaling or surpassing Canopus—and remained violently variable through the 1850s.

Calling the event simply “1837–43” captures its major rise and brightest peak but truncates the longer high state. Modern literature commonly dates the **Great Eruption to about 1838–1858**.

**Documented physical interpretation:** Eta Carinae expelled more than ten solar masses in an event with a substantial fraction of supernova energy but survived. It is therefore the prototype of a **supernova impostor**, not a historical supernova. Light echoes reveal spectra characteristic of an unexpectedly cool explosive outflow. [Nature light-echo study](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10775) [Great Eruption review](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11166)

### Homunculus Nebula

**Documented:** The bipolar, hourglass-shaped **Homunculus Nebula** is ejecta from the nineteenth-century eruption. Proper-motion expansion dates it consistently to the eruption era. The name “Homunculus” is a later visual nickname, not Lacaille’s or Herschel’s.

A smaller internal bipolar structure, the **Little Homunculus**, is associated with a lesser eruption around 1890.

### Keyhole Nebula

The **Keyhole Nebula** is a dark and bright cloud complex embedded in NGC 3372 near Eta Carinae. It is not synonymous with either Eta Carinae or the Homunculus. Popular captions often conflate the three.

NASA describes it as a prominent embedded feature of the wider Carina Nebula. [NASA Carina Nebula details](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/hubble/carina-nebula-details/)

### Indigenous Australian record

**Scholarly reconstruction, debated in detail:** Duane Hamacher and David Frew proposed that a Boorong tradition and associated nineteenth-century ethnographic drawing preserve awareness of Eta Carinae’s Great Eruption. The astronomical fit is plausible and was published in a specialist historical-astronomy journal, but interpretation depends on the dating and transmission of ethnographic material. It should not be reported as an independently dated Aboriginal written observation. [Hamacher and Frew study](https://old.narit.or.th/files/JAHH/2010JAHHvol13/2010JAHH...13..220H.pdf)

## Other notable variable stars and transients

- **AG Carinae:** luminous blue variable surrounded by its own ejecta nebula. It lies in Carina but outside NGC 3372.
- **V382 Carinae:** yellow hypergiant and irregular/semiregular variable.
- **RT Carinae:** red supergiant or hypergiant variable in the Carina complex.
- **V533 Carinae:** luminous A-type/Alpha-Cygni variable.
- **OY Carinae:** eclipsing SU Ursae Majoris-type dwarf nova; important for accretion-disc studies.
- **QU Carinae:** nova-like cataclysmic variable sometimes discussed as a possible Type-Ia-supernova progenitor. That is a research hypothesis, not a prediction that an explosion is imminent.
- **V365 Carinae / Nova Carinae 1948:** recognized retrospectively; its eruption was not observed in real time.
- **V906 Carinae / Nova Carinae 2018:** bright classical nova whose gamma-ray and optical behavior helped establish shocks as an important nova power source.

**Historical-supernova finding:** No securely identified ancient or medieval naked-eye supernova is assigned to modern Carina. Eta Carinae survived and must not be entered as one.

## Chronology of attestation

### Before Greek Argo

**Babylonian — documented star, no documented Carina ship:** Canopus was associated in Mesopotamian star lists with **Eridu**, the southern cult city of Ea/Enki, and appears in the *Three Stars Each* tradition and *MUL.APIN*. This is an antecedent for the star, not evidence for a Babylonian Argo-shaped constellation.

**Disputed:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century authors compared Argo with the boat of Utnapishtim in the Gilgamesh flood story. No Mesopotamian catalogue has yet been shown to group the principal stars of Carina, Puppis, and Vela as that flood boat. Thus “Argo is the Babylonian ark” is unproven.

**Egyptian — documented ship imagery, uncertain stellar identity:** Solar and funerary barks are central to Egyptian religion, and Canopus was visible from Egypt near the southern horizon. But no securely identified pharaonic constellation map equates the modern Carina stars with Osiris’s bark. Claims that Argo was directly borrowed from an Egyptian Boat of Osiris are scholarly conjecture or popular tradition, not a demonstrated textual lineage.

### Eighth–seventh centuries BCE: Homer and early epic

**Myth, earliest surviving literary anchor:** Homer’s *Odyssey* 12.69–72 calls the Argo **πᾶσι μέλουσα**, “known to all,” and says it alone passed the Wandering Rocks on its voyage from Aeetes. Homer does not describe a constellation or narrate the whole expedition; the allusion proves that an Argo voyage was already famous.

Hesiodic and genealogical epic fragments show early Argonautic material, but the attribution and dating of individual fragments are difficult.

### Fifth century BCE: Pindar and tragedy

**Documented mythic narrative:** Pindar’s *Pythian* 4, composed in 462 BCE, gives the earliest substantial surviving connected treatment: Pelias’s oracle, Jason’s arrival, the gathering of heroes, voyage, Medea’s aid, and Golden Fleece.

Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides wrote Argonautic dramas, many lost. Euripides’ surviving *Medea* begins after the voyage but presupposes Jason’s oath, Medea’s betrayal of family, and flight from Colchis.

### Fourth–third centuries BCE: constellation attestation

Eudoxus’s lost *Phaenomena*, known through Aratus and Hipparchus, likely included Argo as a celestial figure.

**Documented, c. 270 BCE:** Aratus, *Phaenomena* 342–352, places Argo beside the Great Dog and describes it being hauled **stern-first**, like a ship backing into harbor. Its prow-to-mast portion is dim or starless; the hull is bright; the steering-oar is detached beneath the Dog’s hind legs. [Aratus text and translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/AratusPhaenomena.html)

### Third century BCE: Apollonius of Rhodes

Apollonius’s *Argonautica* is the fullest surviving ancient epic of the expedition.

- 1.18–19: Argus builds the ship under Athena’s direction.
- 1.111–14: Athena’s assistance is emphasized.
- 1.524–27: a divine beam cut from the speaking oak of Dodona is fitted into the vessel and urges the heroes to depart.
- Books 1–2: muster, Lemnos, Cyzicus, loss of Hylas and Heracles, boxing Amycus, Phineus, Harpies, and passage through the Clashing Rocks.
- Book 3: Medea falls in love through divine intervention and helps Jason perform Aeetes’ trials.
- Book 4: theft of the Fleece, death of Apsyrtus, Circe’s purification, Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, Phaeacians, Libya, the Hesperides, and Talos.

[Apollonius, Book 1](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApolloniusRhodius1.html)

### Pseudo-Eratosthenes, *Catasterismi* 35

**Textual caution:** The surviving *Catasterismi* is transmitted under Eratosthenes’ name but its exact authorship and relationship to Eratosthenes’ original work are disputed. “Eratosthenes says” should therefore usually be understood as “the pseudo-Eratosthenic catasterism tradition says.”

**Myth/catasterism:** Athena placed Argo among the stars because it was the first ship and because of the glory of the Argonauts’ voyage. It is shown only in part. The catalogue counts **27 stars** in the visible figure in this mythographic tradition, a number different from Ptolemy’s later scientific catalogue because the works count and define components differently.

**Variant:** The loss or concealment of the prow could evoke damage at the Clashing Rocks. This explanation appears in later commentary, but Aratus himself says only that the forward portion is dim; he does not explicitly say the Symplegades broke it off.

### First century BCE–first century CE: Roman poetry

**Catullus, poem 64.1–21:** the Argo is treated as the primal ship whose voyage inaugurates seafaring; the sea-nymphs rise to marvel. This “first ship” motif became enormously influential.

**Propertius 2.24b:** uses **Iasonia carina**, “Jasonian keel/ship.”

**Virgil, *Eclogue* 4.34–36:** predicts another Tiphys and another Argo bearing chosen heroes, making the voyage an emblem of recurring heroic history.

**Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 6.721 onward and Book 7:** tells the Argonautic setting and Jason–Medea cycle; *Metamorphoses* 15.337–38 refers to Argo and the Clashing Rocks. Ovid calls the vessel **Pagasaea carina**, the Pagasaean ship, after its Thessalian building place.

**Manilius, *Astronomica* 1:** calls it **nobilis Argo**, “noble Argo,” in his survey of constellations. He does not give Carina an autonomous astrological character.

### Hyginus, first century BCE/CE compilation

**Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.37 — documented text, mythic content:**

- some derive Argo’s name from speed;
- others from Argus, its inventor;
- many say it was the first ship on the sea and therefore was represented in the stars;
- Pindar located its construction at a Magnesian town later called Demetrias;
- Callimachus placed it near Apollo’s temple at Pagasae;
- Aeschylus and others said Athena joined speaking timber to it;
- only part of the ship appears in heaven, from the stern toward the mast;
- Hyginus explains the incompleteness pastorally: so that people suffering shipwreck would not be terrified by the sight of a complete ship in heaven.

[Hyginus, Latin text](https://latin.packhum.org/loc/899/1/46/113-115%2C171-173%2C248-250%2C267-269%2C680-682%2C683-685%2C692-694%2C725-727)

**Hyginus, *Fabulae*:** the Argonauts are treated across the fabulae on Pelias, Jason, Medea, the Argonaut list, Phineus, and related heroes. The *Fabulae* supply mythographic variants but no independent Carina catasterism comparable to *Astronomica* 2.37.

### Pausanias, Pseudo-Apollodorus, and later mythographers

**Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9.16–28:** offers a compact prose itinerary: Pelias sends Jason for the Fleece; Argus builds the ship with Athena; the heroes assemble; they pass through major adventures; Medea aids Jason; Apsyrtus dies; the Argonauts return. This is one of the most convenient ancient mythographic syntheses, but probably postdates Apollonius.

**Pausanias:** refers to local relics, dedications, genealogies, and monuments of Argonauts at multiple places rather than presenting one catasterism. Such passages document competing Greek civic claims to individual heroes and voyage sites.

**Diodorus Siculus, Book 4:** rationalizes and systematizes the expedition as heroic history.

**Valerius Flaccus, *Argonautica*:** a Flavian Latin epic that reshapes Apollonius; Jupiter presents the voyage as a decisive step in transferring civilization and glory westward.

**Orphic *Argonautica*:** late antique poem narrated by Orpheus, adding Orphic rites and cosmology. Its late date makes it evidence for late reception, not archaic Argonaut belief.

**Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*:** repeatedly treats Argo, its heroes, and the first-voyage motif as exempla, but supplies no securely independent origin for the constellation.

### Second century CE: Ptolemy

**Documented scientific catalogue:** In *Almagest* Books 7–8, Ptolemy catalogued 45 stars in Argo Navis. His descriptions, not territorial boundaries, define their shipboard positions. Modern identifications have been reconstructed by Peters and Knobel and later machine-readable comparisons, but several faint-star matches remain uncertain. [Ptolemy catalogue data](https://cdsarc.cds.unistra.fr/viz-bin/ReadMe/V/61?format=html&tex=true) [Peters and Knobel revision](https://starlinesofalmagest.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Almagest-Peters-Knobel-1.pdf)

### Tenth century: al-Sufi

**Documented:** ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Sufi’s *Kitāb ṣuwar al-kawākib al-thābita*, completed about 964, presents **al-Safīna**, the Ship. It revises Ptolemy’s magnitudes and compares the Greek catalogue with indigenous Arabic star lore.

Each constellation was conventionally drawn twice: as seen in the sky and reversed as on a globe. Surviving manuscripts differ, and their pictures must not automatically be attributed to al-Sufi’s own hand.

Al-Sufi’s discussion preserves the crucial distinction between the imported Ptolemaic ship and Bedouin **anwāʾ** names—especially the family of *Suhayl* names—that did not necessarily imagine one Greek-style vessel.

### Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries

- **Dürer, Stabius, and Heinfogel, 1515:** printed northern and southern celestial maps; Argo appears in the Ptolemaic southern sky. The map is among the earliest printed European star charts.
- **Mercator, 1551 globe:** depicts the afterpart of Argo sailing toward obscuring cloud.
- **Palazzo Besta, Teglio, c. 1550:** ceiling cycle includes Argo as a partial ship entering cloud or mist.
- **Hondius, 1598 globe:** retains Argo in the expanded southern sky.

### Bayer, 1603

**Documented:** Johann Bayer’s *Uranometria* gave Argo a full-page engraved treatment and lettered its stars. His imagery combined the ancient stern-first partial ship with elaborate early-modern naval ornament. Bayer’s coordinate plotting was not identical with Ptolemy’s anatomy, and some letters are difficult to reconcile with later catalogues.

### Schiller, 1627

**Documented:** Julius Schiller’s *Coelum Stellatum Christianum*, Augsburg, plate 40, replaced Argo with **Arca Patriarchae Noachi**, Noah’s Ark. Lucas Kilian engraved the plates; Bayer assisted with the underlying stellar work.

Schiller’s southern figures generally drew on the Old Testament. His reform did not displace classical nomenclature. Andreas Cellarius reproduced the Christianized sky in *Harmonia Macrocosmica* (1660/1661), after which the scheme remained chiefly a learned and artistic curiosity. [Linda Hall Library discussion and plate](https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/out-of-this-world/09-bayer-s-uranometria-and-its-legacy-1603-1705/12-schiller-julius-coelum-stellatum-christianum/)

### Hevelius, 1690

**Documented:** Johannes Hevelius’s posthumous *Firmamentum Sobiescianum*, issued by Elisabeth Koopman Hevelius, depicts Argo as the classical visible stern/hull. The surrounding sky includes Halley’s **Robur Carolinum**, later abolished.

### Lacaille at the Cape, 1751–52; publication in 1756 and 1763

**Documented:** Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille observed from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751–52. His planisphere was published in Paris in 1756 in the *Mémoires de l’Académie Royale des Sciences* nominally for 1752.

**Critical correction:** the 1756 map still pictures Argo as a single ship. The subdivision appears in the catalogue organization, not as three separately illustrated constellations on that planisphere.

Lacaille distinguished:

- **Argûs in carina** — Argo in the keel/hull
- **Argûs in puppi** — Argo in the stern
- **Argûs in velis** — Argo in the sails

He also introduced **la Boussole**, later **Pyxis Nautica**, near the ship.

The posthumous *Coelum Australe Stelliferum* (1763) made the catalogue treatment broadly available. [1756 planisphere record](https://galileo.ou.edu/exhibits/planisphere-containing-celestial-constellations.html) [1763 digital edition](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/content/titleinfo/152572)

### Bode, 1782 and 1801

Bode’s *Vorstellung der Gestirne* (1782) helped popularize both ancient and newly invented constellations.

**Documented, 1801:** In the *Uranographia*, Bode represented **Argo Navis** as a single lavish ship in the southern celestial hemisphere and on the regional plate with Hydra’s tail and Centaurus. Carina is therefore present as the ship’s keel/hull and as a catalogue subdivision, not as the isolated modern outline familiar from IAU charts.

The plate title/context identifies **Argo Navis**. Bode’s German explanatory volume uses ship-part terminology; the appropriate German for Carina is **Schiffskiel**, while the Latin catalogue tradition uses **Carina** or **Argus in carina**.

The *Uranographia* contains 20 large copperplate charts; its companion *Allgemeine Beschreibung und Nachweisung der Gestirne* catalogues **17,240 stars, doubles, nebulae, and clusters**. The Marseille record describes 102 constellation figures in the atlas.

**Contested numerical claim:** “830 stars in 109 groups,” as supplied in the brief, could not be verified as a description of the 1801 *Uranographia*. Richard Hinckley Allen says the old Argo contained **829 naked-eye components**, which may explain “830.” Institutional catalogues instead give 102 figures for Bode and 17,240 catalogued objects overall. “109 groups” may refer to a particular collection or indexing project, not Bode’s own published count. [Bode atlas record](https://odyssee.univ-amu.fr/items/show/1035) [ETH facsimile](https://www.e-rara.ch/zut/doi/10.3931/e-rara-11148)

### Nineteenth-century reception

- **John Flamsteed:** continued to use Navis/Argo in the northern European catalogue tradition, but did not supply a Carina Flamsteed sequence.
- **John Herschel, 1841 and 1844:** argued for formal abandonment of the enormous Argo and treatment of Lacaille’s divisions as constellations. He also proposed turning Pyxis into **Malus**, the Mast; this was not adopted.
- **Alexander Jamieson, 1822:** still illustrated Argo as a ship in *A Celestial Atlas*.
- **Urania’s Mirror, 1824/25:** copied Jamieson’s pictorial vocabulary into pierced educational cards. The set’s authorship, advertised as “by a lady,” was probably the Rev. Richard Rouse Bloxam. [Urania’s Mirror history](https://www.atlascoelestis.com/16.htm)
- **Elijah Burritt, 1835/1856:** transmitted many ship-star names and introduced the problematic *Maia placidus/Miaplacidus* into popular atlases.
- **Argelander:** adopted increasingly catalogue-centered, non-mythographic mapping; the far south remained outside much of his primary observational program.
- **Eduard Heis, 1872:** still printed **Argo** as a ship figure even while astronomical catalogues increasingly used subdivisions.
- **Benjamin Gould, 1877–79:** the *Uranometria Argentina* was decisive in treating Carina, Puppis, and Vela independently for southern positional astronomy. Gould wrote that abandoning venerable Argo caused him regret.

### IAU standardization, 1922–1930

**Documented:**

- Rome, 1922: the IAU standardized the list of 88 constellation names and three-letter abbreviations. Carina was retained; Argo Navis was not.
- Cambridge, 1925: Delporte presented a method for exact boundaries.
- Leiden, 1928: the IAU approved his completed boundaries.
- 1930: *Délimitation scientifique des constellations* published the boundaries in the B1875 coordinate frame.

The instruction to keep already named variable stars in their customary constellations helps explain some irregular boundary steps.

## Classical myths and legends

## The celestial placing of Argo

**Pseudo-Eratosthenic tradition:** Athena placed Argo in the sky to honor the first ship and its heroic voyage.

**Hyginian tradition:** the ship’s first voyage and speed merited stellar commemoration. Hyginus’ special explanation for its incomplete form is compassionate: heaven does not display a complete ship to terrify survivors of wrecks.

**Later variant:** the prow is missing because the Symplegades crushed it. This is a reasonable synthesis of the damaged/partial celestial image with the voyage myth, but it is not stated in Aratus’s description and should not be retrojected into every ancient account.

**Aratean image:** the ship is stern-first because it resembles sailors backing into harbor. This is the earliest surviving explicit explanation of its reversed motion.

## The commission and building

**Myth, first extensively attested in Pindar and Apollonius:** Pelias fears an oracle warning him against a man with one sandal. Jason arrives having lost a sandal while carrying Hera across a river. Pelias sends him to Colchis for the Golden Fleece, expecting him to die.

Argus constructs Argo, generally at Pagasae, with Athena’s aid. Apollonius places a prophetic beam from Zeus’s oracular oak at Dodona in the ship. Ancient sources vary over whether it was in the prow, stem, or another structural position.

**Variant:** Hyginus preserves Pindar’s association with the Magnesian city later called Demetrias and Callimachus’s Pagasaean version.

## Why it was called the first ship

**Tradition:** Catullus, Hyginus, and the catasterism tradition call Argo the first ship to sail the sea.

**Contradiction within myth:** Other Greek stories presuppose boats before Jason—Danaus and his daughters, Deucalion’s flood vessel, Charon’s ferry, and Minos’s navy. Ancient myth was not a single reconciled chronology. “First ship” is a poetic and cultic claim about epochal seafaring, not consistent technical history.

## The Argonauts

Lists vary from source to source; the canonical company includes Jason, Argus, Tiphys, Orpheus, Heracles, Castor, Polydeuces, Peleus, Telamon, Zetes and Calais, Idas and Lynceus, Meleager, Admetus, Euphemus, and many others. Atalanta appears in some lists and is excluded in others. Ancient totals often approximate fifty, matching the vessel’s benches or oars, but no universal roster exists.

## Lemnos

In Apollonius Book 1 and later mythography, the Lemnian women kill their husbands after Aphrodite afflicts them with an offensive odor or after the men take Thracian concubines. Hypsipyle saves her father Thoas. The Argonauts remain with the women until Heracles or another hero urges departure. Jason fathers sons by Hypsipyle.

## Cyzicus

The Doliones welcome the Argonauts. Driven back at night by contrary winds, the heroes fail to recognize their hosts; each side mistakes the other for enemies, and Jason kills King Cyzicus. At dawn the error is discovered and funeral rites follow. The episode makes failed navigation and recognition central to the ship’s tragic history.

## Hylas and the departure of Heracles

Hylas is abducted by water nymphs while fetching water. Heracles and Polyphemus search for him, and Argo departs without them. Sources disagree whether the decision was accident, necessity, divine plan, or Tiphys’s order.

## Amycus

Polydeuces defeats the Bebrycian king Amycus in boxing. Some sources have him kill Amycus; others bind him and force an oath.

## Phineus and the Harpies

The blind prophet Phineus is tormented by Harpies who foul or steal his food. The winged Boreads Zetes and Calais chase them. In return, Phineus tells the Argonauts how to pass the Symplegades.

## The Symplegades

The heroes release a dove between the Clashing Rocks. When it passes with only its tail feathers clipped, they row through; Athena or Hera assists. Argo loses only a stern ornament or part of the vessel in some tellings. After the passage, the rocks become fixed.

The frequent modern claim that this explains the celestial ship’s missing **bow** is anatomically inconsistent with versions in which the stern is grazed. Ancient and modern summaries often conflate these details.

## Death of Tiphys and replacement pilot

Tiphys, Argo’s helmsman, dies among the Mariandyni, commonly from illness. Ancaeus replaces him. This is important because **Canopus was not Argo’s canonical pilot**: Canopus belonged to Menelaus’s later Trojan-cycle voyage. The star-name joins two different nautical myth cycles.

## Aeetes’ trials

King Aeetes commands Jason to yoke fire-breathing bulls, plow a field, sow dragon’s teeth, and overcome the resulting earthborn warriors. Medea gives Jason protective drugs and instructions. He throws a stone among the warriors, causing them to kill one another.

## Medea and the Fleece

Medea drugs or charms the sleepless dragon, allowing Jason to take the Golden Fleece. Versions differ over whether Jason, Medea, or Orpheus subdues it and whether it sleeps or is killed.

## Apsyrtus

The death of Medea’s brother has major variants:

- in one tradition Medea kills and dismembers a child Apsyrtus during pursuit, scattering him so Aeetes must stop to gather the remains;
- in Apollonius, Apsyrtus is an adult commander whom Jason ambushes and kills after Medea lures him to a meeting.

Jason and Medea require ritual purification, supplied by Circe in Apollonius.

## Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, and the Planctae

Orpheus overcomes the Sirens’ song with his music; Butes alone leaps overboard and is saved by Aphrodite. Hera, Thetis, and the Nereids help Argo through western maritime dangers. Homer’s *Odyssey* retrospectively declares that Argo alone had successfully passed the Wandering Rocks.

## Libya and the carrying of Argo

Apollonius has the heroes stranded in Libya, carrying the enormous ship across land. Triton gives Euphemus a clod of earth that becomes connected with the foundation of Cyrene. Pindar uses this episode to link the Argonautic myth with Cyrenaean royal legitimacy.

## Talos

The bronze guardian Talos circles Crete. Medea destroys him by magic or deception, or Poias shoots the vulnerable vein at his ankle. Apollonius presents the episode as one of Medea’s most uncanny displays of power.

## Return, dedication, and death of the ship

Variants place Argo’s dedication to Poseidon at the Isthmus of Corinth or elsewhere. In a later Jason tradition, the aged hero sleeps beneath the rotting ship, and a falling timber kills him. The story is attested in later mythographic summaries and scholia; it is not part of Apollonius’s ending.

## Canopus, Menelaus, and Helen

**Separate mythic cycle:** Canopus/Kanobos pilots Menelaus during his wanderings after Troy. He dies in Egypt, in many late accounts from a snakebite, and is buried there. The city and star are etiologically connected with him.

Plutarch’s *De Iside et Osiride* 22 mentions Canopus in Egyptian religious interpretation. Later Christian authors such as Epiphanius and Rufinus transmit stories of Canopus as a deified pilot and of a water-filled image defeating a Chaldaean fire god. These accounts reflect late-antique polemic about Egyptian cult, not Bronze Age biography. [Study of the late-antique Canopus tradition](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/zac-2022-0020/html?lang=en)

## Other traditions in the same stars

## Arabic and Bedouin astronomy

Al-Sufi’s **al-Safīna** is the translated Ptolemaic figure. Indigenous Arabian observers instead emphasized low southern stars as seasonal and directional markers.

**Suhayl:** The name could denote Canopus absolutely, but qualified Suhayls were attached to other Argo stars. These included:

- *Suhayl al-Wazn*, the “weighty” Suhayl
- *Suhayl Ḥaḍār*
- *Suhayl al-Muḥlif*, the Suhayl associated with an oath
- plural or paired forms **al-Muḥlifān/al-Muḥnithān**, whose manuscripts and vocalizations vary

**Cultural tradition:** Canopus’s heliacal rising was a major seasonal sign and the star a proverbial emblem of the south. The personal name **Suhayl** and Persian literary title *Anvār-i Suhaylī*, “Lights of Canopus,” attest its broad prestige, not a belief in a Greek keel.

## Persian tradition

Al-Sufi was ethnically Persian and worked under Buyid patronage, but wrote his great star book in Arabic. Persian manuscripts and translations continued both the Ptolemaic ship and the name Suhayl.

In Persian poetry **Suhayl** can signify a brilliant but far-southern or rarely seen beloved. The traditional claim that Canopus ripens fruit or improves leather appears widely in Arabic and Persian adab; it is seasonal lore, not physical stellar influence.

No ancient specifically Persian “Carina the Keel” matching the IAU outline was located.

## Chinese astronomy

**Documented ancient star:** Canopus was **老人**, *Lǎorén*, “the Old Man,” later **南極老人**, *Nánjí Lǎorén*, “Old Man of the South Pole.” It was associated with longevity, imperial virtue, and auspicious government. Because it barely rises from northern China, a clear appearance was an important omen.

The star appears on the Dunhuang star atlas, British Library Or.8210/S.3326, usually dated to the seventh century, although plotted too far north. The underlying textual tradition is much older. [Dunhuang atlas study](https://idp.bl.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/THE_DUNHUANG_CHINESE_SKY_-A_COMPREHENSIVE_STUDY_OF_THE_OLDEST_KNOWN_STAR_ATLAS.pdf)

**Later Chinese mapping:** β Carinae, θ Carinae, ω Carinae and nearby stars have been identified with **南船**, *Nán Chuán*, “Southern Boat.” Identifications of its faint members vary among reconstructions because the far-southern sky was difficult from traditional Chinese observing centers.

Modern Chinese **船底座** literally means “ship-bottom constellation,” the conventional translation of Carina; it should not be mistaken for an ancient asterism name.

## Indian tradition

Canopus is **Agastya**, the great southern sage.

**Documented textual tradition:** Varāhamihira’s *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* chapter 12 treats the heliacal rising of Agastya and its terrestrial effects. Agastya is credited mythically with making the Vindhya mountains bow, drinking the ocean, and bringing order or sacred culture southward.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** the sage’s southern movement and the star’s low southern appearance became mutually reinforcing symbolism. Dating Sanskrit passages by Canopus visibility is possible only with careful control of latitude, precession, redaction, and observational conditions; spectacularly early dates claimed in popular archaeoastronomy are disputed. [Study of Agastya/Canopus visibility](https://cict.in/cict2023/wp-content/uploads/2025/3-Highly_Technical_Papers/Parasahara-agastya-visibility-IJHS-2014-RNIyengar.pdf.pdf) [Literary study of *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* 12](https://ojs.unica.it/index.php/rhesis/article/download/5631/5496)

Carina lies far from the ecliptic, so it is not itself one of the 27/28 **nakshatras**.

## Hebrew and Kabbalistic mappings

**Finding:** No ancient Hebrew constellation securely corresponding to modern Carina was traced. Biblical **ʾoniyyāh**, “ship,” and Noah’s **tevah**, “ark,” are ordinary textual objects, not demonstrated names for these stars.

Medieval and early-modern Christian Hebraists sometimes mapped Argo to Noah’s Ark and interpreted constellation names through Hebrew roots. Those are confessional reinterpretations, most clearly materialized in Schiller, not evidence for an inherited Jewish star map.

**Kabbalistic claim:** Modern occult diagrams occasionally attach Argo/Carina to paths, tribes, Hebrew letters, or Noah. No standard mapping was found in the *Sefer Yetzirah*, classical Hekhalot literature, or the principal medieval Kabbalistic constellation traditions. Such charts should be labeled modern esoteric synthesis unless a manuscript witness is supplied.

## Christianized sky

Schiller’s **Arca Patriarchae Noachi** is the best-documented Christian replacement. It covered the whole classical Argo region—modern Carina, Puppis, and Vela, with Pyxis often included retrospectively.

This association was attractive because both Argo and Noah’s vessel were culture-saving primordial ships. Schiller’s exact mapping is a seventeenth-century invention, although comparisons between pagan ships and Noah’s Ark are older.

## Astrology, Hermeticism, and magic

### Ptolemy

**Finding:** Ptolemy’s *Tetrabiblos* assigns planetary “temperaments” to many constellation stars, but the surviving standard list does not provide a clearly isolated character for Carina, which did not yet exist independently. Later astrologers assign Argo’s bright stars combinations such as Saturn–Jupiter, but these are later extensions and often vary by author.

### Behenian stars

**Correction:** Canopus is **not** one of the canonical fifteen Behenian fixed stars in the medieval *De quindecim stellis* / Agrippan list. Modern websites sometimes call it “Behenian” or expand the category; that usage is historically inaccurate. Agrippa discusses the fifteen in *De occulta philosophia* 2.47 and 2.52. [Canonical Behenian overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behenian_fixed_star)

### Lunar mansions

Carina is far south of the ecliptic. Neither Arabic **manāzil al-qamar** nor Indian nakshatras geometrically “cross” it. Canopus can be used in paranatellonta, seasonal, horizon, or directional astrology, but it is not a lunar-mansion station.

### Alchemical and Hermetic readings

**Tradition, mostly early modern or modern:** Argo was interpreted as the vessel of initiation, the alchemical vessel, the soul’s ship, or an ark preserving wisdom through dissolution. Analogies to the *navis philosophorum* are symbolically coherent, but no classical catasterism explicitly calls Argo an alchemical vessel. Claims of an ancient continuous Hermetic Carina doctrine remain unverified.

## Iconography

### Farnese Atlas

The marble Farnese Atlas, Roman second century CE and generally understood as copying a Hellenistic celestial globe, preserves constellation reliefs. Argo is represented as a partial ship. Identification of individual relief stars with modern Carina members is difficult because the globe is small, restored, and not a positional catalogue.

### Aratean manuscripts

The ninth-century **Leiden Aratea**, made in the Carolingian world and preserving a late-antique iconographic tradition, depicts Argo as a stern or partial galley with mast, rigging, and oars. [Leiden Aratea facsimile and study](https://www.getty.edu/publications/resources/virtuallibrary/0892361425.pdf)

### Al-Sufi manuscripts

The *Book of the Fixed Stars* illustrates **al-Safīna** twice, mirrored for sky and globe views. Important witnesses include:

- a Baghdad copy dated 1125, Doha Museum of Islamic Art, descended through named exemplars;
- Bodleian Marsh 144, traditionally dated 1009–10 and attributed to al-Sufi’s son, but palaeographic redating to the late twelfth century has been argued;
- Paris, Bibliothèque de l’Arsenal MS 1036, third quarter of the thirteenth century, a Latin al-Sufi showing Argo as a stern-only galley.

The debated date of Marsh 144 is a major manuscript controversy: its copied colophon may preserve an exemplar’s date rather than the surviving codex’s production date. [Al-Sufi manuscript census](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/alsufi/alsufi_manuscripts.htm) [Warburg record for Arsenal MS 1036](https://iconographic.warburg.sas.ac.uk/object-wpc-wid-aill)

### Dürer, 1515

Dürer’s southern star map, prepared with Johannes Stabius and Conrad Heinfogel in Nuremberg, printed Argo in the learned Ptolemaic sky. Its viewpoint and inherited figure conventions connect globe imagery with flat print.

### Palazzo Besta, c. 1550

The “Creation of Heaven” ceiling at Palazzo Besta in Teglio depicts the rear part of Argo moving into cloud or fog, an unusually monumental architectural survival of the partial-ship motif.

### Bayer, 1603

Bayer’s *Uranometria* renders Argo with a richly ornamented hull, sail and nautical fittings. The stars are placed on anatomical details and lettered. Bayer’s plate became a major source for later atlases and for Schiller’s transformation.

### Schiller, 1627

Plate 40 turns the classical vessel into Noah’s Ark. Unlike a simple relabeling, Schiller supplied new Christian anatomical descriptions for the numbered stars.

### Cellarius, 1660/61

Cellarius’s *Harmonia Macrocosmica* presented entire Christian celestial hemispheres derived from Schiller alongside conventional astronomical schemes, giving the failed reform its most sumptuous reception.

### Hevelius, 1690

Hevelius’s Argo is viewed in the mirrored globe convention and shows the ship’s afterpart. Robur Carolinum is visible nearby, documenting a now-rejected royal constellation intruding into the old ship.

### Lacaille, 1756

His planisphere retains one Argo image. This matters iconographically: the famous “division” began as a catalogue solution, not a picture of three dismembered floating ship parts.

### Bode, 1801

Bode’s *Uranographia* provides one of the last great mythographic images of the entire Argo. The vessel is shown among Hydra, Centaurus, Canis Major, Columba, and the new southern figures, while dense plotted stars and nebulae overwhelm the older sparse anatomical scheme. [Bode regional plate description](https://www.georgeglazer.com/archives/maps/archive-celestial/bodecentaur.html)

### Jamieson and *Urania’s Mirror*

Jamieson’s 1822 atlas streamlined the ornate eighteenth-century image for education. The 1824/25 *Urania’s Mirror* cards closely copied Jamieson, punching holes of different sizes so the stars appeared luminous when held to light. [Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2013645050/)

### Coins, portals, calendars, and ceilings

**Finding:** Ships, Noah’s Ark, the Argonauts, and Canopus occur abundantly in ancient coins and monumental art, but secure identification of those images as the constellation Argo rather than a historical, divine, or heraldic ship requires accompanying stars or astronomical context.

No cathedral portal was found that can be securely labeled “Carina” as opposed to Noah’s Ark or the Ship of the Church. Palazzo Besta and manuscript/globe traditions provide stronger astronomical identifications than generic ship imagery.

## People, events, and works

### Navigators and explorers

Canopus has long been a southern directional star:

- Arab seafarers and desert travelers used **Suhayl** as a southern and seasonal marker.
- Indian observers used Agastya’s rising for calendrical and ritual purposes.
- Austronesian and Polynesian navigators gave Canopus multiple names and incorporated it into star paths. In some Polynesian reconstructions it forms one wingtip of a great bird with Sirius and Procyon.
- European blue-water navigators used Canopus for latitude and position once operating far enough south.

**Caution:** general statements that a particular explorer “steered by Canopus” require logbook evidence. Its inclusion in navigational tables proves availability, not use on every voyage.

### Observers and discoverers

- **Lacaille:** catalogued thousands of southern stars and recorded NGC 3372, NGC 2516, and IC 2602 during the Cape expedition.
- **John Herschel:** observed Eta Carinae’s sudden brightening and catalogued southern clusters and nebulae from Feldhausen; published the results in 1847.
- **James Dunlop:** surveyed southern clusters and nebulae from Parramatta in the 1820s, contributing many objects in and near Carina.
- **Benjamin Gould:** systematized southern constellation usage in Argentina.
- **Annie Jump Cannon and Henry Draper Catalogue staff:** supplied spectral classification and HD identifiers for large numbers of Carina stars.
- **Modern HST, Chandra, Spitzer, Herschel, ALMA, and JWST teams:** made the Carina complex a laboratory for massive-star formation, winds, shocks, feedback, and eruptive mass loss.

### Literature

Argo appears in:

- Homer, *Odyssey* 12
- Pindar, *Pythian* 4
- Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica*
- Euripides, *Medea*
- Catullus 64
- Virgil, *Eclogue* 4
- Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 7 and 15
- Valerius Flaccus, *Argonautica*
- Orphic *Argonautica*
- Dante, *Paradiso* 33.94–96, which compares the poet’s wonder with Neptune seeing the shadow of Argo—the voyage as a turning point in world history

The modern constellation name appears widely in science fiction and fantasy, but many uses refer to the nebula, star system, or a fictional location rather than the keel figure.

### Visual art

Major Argonaut subjects include:

- Greek vase paintings of Jason and the dragon
- Roman images of the Argonauts
- Renaissance and Baroque cycles of Jason and Medea
- Eugène Delacroix and Gustave Moreau treatments of Medea or the Argonauts
- astronomical prints by Bayer, Hevelius, Bode, Jamieson, and the makers of *Urania’s Mirror*

A ship in an Argonaut painting is not automatically intended as the constellation; astronomical intent requires stars, celestial placement, or a relevant title.

### Music

Jason and Medea generated operas and dramatic music, including Francesco Cavalli’s *Giasone* (1649), Luigi Cherubini’s *Médée* (1797), and later settings. These belong to the myth’s reception, not specifically to Carina’s astronomical subdivision.

### Ships, vehicles, and technical names

- **USS Carina (AK-74):** U.S. Navy cargo ship named for the constellation; damaged by a Japanese suicide boat at Okinawa in 1945. [Naval history summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Carina)
- **Toyota Carina:** automobile name generally said to derive from the constellation. The attribution is widely repeated but a contemporary Toyota naming memorandum was not located here; label it probable corporate tradition rather than fully documented etymology.
- Numerous yachts and merchant vessels have been named *Carina*, but Latin *carina* and the personal name Carina provide alternative sources; individual cases require registries.
- **Carina software/projects:** the name has been applied to astronomical surveys, computing projects, and instruments, usually through the constellation or nebula.
- There has been no major IAU or NASA spacecraft mission simply named *Carina* comparable to *Cassini* or *Kepler*. Proposed missions and payloads with CARINA acronyms should be treated individually.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Did Lacaille create Carina in 1752, 1755, 1756, or 1763?

All four dates appear because they refer to different stages:

- 1751–52: observations at the Cape
- 1755/1756: preparation and publication cycle of the Academy memoir and planisphere
- 1756: printed planisphere and tables
- 1763: posthumous *Coelum Australe Stelliferum*

The most precise formulation is: **Lacaille devised the catalogue subdivision from his 1751–52 Cape work; it was published in 1756 and fully disseminated in the posthumous 1763 catalogue.**

### 2. Did Lacaille draw three constellations?

No, not on the 1756 planisphere. He drew one Argo while subdividing catalogue entries. The visual dismemberment became more explicit in later atlas practice.

### 3. Is Pyxis a fourth part of Argo?

**Disputed popular formulation:** Pyxis is thematically the ship’s compass and occupies adjacent sky, but Lacaille introduced it as a new instrument constellation. It was not one of his three headings *in carina, in puppi, in velis*. Moreover, a compass is not part of the ancient Argo story and would be anachronistic for a Bronze Age heroic vessel.

### 4. Did the IAU “split Argo in 1930”?

Only partly. The practical split had been developing since Lacaille and was normalized by Herschel, Gould, and nineteenth-century catalogues. The IAU gave final official status to the descendants and exact borders. It did not invent the division ex nihilo in 1930.

### 5. Did Bayer’s letters survive unchanged?

No. The surviving discontinuous sequence reflects Argo, but Lacaille revised and systematized it. Assertions of perfectly untouched Bayer lettering oversimplify the catalogue history.

### 6. Why is the ship stern-first and incomplete?

- Aratus explicitly compares it with a ship backing into harbor.
- The pseudo-Eratosthenic tradition honors the first ship.
- Hyginus says the partial image spares shipwreck survivors fear.
- Later writers connect the missing section to the Symplegades.

These are variants, not pieces of one uncontested account.

### 7. Was Argo truly the first ship?

It is called the first in ancient poetry and catasterism. Other myths contain earlier vessels. “First” is therefore a mythic honorific and civilizational motif, not consistent mythical chronology or archaeological history.

### 8. Was Argo Babylonian, Egyptian, or Indian?

- Individual stars, especially Canopus, have pre-Greek identities.
- Egyptian, Mesopotamian, and Indian cultures had important ship, ark, or southern-star traditions.
- No surviving evidence proves that the entire Greek stellar ship was directly copied from Utnapishtim’s ark, Osiris’s bark, or an Indian *Argha*.
- Similarity is not a transmission chain.

### 9. Canopus’s etymology

The Menelaus-pilot story is ancient Greek etiological tradition. The city probably had a pre-Greek Egyptian name. *Kahi Nub*, “golden earth,” is an influential but disputed proposed etymology. The relationship among the Egyptian place, Greek hero, and star remains incompletely resolved.

### 10. Canopus was pilot of Argo

**Popular error:** Canopus was Menelaus’s pilot. Tiphys, followed by Ancaeus, piloted Argo in the principal Argonautic tradition. The star’s placement on Argo encouraged later conflation.

### 11. Miaplacidus means “placid waters”

Possible, attractive, and widely repeated—but not demonstrated. The earliest recoverable printed form is corrupt-looking, and no matching Arabic stellar phrase has been found.

### 12. Avior is ancient Latin

False. It is a twentieth-century navigational coinage or assignment. Latin-based etymologies are after-the-fact explanations unless the Nautical Almanac Office’s original naming papers are recovered.

### 13. Eta Carinae “went supernova in 1843”

False as an astronomical classification. It survived. “Supernova impostor” is the correct modern class, although nineteenth-century observers naturally spoke of a “new” or extraordinary star.

### 14. Eta Carinae is about to destroy Earth

A recurrent popular scare. It will eventually undergo core collapse, but the timing is unknown; “soon” in stellar evolution need not mean historically soon. At roughly 2.3 kpc, an ordinary supernova would not sterilize Earth. Speculation about a gamma-ray burst additionally depends on uncertain rotation, jet production, and orientation.

### 15. The Keyhole, Homunculus, and Carina Nebula are the same thing

They are nested or neighboring structures, not synonyms:

- Carina Nebula = NGC 3372 complex
- Keyhole = cloud feature within it
- Homunculus = Eta Carinae’s nineteenth-century ejecta

### 16. False Cross as an ancient constellation

No evidence was found for a classical False Cross. It is a practical southern asterism whose first printed naming remains to be established.

### 17. The Farnese Atlas proves exact ancient star coordinates

Disputed. It preserves constellation iconography and perhaps a recoverable ancient celestial geometry, but restorations, scale, sculptural conventions, and uncertain star marks preclude treating every point as a precise catalogue coordinate.

### 18. Bode’s “830 stars in 109 groups”

The **829/830** figure is plausibly related to older estimates of naked-eye stars in the enormous Argo. The **109 groups** could not be reconciled with institutional descriptions of Bode’s 1801 atlas, which give 102 figures, 20 copperplate maps, and 17,240 catalogued objects. Without the collection’s own metadata, this remains unverified.

### 19. “Carina” is ancient as a constellation

The word is ancient and Roman poets called Argo a *carina*. The independently catalogued and eventually bounded constellation **Carina** is an eighteenth-century construction. Confusing lexical antiquity with constellation autonomy backdates Lacaille’s innovation.

### 20. Al-Sufi gave the stars modern Arabic proper names

Al-Sufi recorded both translated Ptolemaic descriptions and indigenous names. Not every Arabic phrase in his Argo chapter is a proper name in the modern IAU sense, and multiple qualified Suhayls make one-to-one identifications hazardous.

## Open questions

1. What is the exact earliest surviving manuscript form and linguistic source behind Burritt’s *Maia placidus*?
2. Do HM Nautical Almanac Office archives preserve the individual who coined **Avior** and an explicit explanation?
3. When did **False Cross** and **Diamond Cross** first appear in print under those exact English names?
4. Which surviving recension most closely represents the original pseudo-Eratosthenic Argo chapter, and how much derives from later epitomators?
5. Can additional Arabic manuscript collation securely vocalize the more corrupt *Suhayl* compounds in al-Sufi’s Argo chapter?
6. What Egyptian lexeme lies behind Greek **Kanōbos/Kanōpos**, and was the star named after the port, the port etiologized through the pilot, or both?
7. Can the “830 stars in 109 groups” Bode-collection description be tied to a specific database schema, inventory, or exhibition record?
8. Which stars did each early edition of Bayer, Lacaille, and Gould intend for the most problematic lower-case Argo letters?
9. How much of the surviving Argo iconography derives from a single late-antique archetype rather than independent readings of Aratus?
10. Can a primary logbook substantiate particular popular claims about named explorers steering specifically by Canopus?
11. Are proposed Aboriginal records of Eta Carinae independent of post-contact astronomical prompting? Existing scholarship makes a plausible case but cannot eliminate every transmission uncertainty.
12. No traceable ancient Hebrew, classical Kabbalistic, or pharaonic Egyptian constellation exactly matching modern Carina was found. Future manuscript or archaeoastronomical discoveries could refine this negative finding, but modern equivalence tables should not presently be retrojected into antiquity.
