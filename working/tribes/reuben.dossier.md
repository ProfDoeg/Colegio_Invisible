# Reuben: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of material:

- **Documented text** — what an ancient or medieval source actually says.
- **Historical inference** — a reconstruction from texts, inscriptions, archaeology, or comparative history.
- **Rabbinic tradition** — midrash, Talmud, targum, or later Jewish interpretation.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — astrological, kabbalistic, Theosophical, or occult attribution.
- **Modern claim** — a recent assertion of descent or symbolism.
- **Legend** — narrative tradition for which independent historical evidence is absent.

Biblical translations below use the **NRSVUE** for continuous English quotation, with comparison to the Masoretic Hebrew, Septuagint, Vulgate, KJV, JPS, and major textual notes. The requested “full blessings” means the entire oracle addressed specifically to Reuben in Genesis 49:3–4 and Deuteronomy 33:6, not all the blessings to all twelve sons.

A caution about scope: “every other correspondence scheme” cannot literally be closed, because new private, internet, and denominational tables continually appear. The comparison below includes the historically influential schemes named in the brief and the principal families from which most later tables descend.

# The son in Genesis

## Birth and name

### Genesis 29:31–32

**Documented text.** Leah, not Rachel, is Reuben’s mother. Genesis calls Leah “unloved” or “hated,” Hebrew **שְׂנוּאָה, sənūʾāh**, probably indicating the less-favored wife rather than necessarily active hatred. YHWH “sees” her condition and opens her womb.

Leah names her son Reuben and says:

> “Because the LORD has looked on my affliction; surely now my husband will love me.”

The sentence supplies two sound-associations:

1. **רָאָה בְּעָנְיִי, rāʾāh bəʿonyî** — “he saw my affliction.”
2. **יֶאֱהָבַנִי, yeʾĕhāḇanî** — “he will love me.”

Neither is a straightforward morphological derivation of *Rəʾūḇēn*. The nearest transparent Hebrew segmentation is **rəʾū bēn**, “see, a son,” the explanation later used in rabbinic interpretation.

**Historical-linguistic dispute.** The name may preserve an older Northwest Semitic personal or group name whose original derivation was no longer understood. Genesis characteristically supplies theological and emotional wordplays rather than philological dictionaries. Proposed connections to “see,” “son,” “love,” Arabic or Aramaean tribal names remain conjectural.

### Genesis 30:14–16: the mandrakes

**Documented text.** During the wheat harvest Reuben finds **דּוּדָאִים, dûḏāʾîm**, usually translated “mandrakes,” and brings them to Leah. Rachel asks for them. Leah exchanges them for Jacob’s company that night; Leah subsequently conceives Issachar.

The plant is probably *Mandragora officinarum*, long associated with sexuality and fertility because of its scent, narcotic properties, and anthropomorphic root. The narrative never says that the fruit itself produces conception: Leah conceives after prayer or divine action in Genesis 30:17, while Rachel, who obtains the mandrakes, remains infertile until Genesis 30:22.

**Afterlife in symbolism.** This minor childhood act becomes Reuben’s dominant Jewish emblem. Numbers Rabbah assigns mandrakes to his banner and associates his red gemstone with fertility.

## The Bilhah episode

### Genesis 35:21–22

After Rachel’s death, Israel camps beyond Migdal-eder:

> “While Israel lived in that land, Reuben went and lay with Bilhah his father’s concubine, and Israel heard of it.”

**Documented text.** The Masoretic text stops abruptly after “Israel heard”; ancient scribal tradition marks the verse unusually. Genesis gives no immediate punishment. Genesis 49:4 and 1 Chronicles 5:1–2 provide its consequences: loss of preeminence and birthright.

**Possible political meaning — historical inference.** In ancient dynastic narratives, possessing a father’s or king’s concubine could signify a claim to household leadership or kingship; compare Absalom in 2 Samuel 16 and Adonijah’s request for Abishag in 1 Kings 2. Some scholars therefore read the episode as a memory, expressed genealogically, of Reuben attempting supremacy over Bilhah-associated groups, Dan and Naphtali.

**Rabbinic mitigation.** Mishnah and Talmud resist a crudely literal reading. Shabbat 55b says that anyone claiming Reuben literally sinned sexually is mistaken: by moving or disturbing Bilhah’s bed after Rachel’s death, he affronted Jacob, and Scripture imputed it as though he had slept with her. Other midrashim preserve both guilt and repentance.

**Targumic development.** Targum Pseudo-Jonathan and the Palestinian targumic tradition expand the episode into a lesson in repentance, often protecting Reuben from the most literal charge while retaining the loss of birthright, kingship, and priesthood.

## Joseph and the pit

### Genesis 37:18–30

**Documented text.** Reuben prevents Joseph’s murder:

> “Let us not take his life.… Shed no blood; throw him into this pit here in the wilderness, but lay no hand on him.”

The narrator explicitly supplies his intention: “that he might rescue him out of their hand and restore him to his father.” Reuben is absent when the others sell Joseph. He returns, finds the pit empty, tears his clothes, and cries, “The boy is gone, and I—where can I turn?”

This is his most favorable episode. Yet his rescue is indirect and unsuccessful. Judah later proposes the sale, and Genesis 42:22 preserves Reuben’s retrospective protest: “Did I not tell you not to sin against the boy?”

**Source-critical observation.** The apparent doubling—Reuben rescues Joseph from death, while Judah proposes sale rather than murder—has often been assigned to different narrative strands. In one reconstruction Reuben belongs to an “E” version in which Midianites remove Joseph; Judah belongs to a “J” version involving Ishmaelites. Modern source criticism is less unanimous about exact documentary allocation but continues to recognize seams in Genesis 37.

## Reuben in Egypt

### Genesis 42:22, 37

Reuben interprets the brothers’ distress as punishment for Joseph’s blood and says he had warned them. He offers his own two sons as security for Benjamin:

> “You may kill my two sons if I do not bring him back to you.”

Jacob refuses. The offer is extravagant and morally awkward—killing Jacob’s grandsons could not restore Benjamin—and contrasts with Judah’s later personal surety in Genesis 43:8–10.

### Genesis 46:8–9; Exodus 6:14; Numbers 26:5–9

Reuben enters Egypt with four sons:

- Hanoch
- Pallu
- Hezron
- Carmi

These become the four principal Reubenite clans. Through Pallu comes Eliab; through Eliab, Dathan and Abiram. Numbers 26 says Eliab also had Nemuel.

### Genesis 48–49

Reuben remains biologically firstborn, but Joseph receives the functional double portion through Ephraim and Manasseh. Genesis 49 explains the loss in moral terms; 1 Chronicles 5:1–2 later systematizes the transfer:

- birthright/double portion → Joseph;
- ruling preeminence → Judah;
- rabbinic tradition adds priesthood → Levi.

# The two blessings

## Jacob’s oracle: Genesis 49:3–4

### Full NRSVUE text

> “Reuben, you are my firstborn,  
> my might and the first fruits of my vigor,  
> excelling in rank and excelling in power.  
> Unstable as water, you shall no longer excel,  
> because you went up onto your father’s bed;  
> then you defiled it—you went up onto my couch!”

### Masoretic Hebrew

> רְאוּבֵן בְּכֹרִי אַתָּה  
> כֹּחִי וְרֵאשִׁית אוֹנִי  
> יֶתֶר שְׂאֵת וְיֶתֶר עָז׃  
> פַּחַז כַּמַּיִם אַל־תּוֹתַר  
> כִּי עָלִיתָ מִשְׁכְּבֵי אָבִיךָ  
> אָז חִלַּלְתָּ יְצוּעִי עָלָה׃

### Key words

- **בְּכֹרִי, bəḵōrî** — “my firstborn.”
- **כֹּחִי, kōḥî** — “my strength/might.”
- **רֵאשִׁית אוֹנִי, rēʾšît ʾônî** — “first of my vigor”; *ʾôn* can denote generative strength.
- **יֶתֶר שְׂאֵת, yeṯer śəʾēṯ** — “surpassing in dignity/rank,” literally an excess of elevation.
- **יֶתֶר עָז, yeṯer ʿāz** — “surpassing in strength.”
- **פַּחַז, paḥaz** — rare and disputed: recklessness, wantonness, turbulence, or boiling/effervescence.
- **כַּמַּיִם, kammayim** — “like water.”
- **אַל־תּוֹתַר, ʾal-tôṯar** — “do not/sYou shall not excel,” from *y-t-r*, to remain, exceed, or have superiority.
- **מִשְׁכְּבֵי אָבִיךָ, miškəḇê ʾāḇîḵā** — literally plural, “the beds of your father,” possibly a plural of abstraction.
- **יְצוּעִי עָלָה, yəṣūʿî ʿālāh** — difficult syntax: “he/you went up [on] my couch.” The sudden third-person verb may be an exclamation, scribal disturbance, or reference to Reuben.

### Ancient versions

**Septuagint:**

> “Roubēn, you are my firstborn, you my strength and the first of my children, hard to endure, hard and self-willed. You boiled forth like water; do not boil over…”

The Greek tradition reads **σκληρός** and **αὐθάδης**, emphasizing hardness and self-will, and renders the water phrase dynamically as overflowing or boiling.

**Vulgate:**

> *Ruben primogenitus meus, tu fortitudo mea et principium doloris mei; prior in donis, maior in imperio. Effusus es sicut aqua: non crescas…*

Gloss:

> “Reuben, my firstborn, you are my strength and the beginning of my sorrow; first in gifts, greater in command. You were poured out like water: may you not increase…”

Jerome’s **principium doloris mei**, “beginning of my sorrow,” differs strikingly from Hebrew “first of my vigor.” **Effusus sicut aqua**, “poured/rushing out like water,” is the phrase quoted—somewhat loosely—by Blavatsky.

**KJV:**

> “Unstable as water, thou shalt not excel.”

That celebrated wording is interpretive. “Unstable” is possible, but Hebrew *paḥaz* may mean turbulent, reckless, or boiling over rather than emotionally changeable.

### Interpretation

The oracle begins as a firstborn’s exaltation and reverses abruptly. Reuben possessed a claim to:

- seniority;
- dignity;
- strength;
- household leadership.

But because he “went up” to Bilhah’s bed, he would not retain superiority. The poem may explain historically why a tribe remembered as firstborn had become marginal.

**Rabbinic reading.** Palestinian targums distribute Reuben’s lost privileges: birthright to Joseph, kingship to Judah, priesthood to Levi. Reuben’s repentance nevertheless wins forgiveness and continued existence.

**Christian typology.** Christian commentators commonly make Reuben a type of natural privilege forfeited by lust or instability, while his attempt to save Joseph becomes incomplete moral courage: good intention without decisive action.

## Moses’ oracle: Deuteronomy 33:6

### Full NRSVUE text

> “May Reuben live and not die out,  
> even though his numbers are few.”

### Masoretic Hebrew

> יְחִי רְאוּבֵן וְאַל־יָמֹת  
> וִיהִי מְתָיו מִסְפָּר׃

### Key dispute

- **יְחִי … וְאַל־יָמֹת** — “May Reuben live and not die.”
- **וִיהִי מְתָיו מִסְפָּר, wîhî məṯāyw mispār** — literally something like “and may his men be a number.”

The problem is whether *mispār*, “number,” implies:

1. **few enough to count** — “may his men be few”;
2. **not few** — by reading or supplying a negative: “nor let his men be few”;
3. simply “may his men be numbered/accounted for.”

The Masoretic pointing contains no explicit **אַל, “not,”** in the second colon.

### Ancient versions

**Septuagint:**

> “Let Reuben live and not die, and let him be many in number.”

Greek **πολὺς ἐν ἀριθμῷ**, “many in number,” resolves the Hebrew positively.

**Vulgate:**

> *Vivat Ruben, et non moriatur, et sit parvus in numero.*

> “Let Reuben live and not die, and let him be small in number.”

Jerome resolves it in the opposite direction.

**KJV:**

> “Let Reuben live, and not die; and let not his men be few.”

The italicized English “not” has no direct counterpart in the Masoretic second colon; it harmonizes with the blessing’s positive form and roughly agrees with the Septuagint.

### Meaning

The blessing is extraordinarily short. It sounds like a plea that a diminished Reuben not disappear. This fits the Song of Deborah’s picture of indecision and the apparent disappearance of Reuben as an independent political unit by the ninth century BCE, although dating the poem and blessing remains disputed.

**Rabbinic tradition.** Deuteronomy Rabbah and related texts connect “live and not die” to forgiveness for Bilhah and to life in the world to come. Targum Onkelos expands it as eternal life and escape from the “second death.”

# History of the tribe

## Egypt and Exodus

### Genealogy

The tribal clans descend from Hanoch, Pallu, Hezron, and Carmi. No Reubenite is separately identified in the plague or departure narratives.

### Dathan, Abiram, and On

Numbers 16 identifies:

- Dathan son of Eliab;
- Abiram son of Eliab;
- On son of Peleth.

All are Reubenites. Dathan and Abiram join Korah’s challenge to Moses and Aaron. The ground swallows their households. On disappears after Numbers 16:1.

**Rabbinic legend.** Sanhedrin 109b credits On’s wife with saving him: she argues that whether Moses or Korah rules, On remains subordinate, then prevents conspirators from retrieving him. The Bible itself neither narrates his rescue nor explains his disappearance.

## Wilderness censuses

| Occasion | Count | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| Sinai census, fighting-age males | 46,500 | Numbers 1:20–21; 2:11 |
| Second census in Moab | 43,730 | Numbers 26:5–7 |
| Change | –2,770 | Arithmetic comparison |

The decline is approximately six percent. Numbers does not directly attribute it to Dathan and Abiram, though their destruction is recalled in the second-census genealogy.

## Camp arrangement

Reuben heads the southern division:

1. Reuben — 46,500;
2. Simeon — 59,300;
3. Gad — 45,650.

Total: **151,450**. They march second, after Judah’s eastern camp. Reuben’s southern position later becomes one basis for assigning a direction, color, element, or zodiacal quarter.

## Prince, spy, and offering

- **Prince/census chief:** Elizur son of Shedeur — Numbers 1:5; 2:10.
- **Spy:** Shammua son of Zaccur — Numbers 13:4. He is among the ten spies associated with the discouraging report.
- **Offering:** Elizur presents on the fourth day — Numbers 7:30–35:
  - silver plate, 130 shekels;
  - silver basin, 70 shekels;
  - both filled with fine flour and oil;
  - gold dish, 10 shekels, filled with incense;
  - young bull, ram, yearling lamb for burnt offering;
  - male goat for sin offering;
  - two oxen, five rams, five male goats, five yearling lambs for well-being offerings.

## The Transjordan request

### Numbers 32

Reuben and Gad possess “very great” cattle. Seeing the pastoral land of Jazer and Gilead, they request it rather than land west of Jordan. Moses accuses them of repeating the spies’ discouragement. They promise to build sheepfolds and fortified towns, leave their dependants, and fight at the front until the western tribes obtain their holdings.

The grant extends to Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh. The legal formulation—an explicit condition and its converse—became the rabbinic model of a valid conditional transaction, the **condition of the children of Gad and Reuben** in Mishnah Qiddushin 3:4.

### Reubenite rebuilt towns in Numbers 32:37–38

- Heshbon
- Elealeh
- Kiriathaim
- Nebo
- Baal-meon
- Sibmah

The text notes that Nebo and Baal-meon received changed names, presumably because the old names invoked non-Israelite deities, but the traditional place-names survived in the literary record.

## Conquest

### Participation west of Jordan

Joshua 1:12–18 and 4:12 say Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh crossed armed ahead of the Israelites. Joshua 22 releases them after they fulfill their obligation.

### Altar of Witness

Returning east, the Transjordanian tribes build a large altar by Jordan. Western Israel assumes apostasy and prepares for war. Phinehas leads an inquiry. The builders answer that the altar is not for sacrifice but a witness that eastern Israelites share in YHWH’s worship. War is avoided.

**Literary-historical significance.** The story voices anxiety about whether Jordan is a religious and ethnic frontier. It also legitimizes Transjordanian membership while subordinating sacrifice to the central sanctuary.

## Judges

### No Reubenite judge

No judge is identified as Reubenite. Reuben supplies neither a named deliverer nor a clearly narrated tribal battle in Judges.

### Song of Deborah

Judges 5:15–16 reproaches Reuben:

> “Among the clans of Reuben  
> there were great searchings of heart.  
> Why did you tarry among the sheepfolds,  
> to hear the piping for the flocks?  
> Among the clans of Reuben  
> there were great searchings of heart.”

Textual difficulties include **פְּלַגּוֹת, pəlaggôṯ**, divisions/clans, and **מִשְׁפְּתַיִם, mišpəṯayim**, sheepfolds, saddlebags, or hearths. The tribe deliberates but does not join the coalition.

**Historical inference.** If the Song preserves an early Iron Age political memory, Reuben was then a pastoral group capable of being summoned but weakly integrated into the western coalition. The Song mentions “Gilead” rather than Gad, further warning against projecting later tribal maps backward unchanged.

### Jephthah and Moabite-Ammonite border traditions

Judges 10–12 concerns Gilead, Gad, and Ammon, but does not call Jephthah a Reubenite. Claims that he was Reubenite are later harmonizations, not biblical genealogy.

## Saul

First Chronicles 5:10 says Reubenites fought the Hagrites “in the days of Saul,” occupied their tents, and expanded eastward. This notice is preserved only in Chronicles and cannot be independently synchronized with Saul’s campaigns in 1 Samuel.

## David

Reubenite participation includes:

- Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh contribute **120,000** armed troops to recognize David at Hebron—1 Chronicles 12:37; the number is collective and likely schematic.
- **Adina son of Shiza**, a Reubenite chief, leads thirty and belongs to David’s warrior list—1 Chronicles 11:42.
- **Eliezer son of Zichri** is tribal officer over Reuben—1 Chronicles 27:16.
- Hebronite Levites supervise royal and sacred business among Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh—1 Chronicles 26:32.

Second Samuel’s account of David’s accession does not single Reuben out.

## Solomon

No individual Reubenite is named in Solomon’s central administration. Solomon’s twelve districts do not straightforwardly reproduce the tribal map, and the old Reubenite territory was likely integrated into broader Transjordanian administration.

## Divided monarchy and Moab

### First Chronicles 5

The Chronicler locates Reubenites from Aroer to Nebo and Baal-meon, eastward toward the wilderness. Under a coalition of Reubenites, Gadites, and half-Manassites, 44,760 warriors defeat Hagrites and allies.

This is a postexilic literary source using older genealogical and territorial memories. Its historical notices may preserve genuine traditions but are not contemporary inscriptions.

### Mesha Stele, about 840 BCE

The Moabite king Mesha says:

- Omri had occupied the land of Madaba;
- Mesha took Nebo and Jahaz;
- “the man/men of Gad had dwelt in the land of Ataroth from of old.”

The inscription mentions **Gad**, Israel, Omri, and towns assigned by Joshua to Reuben, but never Reuben.

This absence is important but not mathematically conclusive. Likely explanations include:

1. Reuben had ceased to function independently and had been absorbed into Gad, Israel, or Moab.
2. Joshua’s allotment reflects a different or later administrative geography.
3. Reuben’s settlement was real but earlier than Mesha’s political horizon.
4. “Reuben” was a literary-genealogical label for communities that external sources called something else.

The stele does not prove that Reuben never existed; it does show that a ninth-century Moabite king did not recognize or need to name an operative Reubenite polity in territory biblical tradition gives Reuben. ## Assyrian deportation

### 732 BCE rather than 722 BCE

First Chronicles 5:6 says Tiglath-pileser deported **Beerah**, a Reubenite leader. Verse 26 names Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh and destinations:

- Halah;
- Habor;
- Hara;
- the river Gozan.

Tiglath-pileser III’s western campaigns culminated in annexations and deportations around **734–732 BCE**. Thus the specific Transjordanian removal is best associated with 732, not with Samaria’s fall in 722/720.

Second Kings 15:29 names Naphtali and Galilee in Tiglath-pileser’s deportations but does not name Reuben. Second Kings 17 describes the later fall of Samaria without tribal differentiation. Chronicles is the only text explicitly placing Reuben in the earlier deportation.

### Fate

No securely identifiable Reubenite political or ethnic community is documented afterward. Individuals or families may have:

- remained in Transjordan;
- moved into Judah;
- been incorporated into other Israelite groups;
- been deported into Assyria.

The language “lost tribe” describes a later memory problem, not evidence that every person vanished.

## Babylonian exile

Reuben was not a territorial tribe of the kingdom of Judah and is not specifically deported by Nebuchadnezzar. Later texts nevertheless restore Reuben symbolically within “all Israel.”

## Chronicles and genealogy

First Chronicles 5:1–10 names:

- Reuben
- Hanoch
- Pallu
- Hezron
- Carmi
- Joel
- Shemaiah
- Gog
- Shimei
- Micah
- Reaiah
- Baal
- Beerah
- Jeiel
- Zechariah
- Bela
- Azaz
- Shema

Some relationships are difficult: the genealogy is selective, telescoped, and possibly combines more than one line. “Baal” as a personal element need not imply devotion to the Canaanite storm-god; early Israelite names could use *baʿal*, “lord/master,” before its later polemical avoidance.

## Ezekiel’s restoration

Ezekiel 48 assigns Reuben the second transverse band south of Ephraim and north of Judah. This is an ideal, geometric Israel, not a record of Persian-period resettlement.

The new city has a **north gate named Reuben**, beside Judah and Levi. The arrangement restores Reuben’s name and inclusion despite historical disappearance.

## Revelation 7

Revelation 7:5 lists 12,000 from Reuben immediately after Judah. Reuben is **not omitted**. The conspicuously omitted tribe is **Dan**; Joseph appears, and Manasseh appears separately, while Ephraim is not named under his own name.

Possible explanations for Dan’s omission include later associations with idolatry, the sanctuary at Dan, or hostile eschatological legend. None is explicitly supplied by Revelation.

# Region: territory and towns

## Biblical boundaries

Joshua 13:15–23 assigns Reuben:

- from Aroer on the rim of the Arnon gorge;
- the city in the middle of the valley;
- the plateau or **Mishor** by Medeba;
- Heshbon and its dependent towns;
- east of the Dead Sea and lower Jordan;
- territory formerly ruled by Sihon the Amorite.

The Arnon/Wadi Mujib is the clearest southern frontier. The northern line toward Heshbon is much less stable; Gadite claims overlap. Jordan and the Dead Sea form the west. The eastern edge grades into steppe.

## Town list in Joshua 13

- Aroer
- Medeba
- Dibon
- Bamoth-baal
- Beth-baal-meon
- Jahaz/Jahzah
- Kedemoth
- Mephaath
- Kiriathaim
- Sibmah
- Zereth-shahar
- Beth-peor
- slopes of Pisgah
- Beth-jeshimoth
- Heshbon and associated plateau settlements

Bezer in the wilderness becomes a Levitical city and city of refuge in Reuben—Deuteronomy 4:43; Joshua 20:8; 21:36.

## Geography

The region is the high plateau of modern central Jordan north of Wadi Mujib and east of the Dead Sea:

- rolling agricultural upland;
- good pasture;
- intermittent wadis descending sharply west;
- routes linking Ammon, Moab, the Jordan crossings, and the King’s Highway;
- rainfall greater in the west, increasing aridity toward the eastern steppe.

Its exposure explains shifting Israelite, Gadite, Ammonite, and Moabite control.

# Symbols and emblems

## Water

Genesis 49 supplies no animal. Its characteristic object is turbulent or overflowing **water**. Later Christian, Masonic, occult, and popular heraldry consequently uses:

- waves;
- a stream;
- a water jar;
- Aquarius.

This is interpretive, not a biblical command for a standard.

## Mandrakes and the banner

Numbers Rabbah 2:7 states:

> “Reuben—his stone was ruby, the color of his flag was red, and mandrakes were depicted upon it.”

That is the clearest traditional assignment. The midrash is medieval in compilation, though it may preserve older motifs. [Numbers Rabbah 2:7](https://www.sefaria.org/Bamidbar_Rabbah.2.7?lang=bi&with=Translations)

Ibn Ezra on Numbers 2:2 reports an alternative “ancient” device: a **human form**, derived from the anthropomorphic mandrake. Judah had a lion, Ephraim an ox, Dan an eagle, together resembling Ezekiel’s four creatures. This explains why later cycles sometimes show Reuben as a man rather than a plant.

## Breastplate stone

### Biblical text

Exodus 28:17 and 39:10 make **אֹדֶם, ʾodem**, the first stone in the first row. Exodus does not explicitly say which name appeared on which stone. Josephus supplies birth order; later Jewish tradition assigns the first stone to firstborn Reuben.

### Comparison

| Tradition | First stone / Reuben | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Masoretic Hebrew | **ʾodem**, “red [stone]” | Color term, mineral uncertain |
| Septuagint | **sardion** | Sard/carnelian |
| Josephus, *Antiquities* III.7.5 | sardonyx/sard-like first stone in varying English renderings; names in birth order | Manuscript and translation terminology varies |
| Vulgate | **sardius** | Sard |
| Numbers Rabbah 2:7 | **odem**, treated as ruby/red gemstone | Reuben’s banner matches its red color |
| Bahya ben Asher | ruby/red stone with fertility associations | Links stone to mandrakes |
| Common Jewish lists | ruby | Traditional, not mineralogically certain |
| Modern gemology | sard or carnelian most plausible; garnet/red jasper possible | Ruby is difficult to engrave and may be anachronistically precise |

“Odem means ruby” is therefore too strong. It means a red material. Sard/carnelian best matches the ancient Greek and Latin witnesses and archaeological availability. [Gemological review](https://www.jewishbible.org/articles/gems-in-the-high-priests-breastplate/), [comparative LXX study](https://www.scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S0259-94222020000400043&script=sci_arttext)

## Chagall’s Reuben window

Marc Chagall’s twelve windows for the Hadassah-Hebrew University Medical Center synagogue were dedicated in Jerusalem in 1962. The series draws from Genesis 49, Deuteronomy 33, and the breastplate.

The Reuben window is dominated by rich red tones and fluid organic forms. Its imagery evokes firstborn vigor, water, mandrakes/fertility, and loss. Chagall did not merely reproduce a standardized heraldic badge; he transformed textual motifs into a modern symbolic field. Human beings are generally avoided in the cycle, consistent with synagogue sensitivity about figuration. [Hadassah account](https://www.hadassah.org.il/en/chagall_the_inspiration/)

## Synagogue cycles and Jewish art

Common Reuben devices include:

- red mandrakes;
- a mandrake-bearing human;
- water or waves;
- a red gemstone;
- the inscription **ראובן**;
- occasionally a rising sun, through folk analysis “see a son.”

No single device has universal halakhic authority.

## Israeli stamps, coins, medals, and civic use

Israel’s mid-century nation-building frequently mobilized “twelve tribes” imagery in stamps, synagogue art, medals, school graphics, and decorative arts. Chagall’s windows themselves were reproduced on stamps and commemorative objects.

A recurring distinction is necessary:

- A state-issued stamp or medal is a documented modern artwork.
- Its emblem may draw from midrash.
- It does not establish that ancient Reubenites historically carried that device.

No circulating Israeli coin identifies itself as an ancient Reubenite coin. Commercial “tribal coins” and medals are commemorative products.

Municipal arms in the former tribal regions sometimes employ biblical imagery, but there is no uniform official Israeli Reuben emblem. Much of Reuben’s biblical territory is in modern Jordan, making claims of direct municipal continuity especially misleading.

## Month, letter, sense, limb, and zodiac

The widely circulated Sefer Yetzirah commentarial table gives Reuben:

- month: **Tammuz**;
- letter: **ח, ḥet**;
- zodiac: Cancer;
- sense: sight;
- limb: right or left hand/foot in variant tables.

Recensions and commentaries differ in ordering and anatomy. The tribal-month table is often presented as though all details appear identically in the earliest text; they do not. It is safer to call it the **Sefer Yetzirah tradition**, especially as standardized in later commentaries, rather than a single uncontested original chart.

Tammuz suits later moral interpretation because Reuben “saw” the mandrakes, saw Joseph’s danger, and was implicated in the spies’ defective seeing. Those links are homiletical.

# The zodiac overlay

## Blavatsky first: the verified passage

The explicit Reuben assignment occurs in *The Secret Doctrine* (1888), volume I, printed page 651—not volume II.

Blavatsky introduces the table as something claimed by “Roman Catholic writers” and “mystics,” not as a correspondence she independently demonstrates. Her exact Reuben sentence is:

> “Man, or the Aquarius, is in the sphere of Reuben, who is declared as ‘unstable as water’…”

She continues that the Vulgate makes him “rushing like water,” then assigns other Genesis images to signs. The passage appears in H. P. Blavatsky, *The Secret Doctrine*, vol. I, p. 651. [Facsimile pages 601–676](https://www.theosociety.org/pasadena/sd-scan/sd1_601_676.pdf), [index confirming I.651](https://www.theosociety.org/pasadena/sd-index/SDIndex-JVM.pdf)

Her reasoning is:

1. sets of twelve are treated as zodiacal;
2. Jacob’s Genesis 49 oracles supply animals and objects resembling signs;
3. tribal banners allegedly repeated those symbols;
4. the twelve breastplate stones allegedly repeated the zodiac;
5. Reuben’s water simile suits the human water-bearer Aquarius.

Blavatsky is also skeptical and sarcastic about literalizing the scheme: she asks why, if the tribes and signs were indissolubly linked, ten zodiacal signs did not disappear with the “lost tribes.”

### Isis Unveiled finding

A dedicated search of the 1877 *Isis Unveiled*, volume II, finds **no explicit mention of Reuben** and no Reuben–Aquarius assignment. The book discusses the zodiac, patriarchs, Ezekiel’s wheel, Aquarius, and the claim that the twelve tribes lacked historical proof, but the specific identification appears in *The Secret Doctrine* I.651.

Relevant *Isis Unveiled* passages are volume II, pp. 456–472 in the original pagination, especially pp. 456–467 on the ten/twelve signs and patriarchal cycles. The statement sometimes attributed to *Isis Unveiled II* is therefore a conflation with *The Secret Doctrine*. [TUP *Isis Unveiled* II](https://www.theosociety.org/pasadena/isis/isis_unveiled_volume_2.pdf)

## Josephus and Philo

Josephus, *Antiquities* III.7.7, says the twelve stones on the high priest’s breastplate signify the months or zodiacal circle, and the twelve loaves signify the months. He does **not** there provide a tribe-by-sign table. Since he says tribal names were engraved according to birth order, later authors could align:

- Reuben = first stone;
- first stone = first month/sign;
- therefore Reuben = Aries.

But that inference is not Josephus’s explicit statement.

Philo repeatedly interprets twelve as the zodiac or cosmic order, especially in *On the Life of Moses* II and *Who Is the Heir?* He likewise does not furnish a securely explicit Reuben–Aquarius table. Appeals to “Josephus and Philo” often authenticate the general twelvefold symbolism, not the later individual assignments.

## Why the schemes conflict

They solve different puzzles:

- **Birth order:** Reuben is first, so Aries.
- **Jacob’s imagery:** water, so Aquarius.
- **Rabbinic calendar:** Reuben is Tammuz, so Cancer.
- **Breastplate order:** first stone may be aligned with Aries.
- **Camp order:** south or second marching division can generate different elemental schemes.
- **Four living creatures:** Reuben’s man/human banner suggests Aquarius.
- **Astronomical starting point:** some calendars begin with Nisan/Aries; others reorder tribes by camp, mothers, blessings, or canonical lists.

The famous animals create partial anchors:

- Judah → Leo, because lion;
- Zebulun → Pisces or a maritime sign, because ships;
- Issachar → Taurus in some schemes, because the burden-bearing ass/stable;
- Dan → Scorpio, because serpent/sting;
- Naphtali → Capricorn in Blavatsky, despite a hind not being a goat;
- Benjamin → wolf, for which no zodiac sign is exact;
- Reuben → Aquarius, because water;
- Joseph → Sagittarius, because bow.

Once these forced matches are made, remaining tribes must be fitted opportunistically. That is why no complete mapping follows naturally from Genesis 49.

## Breastplate as zodiac

Josephus supplies genuine ancient Jewish evidence for interpreting twelve stones cosmically. It does not prove:

- each stone had a zodiac glyph;
- tribal banners bore zodiac signs;
- Reuben was anciently Aquarius;
- Moses’ camp was an “amphictyonic zodiac.”

Those are later expansions.

## Judges, kings, prophets, and priests

- **Judges:** none securely identified.
- **Kings:** none. Reuben loses ruling preeminence to Judah.
- **Canonical prophets:** none is securely called Reubenite.
- **Aaronide or Levitical priests:** none can be Reubenite by patrilineal tribal definition. Levitical towns existed in Reuben, but their priests were Levites, not Reubenites.
- **Prophetic claim:** “Beeri,” Hosea’s father, is sometimes linked by later tradition to Beerah or to Reuben. The biblical text does not do so.
- **Villains:** Dathan and Abiram are paradigmatic rebels.
- **Ambivalent figures:** Shammua belongs to the condemned spies; Reuben himself both sins and attempts rescue.
- **Heroes:** Adina and the Hagrite-war fighters in Chronicles.

Statements that Reuben produced a particular judge, king, or major prophet should therefore be treated with suspicion unless a nonbiblical tradition is explicitly cited.

# Features and character

## Firstborn potential and forfeiture

Reuben embodies a recurring biblical theme: natural primacy does not guarantee election. The firstborn is displaced:

- Abel over Cain in divine regard;
- Isaac over Ishmael;
- Jacob over Esau;
- Joseph/Judah over Reuben;
- Ephraim over Manasseh;
- David over older brothers.

His story distributes the functions of primogeniture across Israel:

- Joseph receives the double portion;
- Judah receives leadership;
- Levi receives priesthood.

## Impetuosity

Genesis 49’s *paḥaz* and the Bilhah episode produce the traditional character of impulsiveness: energetic, passionate, but insufficiently governed.

Positive forms of the same trait appear in his quick intervention for Joseph and immediate self-reproach at the empty pit.

## Incomplete rescue

Jewish and Christian interpreters repeatedly notice that Reuben wanted to save Joseph but did not complete the act. Judah, despite proposing sale, later becomes Benjamin’s effective guarantor. Reuben can therefore represent conscience without practical courage.

## Repentance

Rabbinic tradition greatly enlarges Reuben’s penitence:

- he is absent during Joseph’s sale because he is fasting or occupied in sackcloth for the Bilhah offense;
- he is the first to model repentance;
- Hosea’s call to return is consequently linked to Reuben in some midrashic chains;
- Moses’ “let Reuben live” means his repentance was accepted.

Genesis itself does not say why Reuben was away and does not narrate this penitential practice.

## Mandrakes and filial care

By bringing the mandrakes to Leah, Reuben becomes a symbol of care for his humiliated mother. Midrash can interpret his offense involving Bilhah as misdirected zeal for Leah’s honor after Rachel’s death.

## Testament of Reuben

The *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, a Jewish work in Christian transmission and redaction, presents Reuben’s deathbed discourse as a warning against sexual desire. He confesses the Bilhah offense, describes prolonged repentance, and warns his descendants about women and fornication.

This is pseudepigraphal moral literature, not an autobiographical document from Jacob’s son. Its surviving form contains Christian interpolations or editorial layers whose extent remains debated.

## Bereshit Rabbah

Genesis Rabbah preserves multiple lines:

- “See the difference between my son and Esau,” elaborating **rəʾū bēn**;
- Reuben acts for his mother’s honor;
- his repentance precedes or models later repentance;
- he loses privilege yet remains within Israel.

Rabbinic literature does not offer only exoneration: some passages retain grave wrongdoing, while others reinterpret the act to defend patriarchal righteousness.

## Pseudo-Jonathan and Palestinian targums

These expand Genesis 49 into an institutional explanation. Reuben should have received:

- firstborn status;
- kingship;
- priesthood.

His sin redistributes them. Yet repentance protects him from annihilation and eschatological death.

## Messiah traditions

Reuben has no major independent messianic claimant.

- **Messiah ben David** belongs to Judah.
- **Messiah ben Joseph/Ephraim** belongs to Joseph.
- Reuben’s relevance is structural: his lost birthright explains why Joseph and Judah possess the two major messianic lines.
- Claims of a “Messiah ben Reuben” are marginal modern speculation, not a standard rabbinic doctrine.

## Christian typology

Recurring types include:

- privilege forfeited by sin;
- baptismal or emotional instability through water;
- fleshly lust;
- remorse without completed obedience;
- mercy, because the tribe is preserved in Deuteronomy 33 and Revelation 7;
- incomplete precursor to Christ’s rescue of the innocent Joseph-type.

These are theological interpretations, not historical claims.

## Islamic tradition

The Qur’an speaks collectively of Jacob’s descendants as **al-asbāṭ**, “the tribes,” and repeatedly names the twelve springs given to Moses—Qur’an 2:60 and 7:160. Reuben is not named in the Qur’an.

Later Islamic histories and *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* identify Jacob’s eldest son as **Rūbīl/Rūbīn**. In many retellings he opposes killing Joseph and recommends the well, paralleling Genesis. Details differ among al-Ṭabarī, al-Thaʿlabī, and later storytellers. These are exegetical expansions, not independent Iron Age evidence.

## Art and literature

Reuben appears in:

- medieval manuscript cycles of Jacob’s sons;
- cathedral and synagogue tribal cycles;
- mystery plays concerning Joseph;
- Thomas Mann’s *Joseph and His Brothers*;
- Chagall’s Hadassah windows;
- modern tribal banners, jewelry, and heraldry;
- occult diagrams built from Genesis 49.

Artists oscillate between water, mandrakes, a red jewel, and a human figure because the textual tradition never supplies one exclusive emblem.

# Afterlife and claimants

## The core historical conclusion

No modern population has demonstrated descent specifically from the ancient tribe of Reuben by an unbroken contemporary documentary, archaeological, linguistic, or genetic chain.

This does not disprove every line of biological descent. It means that “Reubenite” identity cannot presently be isolated and verified.

## Samaritans

Samaritan tradition maintains descent principally from Joseph’s tribes Ephraim and Manasseh, with Levites. It does not centrally claim Reuben. Genetic studies have found affinities between Samaritan paternal lines and other Levantine/Jewish populations, but genetics cannot assign an individual to biblical Reuben.

## Beta Israel

Traditional and modern accounts often associate Beta Israel with Dan, not Reuben. Other origin models involve ancient Jewish migration, local Ethiopian development, conversion, or mixed processes. Their recognized Jewish status does not constitute evidence of Reubenite descent.

## Bnei Menashe

The community’s name claims Manasseh, not Reuben. Oral traditions and modern religious identification have been important to migration and conversion policy, but linguistic and genetic evidence does not establish descent from a discrete Iron Age Manassite—or Reubenite—population.

## Pashtun claims

Pashtun-Israelite traditions variously assign tribes through ethnonyms and genealogies. Reuben is occasionally included in generalized “ten tribes” charts, but the best-known identifications focus on Joseph, Benjamin, or composite Israelite ancestry.

Evidence offered includes customs, names, and oral genealogy. Problems include late attestations, selective comparison, Islamic biblical influence, and absence of a specific ancient migration chain. Population genetics places Pashtuns primarily within South-Central Asian histories; it does not identify Reuben.

## Igbo claims

Some Igbo communities and movements claim Israelite ancestry, sometimes assigning clans to individual tribes. Reuben appears in certain modern tables, but these assignments are not uniform or traceable to ancient records.

Parallels in circumcision, food rules, naming, or social institutions are not unique to Judaism. Genetic evidence does not demonstrate descent from a particular biblical tribe. The claims remain significant as religious and ethnic self-understanding.

## Lemba

The Lemba preserve traditions of northern or Middle Eastern origin and Jewish-like practices. Genetic work has shown some West Asian paternal ancestry in parts of the population; the once-famous attempt to equate a “Cohen modal haplotype” with a priestly clan was overstated in popular retelling.

Even genuine West Asian ancestry would not identify Reuben. Lemba tradition is therefore relevant to broader Jewish-diaspora debates, not specific Reubenite proof.

## Bene Ephraim of Andhra Pradesh

Their declared identification is Ephraim, not Reuben. Their modern Jewish practice and narrative of lost-Israelite origin are documented, but an ancient tribal connection has not been established.

## British Israelism

British Israelism identifies Anglo-Saxon peoples with the lost northern tribes, especially Ephraim and Manasseh. Reuben is distributed among European nations differently by different writers—often France, Belgium, or another continental population.

The movement arose mainly in the nineteenth century, spread through writers such as John Wilson and Edward Hine, and later influenced Christian Identity. Its evidence relies on speculative etymology, heraldry, prophecy, and migration legends. Historical linguistics, archaeology, and genetics do not support the identifications.

## Mormon patriarchal lineages

In the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, a patriarchal blessing may declare lineage in a tribe of Israel, most frequently Ephraim or Manasseh but sometimes Reuben. This is a theological or adoptive covenantal identity, not ordinarily a claim derived from population genetics. Church explanations allow lineage to be literal, mixed, or adoptive in a spiritual sense.

## Other modern Reuben claimants

Small Hebrew-Israelite, African Israelite, evangelical, and internet communities sometimes identify particular peoples with Reuben through:

- national temperament;
- flags and heraldry;
- river or water geography;
- the syllable *Rub-/Ruben-/Reu-*;
- presumed migration routes;
- private revelation.

Such resemblance-based arguments are not adequate genealogical evidence.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. What does Reuben mean?

### Secure

Genesis associates the name with YHWH seeing Leah’s affliction and her hope of Jacob’s love.

### Disputed

- “See, a son” is linguistically transparent but first explicit as rabbinic wordplay.
- “He has seen my misery” does not exactly generate *Reuben*.
- “He will love me” depends on a second pun rather than the name’s consonants.
- Proposed extra-Israelite or Mesopotamian tribal cognates are possible but unproved.

## 2. Did the patriarch exist?

There is no extrabiblical contemporary evidence for Reuben son of Jacob. Historical-critical scholarship usually treats tribal patriarchs as eponymous ancestors—narrative embodiments of later peoples and relationships.

That is not proof that no ancestral individual lay behind the tradition. It means the biography is unrecoverable as ordinary history.

## 3. Did Reuben ever exist as a tribe?

### Evidence favoring some historical group

- Reuben appears in several independent-looking poetic, genealogical, territorial, and narrative contexts.
- The Song of Deborah treats it as a recognizable pastoral group.
- Deuteronomy 33 fears for its survival.
- Chronicles preserves Reubenite names and an Assyrian-deportation memory.

### Evidence for weakness or literary construction

- No extrabiblical inscription names Reuben.
- The Mesha Stele names Gad in supposed Reubenite territory.
- Territorial accounts overlap and conflict.
- Reuben produces no judge, king, prophet, or sustained polity.
- Biblical explanations of decline are schematic and moralizing.

A cautious reconstruction is that a Reuben-associated group probably existed early in Israel’s tribal memory but lost autonomy very early, perhaps being absorbed into Gad, Moab, or the broader kingdom.

## 4. Amphictyony

Twentieth-century scholarship, especially Martin Noth, compared Israel’s twelve tribes to Greek amphictyonies—sacred leagues centered on a sanctuary. Reuben’s inclusion despite weakness could then reflect ideal league membership.

The theory is no longer a consensus model:

- “twelve” is often schematic;
- biblical lists vary;
- Levi and Joseph/Ephraim/Manasseh are adjusted to retain twelve;
- no single central sanctuary is demonstrable for the whole supposed early league;
- Greek institutional parallels may be too remote.

## 5. Merneptah Stele

The Egyptian stele, conventionally dated about 1208 BCE, names **Israel** in Canaan and marks it as a people rather than a city-state. It does not name Reuben or any tribe.

It establishes that a population called Israel existed by the late thirteenth century BCE. It cannot prove the twelve-tribe system existed then.

## 6. Song of Deborah date

The Song is often regarded as one of the Bible’s earliest poems, sometimes placed near the twelfth or eleventh century BCE. Other scholars identify archaizing language, later editorial work, or a more extended compositional history.

Thus it is valuable evidence for early tribal geography but not a securely dated contemporary battle report.

## 7. Mesha and territorial contradiction

Joshua calls Nebo, Medeba, Dibon, and related towns Reubenite. Mesha presents the region as contested between Moab and Israel and calls Ataroth ancient Gadite territory.

This may reflect:

- different periods;
- fluid ethnicity;
- late schematic cartography;
- Reuben’s absorption into Gad;
- Moabite ideological claims.

The inscription weighs against treating Joshua’s map as a timeless cadastral record.

## 8. The Bilhah act

Options include:

- literal intercourse;
- attempted household coup;
- moving Bilhah’s bed;
- symbolic memory of tribal conflict;
- later moral explanation for Reuben’s decline.

Genesis 35 most naturally states intercourse. Rabbinic mitigation is explicit reinterpretation. Political symbolism can coexist with a literal narrative but remains reconstruction.

## 9. “Unstable as water”

“Unstable” is KJV tradition, not the only meaning of **paḥaz**. Alternatives:

- turbulent as water;
- reckless as water;
- boiling over;
- wanton;
- impetuous.

Blavatsky’s Aquarius depends on the English/Vulgate image, not on a necessary astrological sense in Hebrew.

## 10. Deuteronomy 33:6

The Hebrew may mean:

- “let his men be few”;
- “let his men not be few”;
- “let his men be numbered.”

The Septuagint chooses abundance; the Vulgate fewness; KJV supplies “not.” The disagreement is ancient and real.

## 11. Stone identity

**Odem** identifies color, not a modern mineral species.

- Traditional ruby is symbolically powerful.
- Sard/carnelian has the strongest ancient-version and archaeological case.
- Garnet and red jasper remain possible.
- Modern tables claiming scientific certainty exceed the evidence.

## 12. Banner historicity

Numbers 2 says tribes camped by standards and ancestral-house insignia. It does not describe their colors or pictures.

Red-with-mandrakes is midrashic, first securely documented in the medieval compilation of Numbers Rabbah. It should not be reported as an archaeologically attested wilderness flag.

## 13. Zodiac assignments

- Aquarius: blessing and human-banner logic.
- Cancer: month/Tammuz logic.
- Aries: birth-order logic.
- Pisces: generalized water logic in some popular tables.

There is no evidence that an Iron Age Reubenite community used any zodiac sign as its tribal badge.

## 14. Assyrian versus Babylonian disappearance

Reuben’s explicit deportation belongs to Tiglath-pileser III, probably 732 BCE. It should not be casually moved to:

- Samaria’s fall in 722/720;
- Jerusalem’s Babylonian fall in 587/586.

Later “ten tribes” language merges several Assyrian annexations and deportations into one collective memory.

## 15. Modern descent claims

Genetics cannot test “Reuben” without an authenticated ancient Reubenite reference population. Shared Near Eastern ancestry is not equivalent to tribal identification. Absence of proof, however, is not proof that no descendants survive biologically; it is a limit on historical identification.

# Open questions

1. Was Reuben originally located west of Jordan and only later remembered in Transjordan?
2. Does the Bilhah story encode conflict with Dan and Naphtali, the “sons of Bilhah”?
3. Does Deuteronomy 33 preserve a moment when Reuben was near extinction?
4. How much of Joshua 13 reflects Iron Age monarchy-era geography rather than early settlement?
5. Did Reuben merge primarily into Gad, Moab, Judah, or several populations?
6. Does Chronicles preserve an authentic Assyrian deportation list or a later synthesis?
7. Which recension first contains the complete Reuben–Tammuz–Cancer attribution in its presently circulated form?
8. What exact plate and ordering did Kircher intend, and which later tables silently altered it?
9. Did Rolleston derive her Reuben–Aquarius assignment directly from earlier Catholic/Kircherian tables, or from independent blessing exegesis?
10. Can archaeological survey distinguish Reubenite from Gadite or Moabite material culture? Presently, no accepted diagnostic “Reubenite pottery” exists.
11. Why did Revelation omit Dan but restore Reuben so prominently?
12. How old is the red-mandrake banner tradition behind Numbers Rabbah?
13. Did “Reuben” begin as a personal name, clan name, geographic name, or retrospective genealogical construction?
14. Are any named deportee communities in Assyrian records capable of being correlated with Chronicles’ Reubenites? No secure match has yet been demonstrated.
