# Joseph: Research Dossier

## Method and labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of material:

- **[Text]** What a biblical or other primary text actually says.
- **[Textual issue]** A variant, ambiguity, or disputed translation.
- **[Historical reconstruction]** A modern scholarly inference from texts, inscriptions, geography, and archaeology.
- **[Rabbinic tradition]** Midrashic, talmudic, targumic, or later Jewish interpretation.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** Astrological, kabbalistic, occult, or “gospel in the stars” systems.
- **[Modern claim/legend]** A community’s claimed descent or a later cultural identification, distinguished from demonstrable genealogy.

Dates before the monarchy are not independently recoverable from the biblical narratives. “Patriarchal period,” “Exodus,” and “conquest” below therefore sometimes describe the story’s chronological setting rather than securely dated events.

The Bible can count “twelve tribes” in more than one way. A genealogical list includes Levi and Joseph; a territorial list commonly omits landless Levi and replaces Joseph with Ephraim and Manasseh. Consequently “tribe of Joseph,” “house of Joseph,” Ephraim, and Manasseh overlap without being exact synonyms.

# The son in Genesis

## Birth and name

**[Text: Genesis 29–30]** Rachel is initially barren while Leah and the two maidservants bear children. Genesis 30:22–24 says that God “remembered Rachel,” opened her womb, and she bore Joseph:

> And she conceived, and bare a son; and said, God hath taken away my reproach:  
> And she called his name Joseph; and said, The LORD shall add to me another son.

The first saying uses אָסַף, *ʾāsap̄*, “remove/gather”; the naming formula uses יָסַף, *yāsap̄*, “add.” The latter is the explicit folk etymology: יֹסֵף יְהוָה לִי בֵּן אַחֵר, *yōsēp̄ YHWH lī bēn ʾaḥēr*, “May YHWH add to me another son.”

**[Textual issue]** The name is linguistically compatible with an abbreviated theophoric form, “May [God] add,” or “He adds.” Genesis supplies a literary explanation, not necessarily the historical origin of the personal name.

**[Text: Genesis 35:16–20]** Rachel’s wish is narratively fulfilled when Benjamin is born. She dies in childbirth near Ephrath/Bethlehem. Joseph is thus Rachel’s firstborn but not her only son.

## Joseph before the novella

- **Genesis 30:22–24:** birth and naming.
- **Genesis 33:1–7:** during the encounter with Esau, Rachel and Joseph approach Jacob last and bow.
- **Genesis 35:24:** Joseph appears in the formal list of Jacob’s sons.

## Genesis 37: coat, report, and dreams

Joseph is seventeen and shepherds with his brothers. He brings Jacob a “bad report” about the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah. Jacob loves him “more than all his children” because he is “the son of his old age” and makes him a כְּתֹנֶת פַּסִּים, *kətōnet passîm*.

**[Textual issue]** “Coat of many colours” is traditional English, but the Hebrew is obscure. Alternatives include “ornamented tunic,” “long robe with sleeves,” or a special ankle-and-wrist-length garment. The same expression describes Tamar’s royal dress in 2 Samuel 13:18–19; nothing in the Hebrew explicitly says “many colours.”

Joseph reports two dreams:

1. His brothers’ sheaves bow to his sheaf.
2. The sun, moon, and eleven stars bow to him.

Jacob rebukes him, interpreting the figures as himself, Joseph’s mother, and brothers, though Rachel is already dead in the received narrative. This is one of several seams used in source-critical study.

At Dothan the brothers plan to kill Joseph. Reuben proposes putting him in a pit, intending to rescue him. Judah later proposes sale rather than murder. A caravan is variously called Ishmaelite and Midianite; Joseph is sold for twenty silver pieces and taken to Egypt. The brothers stain the tunic with goat blood and allow Jacob to infer that a wild animal killed him.

**[Textual and compositional issue]** Genesis 37 alternates Reuben/Judah, Ishmaelites/Midianites, and “sold”/“drawn from the pit.” Many source critics infer intertwined versions. A literary approach instead treats the shifting agents as deliberate complexity. Neither solution is demonstrable by archaeology.

## Genesis 39: Potiphar

Potiphar, “an officer of Pharaoh, captain of the guard,” buys Joseph. Because YHWH is “with Joseph,” Potiphar makes him overseer. Potiphar’s wife repeatedly solicits him. Joseph refuses on grounds of loyalty to his master and sin against God. She seizes his garment; he leaves it and flees. She uses it as evidence in a false accusation, and Joseph is imprisoned.

**[Rabbinic tradition]** Joseph becomes *Yosef ha-Tzaddiq*, “Joseph the Righteous,” chiefly because of sexual self-control. Genesis Rabbah 87:5 offers several motives for his resistance, while other passages criticize his earlier vanity—he curls his hair and behaves like a pampered youth—so his virtue is represented as tested and acquired, not merely innate. Genesis Rabbah 85:2 says Potiphar’s wife foresaw astrologically that descendants would come from her house through Joseph but mistook whether they would come through herself or her daughter. [Genesis Rabbah materials](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.39.1?lang2=en&with=Bereshit+Rabbah)

## Genesis 40: prison dreams

Joseph prospers under the prison keeper. Pharaoh’s cupbearer and baker dream:

- The cupbearer’s vine has three branches: he will be restored in three days.
- The baker carries three baskets: he will be executed in three days.

Joseph insists that interpretations belong to God. Both predictions occur, but the restored cupbearer forgets him.

## Genesis 41: Pharaoh’s dreams and Joseph’s elevation

Two years later Pharaoh dreams of:

- seven good cows devoured by seven gaunt cows;
- seven full ears swallowed by seven thin, east-wind-blasted ears.

Joseph interprets both as seven abundant years followed by seven years of famine, saying the doubled dream shows that God has fixed the event. He recommends centralized storage of a fifth of the produce.

Pharaoh appoints him second in authority, gives him his signet, fine linen, and a gold chain, and has him ride in the second chariot. Joseph is thirty.

### Zaphenath-paneah and Asenath

Pharaoh calls him צָפְנַת פַּעְנֵחַ, *Ṣāp̄ənat Paʿnēaḥ*, and gives him Asenath, daughter of Potiphera priest of On.

**[Textual/philological issue]** The Egyptian name’s meaning remains disputed. Proposed Egyptian reconstructions include “the god speaks and he lives” and variants involving “revealer/sustainer of life.” The popular “revealer of secrets” reflects later Jewish interpretation more than secure Egyptology.

Asenath is an Egyptian name associated with the goddess Neith. On is Heliopolis, a major solar cult center.

**[Rabbinic legend]** To avoid making Joseph’s wife wholly foreign, Pseudo-Jonathan, Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer 38, and related traditions make Asenath the exposed daughter of Dinah and Shechem, adopted by Potiphera. This is absent from Genesis. [Jewish Encyclopedia, “Asenath”](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/1904-asenath)

Joseph’s sons are:

- **Manasseh**, מְנַשֶּׁה: “God has made me forget” my hardship and father’s house, linked to נשה, “forget.”
- **Ephraim**, אֶפְרָיִם: “God has made me fruitful” in affliction, linked to פרה, “be fruitful.”

## Genesis 42–45: famine and reconciliation

Jacob sends ten sons to Egypt but keeps Benjamin home. They bow before Joseph, fulfilling the dreams, but do not recognize him. Joseph accuses them of spying, imprisons them for three days, retains Simeon, and demands Benjamin. He secretly returns their silver.

On the second journey Judah guarantees Benjamin. Joseph is overcome on seeing him, but conducts a final test: his silver cup is planted in Benjamin’s sack. Judah offers himself as slave in Benjamin’s place, recounting Jacob’s attachment to Rachel’s remaining son. Joseph then reveals himself:

> I am Joseph; doth my father yet live?

He interprets the brothers’ crime providentially: “God did send me before you to preserve life” (45:5). The brothers’ responsibility is not erased, but the narrator places it within a larger deliverance.

## Genesis 46–47: settlement and famine policy

Jacob’s household descends to Egypt and settles in Goshen. Joseph presents selected brothers to Pharaoh. During the famine he gathers money, livestock, land, and finally labor for Pharaoh; Egyptian farmers must thereafter pay a fifth, while priestly land remains exempt.

**[Ethical and historical controversy]** Readers variously see Joseph as savior, prudent administrator, or architect of royal economic concentration. No Egyptian inscription identifies the biblical Joseph or independently confirms this national famine program.

## Genesis 48: Ephraim, Manasseh, and crossed hands

The dying Jacob declares:

> Thy two sons, Ephraim and Manasseh … are mine; as Reuben and Simeon, they shall be mine.

This grants Joseph the functional double portion later stated explicitly in 1 Chronicles 5:1–2. Joseph positions firstborn Manasseh beneath Jacob’s right hand, but Jacob crosses his arms, placing the right hand on younger Ephraim. Joseph objects. Jacob answers that Manasseh too will become great, “but truly his younger brother shall be greater than he.”

Jacob’s formula, “God make thee as Ephraim and as Manasseh,” became the traditional Jewish blessing for sons.

**[Textual observation]** Genesis 48 both unifies Joseph’s sons under one father and explains why later lists can count them separately. It also establishes Ephraim’s precedence, matching the later dominance of the central northern kingdom.

## Genesis 50: death, forgiveness, and bones

After Jacob’s death the brothers fear revenge. Joseph again says:

> Ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass … to save much people alive.

Joseph dies aged 110, is embalmed, and makes Israel swear to carry his bones from Egypt.

- **Exodus 13:19:** Moses takes the bones.
- **Joshua 24:32:** they are buried at Shechem in land Jacob had acquired.
- **Hebrews 11:22:** Joseph’s command concerning his bones is an act of faith.

The traditional Joseph’s Tomb stands near Nablus/ancient Shechem. The site has a long sacred history, but archaeology cannot identify its occupant.

# The two blessings

## Jacob’s blessing: Genesis 49:22–26

The following is the complete Joseph unit in the public-domain King James Version:

> **22** Joseph is a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough by a well; whose branches run over the wall:  
> **23** The archers have sorely grieved him, and shot at him, and hated him:  
> **24** But his bow abode in strength, and the arms of his hands were made strong by the hands of the mighty God of Jacob; (from thence is the shepherd, the stone of Israel:)  
> **25** Even by the God of thy father, who shall help thee; and by the Almighty, who shall bless thee with blessings of heaven above, blessings of the deep that lieth under, blessings of the breasts, and of the womb:  
> **26** The blessings of thy father have prevailed above the blessings of my progenitors unto the utmost bound of the everlasting hills: they shall be on the head of Joseph, and on the crown of the head of him that was separate from his brethren.

### Hebrew and disputed readings

- בֵּן פֹּרָת יוֹסֵף, *bēn pōrāt Yôsēp̄*: literally something like “a fruitful son is Joseph.” *Pōrāt* is traditionally related to פרה, “be fruitful.”
- בֵּן פֹּרָת עֲלֵי־עָיִן, “a fruitful son beside a spring/eye.”
- בָּנוֹת צָעֲדָה עֲלֵי־שׁוּר, *bānôt ṣāʿădāh ʿălê-šûr*: KJV “branches run over the wall.” Literally the subject appears to be “daughters.” Suggestions include branches, young women looking over a wall, or a plant’s offshoots.
- וַיְמָרֲרֻהוּ וָרֹבּוּ, “they embittered/attacked him.”
- בַּעֲלֵי חִצִּים, “masters of arrows,” hence archers.
- וַתֵּשֶׁב בְּאֵיתָן קַשְׁתּוֹ, “his bow remained in firmness/enduring strength.”
- וַיָּפֹזּוּ זְרֹעֵי יָדָיו is difficult. It can mean that his arms were agile, supple, strengthened, or “gilded.”
- מִידֵי אֲבִיר יַעֲקֹב, “by the hands of the Mighty One of Jacob.”
- מִשָּׁם רֹעֶה אֶבֶן יִשְׂרָאֵל is exceptionally obscure: “from there, the shepherd, the stone of Israel.” Some emend or repoint it; others understand God as Israel’s shepherd/rock.
- אֵת שַׁדַּי: “with Shaddai,” traditionally “Almighty.” A deliberate sound cluster follows: שָׁמַיִם *šāmayim*, תְּהוֹם *təhôm*, שָׁדַיִם *šādayim* (“breasts”), רָחַם *rāḥam* (“womb”).
- נְזִיר אֶחָיו, *nəzîr ʾeḥāw*: KJV “separate from his brethren.” It can also mean the “consecrated one” or “prince among” his brothers; the root underlies *Nazirite*, but Joseph is not portrayed as a technical Nazirite.

**[Historical-literary reading]** The poem celebrates fertility, military resilience, and divine abundance. Many scholars understand it as a tribal oracle describing the prosperous central highlands and political strength of Joseph/Ephraim, cast retrospectively as a patriarchal blessing.

## Moses’ blessing: Deuteronomy 33:13–17

Complete Joseph unit, KJV:

> **13** And of Joseph he said, Blessed of the LORD be his land, for the precious things of heaven, for the dew, and for the deep that coucheth beneath,  
> **14** And for the precious fruits brought forth by the sun, and for the precious things put forth by the moon,  
> **15** And for the chief things of the ancient mountains, and for the precious things of the lasting hills,  
> **16** And for the precious things of the earth and fulness thereof, and for the good will of him that dwelt in the bush: let the blessing come upon the head of Joseph, and upon the top of the head of him that was separated from his brethren.  
> **17** His glory is like the firstling of his bullock, and his horns are like the horns of unicorns: with them he shall push the people together to the ends of the earth: and they are the ten thousands of Ephraim, and they are the thousands of Manasseh.

### Hebrew and versions

- מִמֶּגֶד, *mimmeged*: repeated six times; “with the choice/excellent things,” “precious produce,” or “best gifts.”
- מִטָּל, *miṭṭāl*, “from dew.” Some ancient witnesses and modern translations construe the line slightly differently.
- תְּבוּאֹת שָׁמֶשׁ, “produce/yield of the sun.”
- גֶּרֶשׁ יְרָחִים, “the yield driven forth by the moons”; plural “moons” can signify months/seasons.
- שֹׁכְנִי סְנֶה, *šōkənî sənēh*: “the favor of the one who dwelt in the bush,” evoking Moses’ burning bush. The phrase is grammatically unusual.
- נְזִיר אֶחָיו repeats Genesis 49’s “one set apart/prince among his brothers.”
- בְּכוֹר שׁוֹרוֹ הָדָר לוֹ, *bəkôr šôrô hādār lô*: “a firstborn bull—majesty is his.” KJV’s “his glory is like” adds a comparison not explicit in the Hebrew.
- קַרְנֵי רְאֵם קַרְנָיו, *qarnê rəʾēm qarnāw*: “his horns are the horns of a *reʾēm*.”
- **[Textual issue]** *Reʾēm* is a powerful wild bovine, probably the aurochs or wild ox. Greek μονόκερως and Latin *unicornis* produced “unicorn(s)” in older English. It is not evidence for the later heraldic horse-like unicorn.
- עַמִּים יְנַגַּח, “he shall gore peoples.”
- רִבְבוֹת אֶפְרַיִם … אַלְפֵי מְנַשֶּׁה, “myriads of Ephraim … thousands of Manasseh,” explicitly giving Ephraim precedence.

### Septuagint and Vulgate

The Septuagint preserves the agricultural abundance and horn imagery, rendering *reʾēm* with μονόκερως, “one-horned.” The Vulgate has:

> *Quasi primogeniti tauri pulchritudo eius, cornua rinocerotis cornua illius; in ipsis ventilabit gentes usque ad terminos terrae.*

“His beauty is as the firstborn of a bull; his horns are horns of a rhinoceros; with them he shall toss the nations to earth’s ends.”

Thus “unicorn,” “rhinoceros,” and “wild ox” represent ancient and modern attempts to identify *reʾēm*, not three different Hebrew animals.

# History of the tribe

## Egypt, Exodus, and wilderness organization

### Genealogical entry

Genesis 46:20 and Numbers 26:28 identify Ephraim and Manasseh as Joseph’s Egyptian-born sons. Exodus does not narrate a separate “tribe of Joseph”; Exodus 1:5 notes that Joseph was already in Egypt, and 13:19 foregrounds his bones.

### First census: Numbers 1

**[Text]**

- Ephraim: **40,500** fighting-age males (Numbers 1:32–33).
- Manasseh: **32,200** (1:34–35).
- Combined Joseph: **72,700**.

The representatives are:

- Ephraim: Elishama son of Ammihud.
- Manasseh: Gamaliel son of Pedahzur.

**[Historical-critical caution]** Taken literally, Numbers’ totals imply a migrating population of several million, for which Sinai archaeology provides no corroboration. Proposed explanations include late administrative idealization, military units/clans rather than individual thousands, or theological numerology. None commands consensus.

### Camp and standard: Numbers 2

The western division is headed by Ephraim:

1. Ephraim—40,500.
2. Manasseh—32,200.
3. Benjamin—35,400.

Total: **108,100**. It marches third, after Judah’s eastern and Reuben’s southern divisions. [Numbers 2](https://www.sefaria.org/Numbers.2?aliyot=0&lang=en)

### Princes’ offerings: Numbers 7

On successive days:

- Seventh day: Elishama son of Ammihud, prince of Ephraim (7:48–53).
- Eighth day: Gamaliel son of Pedahzur, prince of Manasseh (7:54–59).

Each gives the same standardized offering: a 130-shekel silver dish, 70-shekel silver bowl, both filled with fine flour and oil; a ten-shekel gold pan of incense; a bull, ram, yearling lamb, and male goat; and two oxen, five rams, five goats, and five yearling lambs for well-being offerings.

### Scouts and leadership

- Joshua/Hoshea son of Nun represents Ephraim (Numbers 13:8,16).
- Gaddi son of Susi represents “the tribe of Joseph, namely, Manasseh” (13:11).
- Joshua and Caleb alone give a favorable report.
- Kemuel son of Shiphtan represents Ephraim and Hanniel son of Ephod Manasseh in the allotment commission (Numbers 34:23–24).

### Second census: Numbers 26

- Manasseh: **52,700**, an increase of 20,500.
- Ephraim: **32,500**, a decrease of 8,000.
- Combined: **85,200**.

The text identifies Manassite clans through Machir and Gilead and preserves the daughters of Zelophehad—Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, and Tirzah—whose inheritance case changes Israelite law (Numbers 27; 36). [Numbers 26](https://www.sefaria.org/Numbers.26.5-51)

## Transjordan: eastern Manasseh

Numbers 32 and Deuteronomy 3 assign lands conquered from Sihon and Og to Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh. Machir takes Gilead; Jair takes settlements later called Havvoth-jair; Nobah takes Kenath.

**[Textual-geographical issue]** “Half-tribe” is probably an administrative explanation for Manassite groups on both sides of the Jordan. In the old Song of Deborah, “Machir” and “Gilead” appear as active territorial groups rather than a neatly named “east-Manasseh.”

## Joshua and the allotments

### Ephraim: Joshua 16

Ephraim occupies the central hill country, broadly between Benjamin to the south and Manasseh to the north. The boundary runs from the Jordan near Jericho through the Bethel/Luz region, Ataroth, lower Beth-horon, Gezer, and the Mediterranean, though many boundary sites remain uncertain.

Ephraim fails to expel the Canaanites of Gezer, who are subjected to forced labor (Joshua 16:10).

### Manasseh: Joshua 17

Western Manasseh receives territory north of Ephraim extending toward Asher and Issachar. Important places include:

- Shechem;
- Tappuah;
- the Wadi Kanah boundary;
- Beth-shean;
- Ibleam;
- Dor;
- Endor;
- Taanach;
- Megiddo.

The daughters of Zelophehad receive land. Manasseh cannot initially dispossess Canaanites in the valley cities, especially where iron chariots dominate. Joshua tells the “house of Joseph” to clear forested hill country and expand.

**[Contested territory]** Joshua 17:11 assigns enclaves located inside Issachar and Asher to Manasseh. The description likely reflects overlapping claims and changing control rather than a surveyed modern border.

### Religious centers

- **Shechem:** covenant ceremonies in Joshua 8 and 24; Joseph’s burial.
- **Shiloh:** sanctuary and ark center in Ephraim (Joshua 18; 1 Samuel 1–4).
- **Bethel:** southern edge of Ephraim/Joseph territory; its tribal attribution varies.
- **Samaria:** founded as Omri’s capital in the ninth century BCE, in central hill country traditionally associated with Manasseh.
- **Mount Ebal and Gerizim:** cultic and covenant landscape around Shechem.

## Judges

### “House of Joseph” and Bethel

Judges 1:22–26 depicts the house of Joseph capturing Bethel after a local man reveals its entrance. Like Judah’s conquest account, this may preserve a separate tribal conquest tradition.

### Deborah and the oldest tribal constellation

Judges 5 praises participants from Ephraim and “Machir”:

> Out of Ephraim came down they whose root is in Amalek;  
> after thee, Benjamin …  
> out of Machir came down governors.

The Song names Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir, Zebulun, Issachar, Naphtali, Reuben, Gilead, Dan, and Asher, but not Judah, Simeon, Levi, or Joseph.

**[Historical reconstruction]** The Song is often—though not unanimously—regarded as among the Bible’s oldest poems. Its nonstandard tribal list is important evidence that the fixed twelve-tribe genealogy is later than looser regional coalitions. Machir may here be the principal Manassite group. [Cambridge discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/war-memory-and-national-identity-in-the-hebrew-bible/deborah-mother-of-a-voluntary-nation/2AB08F1422067F7DCDDDF433633F88E1)

### Gideon

Gideon is an Abiezrite of Ophrah, within Manasseh (Judges 6:15). He defeats Midian. Ephraim angrily complains that it was not summoned earlier; Gideon defuses the dispute by flattering Ephraim’s achievement (8:1–3).

Gideon’s son Abimelech, through a Shechemite mother, murders seventy brothers and becomes ruler at Shechem. His violent three-year reign ends when a woman drops a millstone on his head (Judges 9).

### Tola

Tola son of Puah lives at Shamir “in the hill country of Ephraim” (Judges 10:1–2), though his genealogy is Issacharite. Residence does not equal tribal descent.

### Jephthah: the question mark resolved

Jephthah is a **Gileadite**, son of Gilead (Judges 11:1), and is frequently placed within eastern Manasseh because Numbers and Joshua make Gilead son/territory of Machir-Manasseh. Judges itself, however, can use “Gilead” as a regional identity. Calling him unambiguously “of the tribe of Manasseh” goes beyond the immediate text.

After Jephthah’s victory, Ephraim confronts him. Gileadites seize Jordan fords and identify fleeing Ephraimites through their pronunciation of *shibboleth*; 42,000 Ephraimites are killed (Judges 12:1–6).

### Abdon

Abdon son of Hillel of Pirathon, in Ephraimite hill country, judges Israel (Judges 12:13–15). His tribal genealogy is not separately stated.

## Samuel, Saul, and the ark

Samuel’s family is called Ephraimite in 1 Samuel 1:1 by residence, but 1 Chronicles 6 gives him a Levitical/Kohathite genealogy. This is a real difference in presentation, often harmonized as “Levites living in Ephraim.”

Shiloh’s sanctuary is destroyed or abandoned after the Philistines capture the ark. Jeremiah 7:12–14 later treats Shiloh as a warning to Jerusalem. Archaeology at Shiloh shows destruction in the Iron I period, but cannot identify a particular biblical battle with certainty.

Saul is Benjaminite, but Mount Ephraim, Bethel, Gilgal, and the central highlands form much of his theater. Manassite warriors join David before Saul’s final battle (1 Chronicles 12:19–21).

## David and Solomon

David’s census lists Ephraim and half-Manasseh among his divisions (1 Chronicles 27:20–21). Chronicles reports large contingents:

- Ephraim: 20,800 “mighty men of valour” supporting David.
- Half-Manasseh: 18,000 designated to make David king (1 Chronicles 12:30–31).

Solomon’s administrative districts cut across older tribal borders. One officer, Ben-hur, governs Mount Ephraim; another governs Taanach, Megiddo, and Beth-shean; Baana governs Asher and Aloth; Geber administers Gilead (1 Kings 4). This suggests royal districts superseding or overlaying tribal organization.

1 Kings 9:15–17 says Pharaoh captured and burned Gezer, then gave it as dowry to Solomon’s Egyptian wife; Solomon rebuilt it.

## The divided monarchy: “Ephraim” becomes Israel

Jeroboam I, an Ephraimite from Zeredah and Solomon’s labor superintendent, leads the secession of the northern tribes (1 Kings 11–12). Shechem becomes his first capital; he establishes royal sanctuaries at Bethel and Dan.

Thereafter:

- “Israel” means the northern kingdom.
- “Ephraim,” its dominant central tribe, becomes a prophetic synecdoche for the kingdom, especially in Hosea and Isaiah.
- Samaria becomes the Omride capital around 880 BCE.
- Manasseh remains an important region and clan complex, but Ephraim supplies the kingdom’s common poetic name.

Hosea’s “Ephraim” is both beloved child and condemned idolater: joined to idols, politically unstable, and yet the object of divine compassion (Hosea 4–14). Isaiah 7 pairs “Ephraim” with Aram during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis.

## Assyrian conquests: 732 and 722/720 BCE

### 732 BCE

Tiglath-pileser III annexes Galilee and Gilead during the Syro-Ephraimite war. 2 Kings 15:29 lists Ijon, Abel-beth-maacah, Janoah, Kedesh, Hazor, Gilead, Galilee, and Naphtali. 1 Chronicles 5:26 specifically says Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh were carried to Halah, Habor, Hara, and the Gozan river.

**[Historical reconstruction]** Assyrian records and the biblical account jointly support major territorial loss and deportation, but do not permit every deportee to be assigned a genealogical tribe. A reconstruction includes Manassite territory among the affected regions. [Galil on Tiglath-pileser III](https://www.bsw.org/biblica/vol-81-2000/a-new-look-at-the-inscriptions-of-tiglath-pileser-iii/305/article-p520.html)

### 722/720 BCE

Shalmaneser V besieges Samaria; 2 Kings 17 credits Assyria with its capture. Sargon II subsequently claims to have captured Samaria and deported 27,290 or 27,280 inhabitants, installed an Assyrian governor, and resettled people there.

**[Text and inscription]** The Assyrian number concerns deportees from Samaria, not the disappearance of every northern Israelite. [Sargon’s annals](https://cojs.org/the_annals_of_sargon_ii-_c-_722_bce/)

**[Historical reconstruction]** Many inhabitants remained; others fled south to Judah; deportees were redistributed within Assyria. Archaeology and later texts therefore contradict the literal popular picture of ten entire tribes vanishing overnight. Tribal political structures dissolved, but northern Israelite populations continued locally and in diaspora.

## Judah, reform, and remnants

Chronicles repeatedly depicts northern remnants joining Judah:

- Asa attracts people from Ephraim and Manasseh (2 Chronicles 15:9).
- Hezekiah invites Ephraim and Manasseh to Passover; some mock, others come (30:1–12).
- Josiah’s reform reaches Manasseh, Ephraim, Simeon, and Naphtali (34:6,9).

**[Source-critical caution]** Chronicles, composed in the Persian or early Hellenistic period, advances a program of “all Israel” centered on Jerusalem. Its reports may preserve memory of northern migration and survival while also serving that theological agenda.

Joseph groups were not the principal subjects of the Babylonian deportation of 597/586 BCE, which affected Judah. Descendants of northern refugees may, however, have been incorporated into Judah long before it.

## Genealogies in Chronicles

- 1 Chronicles 5:1–2 explicitly transfers Reuben’s birthright to Joseph’s sons, while kingship belongs to Judah.
- 1 Chronicles 7:14–19 gives Manasseh’s genealogy, including Machir, Gilead, and Zelophehad’s daughters.
- 1 Chronicles 7:20–29 gives an unusual Ephraim genealogy, including sons Ezer and Elead killed in a livestock raid by men of Gath, Ephraim’s mourning, and the birth of Beriah. It culminates in Joshua son of Nun.

These genealogies do not fully agree with Numbers; they preserve distinct clan traditions.

## Ezekiel’s ideal land

Ezekiel 47:13 says Joseph receives two portions. Ezekiel 48 places:

- Manasseh in a horizontal strip south of Naphtali;
- Ephraim immediately south of Manasseh;
- Joseph’s city gate on the east, with Benjamin and Dan.

The plan is symbolic and never historically implemented. At the city gates Ephraim and Manasseh are recombined under “Joseph,” allowing Levi also to receive a gate. [Ezekiel 48](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+48&version=ERV%3BLEB%3BKJV%3BNIV%3BNKJV)

## Revelation 7

Revelation 7 seals 12,000 from each of twelve names. It includes:

- Manasseh;
- Joseph;
- Levi;

but omits:

- Dan;
- Ephraim under his own name.

The probable arithmetic is that “Joseph” stands for Ephraim while Manasseh remains separate. The text never explains Dan’s omission. Later Christian theories blame Danite idolatry or associate Dan with Antichrist, but these are interpretations, not statements in Revelation. A scribal-error theory has little manuscript support.

# Region

## Ephraimite highlands

The hill country of Ephraim lies in today’s central West Bank, approximately from the Bethel region north toward the Wadi Kanah and Shechem approaches. It is limestone hill country with:

- cultivable valleys and terraced slopes;
- vineyards and olives;
- springs;
- routes along the watershed ridge;
- access eastward toward the Jordan Valley and westward toward the coastal plain.

Important biblical sites include Bethel, Shiloh, Timnath-serah, Gezer on the western margin, Beth-horon, Shechem in the larger Joseph complex, and Mount Ephraim.

## Western Manasseh

Western Manasseh encompasses the Samarian hills around Shechem and extends toward the Jezreel Valley. Its claims include strategic valley cities—Megiddo, Taanach, Beth-shean, Ibleam, and Dor—that the text itself says were not initially controlled.

## Eastern Manasseh

Eastern Manasseh includes parts of:

- Gilead;
- Bashan;
- Argob;
- the Yarmuk and upper Jabbok regions.

Its limits fluctuate among Numbers, Deuteronomy, and Joshua. “Gilead” can designate both a person/clan and a region.

## Modern map and names

Approximate modern correlates:

- **Bethel:** usually identified with Beitin, north of Jerusalem.
- **Shiloh:** Khirbet Seilun/Tel Shiloh.
- **Shechem:** Tell Balata, beside modern Nablus.
- **Samaria:** Sebastia.
- **Gezer:** Tel Gezer.
- **Megiddo:** Tel Megiddo.
- **Taanach:** Ti‘innik/Tel Ta‘annek.
- **Beth-shean:** Tell el-Husn/Tel Beth Shean.
- **Joseph’s Tomb:** a traditional shrine east of central Nablus.

Modern Israeli names preserving Joseph-line terminology include the **Mateh Binyamin**, **Shomron**, and former **Ephraim** administrative/geographical usages; “Har Efraim” remains a common Hebrew geographical term. The name **Menashe** appears in the Menashe Regional Council, Ramot Menashe, and the biblical-landscape designation “Manasseh Hill Country.”

These modern labels commemorate biblical geography; they do not demonstrate uninterrupted tribal occupation.

# Archaeology and historical-critical reconstruction

## Was Joseph ever one tribe?

Three major models have been proposed:

1. **Traditional genealogical model:** a historical ancestor Joseph produced Ephraim and Manasseh.
2. **Segmentary-genealogy model:** Ephraim and Manasseh were social-territorial groups later represented as brothers and sons of Joseph to express alliance.
3. **Political-memory model:** “Joseph” retrospectively personified the central/northern highland population, with Genesis’ elevation of Joseph reflecting northern prestige.

The Bible itself preserves layers: early poetry uses Ephraim and Machir; conquest narratives use “house of Joseph”; genealogies create Joseph → Ephraim/Manasseh; prophets use Ephraim for an entire kingdom.

## Amphictyony

Martin Noth’s twentieth-century amphictyony theory compared the twelve tribes with Greek and Italian leagues organized around a sanctuary. The changing tribal lists were explained by a stable sacral number twelve with members entering and leaving.

The model was influential but is now widely regarded as insufficiently demonstrated. There is no independent evidence for an Israelite institution matching a Greek amphictyony, and the analogy can force diverse texts into one constitutional structure. The symbolic importance of twelve remains plausible without the institutional theory.

## Merneptah Stele

The Egyptian victory inscription usually dated about 1208 BCE contains the earliest widely accepted extrabiblical “Israel.” Its determinative marks Israel as a people rather than a city-state:

> Israel is laid waste; his seed is no more.

It proves that a population called Israel existed in Canaan by the late thirteenth century BCE. It does not name Joseph, Ephraim, Manasseh, Jacob, an Exodus, or twelve tribes. [BASOR study](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/1357179)

## Highland settlement

Surveys identify a marked rise in small agrarian settlements in the central highlands during Iron Age I, especially in areas later called Manasseh and Ephraim. Proposed explanations include:

- pastoral groups becoming sedentary;
- internal Canaanite social transformation;
- migrants joining local populations;
- some combination of these.

The evidence fits Israelite ethnogenesis in the highlands better than a single sweeping military conquest. It does not allow archaeologists to label an individual village “Ephraimite” by pottery alone. [Israel Exploration Society, Finkelstein](https://www.israelexplorationsociety.com/product-page/archaeology-of-the-israelite-settlement)

## Mesha Stele

The ninth-century BCE Moabite inscription of King Mesha names Israel and Omri and describes struggles over Transjordanian towns. It does not identify Joseph as a tribe. It independently confirms the northern kingdom’s regional power and Omride control east of the Jordan, relevant to territory biblically associated with Gad, Reuben, and Manasseh.

## Song of Deborah

The absence of Judah and presence of Machir and Gilead are significant. The poem suggests a coalition whose members and names differ from later canonical twelve-tribe lists. It is among the strongest textual arguments that Israel’s tribal map evolved.

# Symbols and emblems

## Fruitful plant

Genesis 49’s *bēn pōrāt* produces the vine/bough/tree beside a spring. It expresses Joseph’s fertility and is reinforced by Ephraim’s name. Modern art commonly depicts grapes, branches, water, or sheaves.

## Bow and arrows

The archers attack Joseph, but his bow remains firm. In narrative interpretation the hostile archers are his brothers, Potiphar’s wife, or political enemies; in tribal interpretation they symbolize military endurance. Blavatsky uses the bow to make Joseph Sagittarius.

## Bull and wild ox

Deuteronomy 33 gives Joseph the “firstborn bull” and *reʾēm* horns. The two horns become Ephraim’s “myriads” and Manasseh’s “thousands.” Later iconography commonly assigns:

- Ephraim: bull/ox.
- Manasseh: wild ox, buffalo, rhinoceros, or unicorn.

## Breastplate stone

Exodus 28:17–21 and 39:10–14 list twelve stones but do **not** say which tribe belongs to which stone. The assignment requires an ordering rule.

### Masoretic list

1. *ʾodem*
2. *piṭdah*
3. *bāreqet*
4. *nōp̄ek*
5. *sappîr*
6. *yāhalōm*
7. *lešem*
8. *šəbô*
9. *ʾaḥlāmāh*
10. *taršîš*
11. *šōham*
12. *yāšəpēh*

If the names follow Genesis birth order, Joseph is eleventh and receives *šōham*.

### Comparative assignment

| Authority | Eleventh stone / Joseph result | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Masoretic Hebrew + birth order | *šōham*, conventionally onyx | Exodus never explicitly states birth order for the breastpiece |
| Vulgate | *onychinos*, onyx | Follows an ancient mineral vocabulary not modern mineralogy |
| Josephus, *Antiquities* III.7.5 | Eleventh is onyx; names in birth order | His sequence differs from both MT and LXX elsewhere |
| Septuagint | Fourth row chrysolite, beryl, onyx; Joseph may fall on beryl if strict birth order is imposed | Greek order differs; translations and manuscripts vary |
| Numbers Rabbah 2:7 | Joseph = *šōham* | Late-antique/medieval exegetical tradition, not Exodus itself |
| Modern gemology | Identification ranges through onyx, sardonyx, beryl, malachite, or other stones | Ancient color/appearance terms do not map securely onto mineral species |

Josephus explicitly says the names were engraved in birth order, making Joseph the eleventh. His stone list is sardonyx, topaz, emerald; carbuncle, jasper, sapphire; ligure, amethyst, agate; chrysolite, onyx, beryl. [Josephus, *Antiquities* III](https://earlyjewishwritings.com/text/josephus/ant3.html)

The Vulgate has *chrysolitus, onychinus et berillus* in its last row. [Vulgate Exodus 28](https://vulgate.org/ot/exodus_28.htm)

A modern “Joseph = onyx” statement is therefore a defensible traditional assignment, not a scientifically certain translation.

## Banner traditions

### Numbers Rabbah 2:7

**[Rabbinic tradition]** Each banner’s color corresponds to its breastplate stone. Joseph’s *šōham* produces an extremely black banner bearing Egypt because Ephraim and Manasseh were born there. Subsidiary devices are a bull for Ephraim and *reʾēm* for Manasseh. [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Numbers.2.2?lang2=en&with=Bamidbar+Rabbah)

The English “unicorn” comes from older translation tradition; “wild ox” better represents the Hebrew zoological term.

### Pseudo-Jonathan

**[Targumic tradition]** Pseudo-Jonathan on Numbers 2:18 describes the western camp’s standard as three-colored silk corresponding to the stones of Ephraim, Manasseh, and Benjamin, bearing their names, a young man, and the verse “the cloud of YHWH was over them by day.” This is not identical to Numbers Rabbah’s black Joseph banner. The traditions should not be conflated. [Targum text portal](https://www.targum.info/pj/psjon.htm)

## Chagall’s Hadassah window

Marc Chagall’s twelve synagogue windows were installed at Hadassah–Ein Kerem and dedicated in 1962. His Joseph window uses:

- golden wheat recalling Pharaoh’s dreams;
- the fruitful-vine blessing;
- dream imagery;
- blood-stained incomplete hands interpreted in relation to the Holocaust;
- a shofar signifying awakening and redemption.

Chagall’s cycle is an artistic synthesis, not a reproduction of Numbers Rabbah. [Hadassah’s Joseph-window guide](https://www.hadassah.org.il/en/joseph/)

## Israeli stamps, medals, and modern iconography

- Israel issued a **Twelve Tribes** stamp series beginning in 1955; the Joseph stamp was dark green and used a modernized tribal device. [Stamp record](https://stampdata.com/stamps.php?fdate=11%2F8%2F1955&fissuer=432)
- Keren Kayemeth LeIsrael had a Twelve Tribes label/stamp series dated 1950. [KKL archive](https://www.kkl.org.il/stamps-search/?itemid=%7B721118C3-557B-473A-B9EF-8091B3BD14B1%7D)
- The Israel Government Coins and Medals Corporation issued Salvador Dalí’s Twelve Tribes designs as state medals in the 1990s. Dalí’s Joseph is a unicorn/cornucopia derived from Deuteronomy 33. [Israel Mint](https://en.israelmint.com/default/35228455.html)

These products helped standardize the bull, horn, crops, bow, and vine as modern Joseph emblems, despite the underlying plurality of textual traditions.

# The zodiac overlay

## Blavatsky first: exact text

### *The Secret Doctrine* (1888), volume I, page 649

Blavatsky writes:

> “The Old Testament is full of allusions to the twelve zodiacal signs, and the whole scheme is built upon it—heroes, personages, and events. Thus in the dream of Joseph, who saw eleven ‘stars’ bowing to the twelfth, which was his ‘star,’ the zodiac is meant.”

She continues that the twelve sons of Jacob are another reference to the zodiac and invokes Villalpando and Philo. [Facsimile, pp. 649–651](https://www.theosociety.org/pasadena/sd-scan/sd1_601_676.pdf)

### *The Secret Doctrine*, volume I, page 651

The Joseph assignment is explicit:

> “Saggitarius in Joseph, because ‘his bow abode in strength.’”

The original prints “Saggitarius,” a misspelling of Sagittarius. This is not merely an inference from an index; it appears in Blavatsky’s table-like prose.

On the same page she gives the scheme:

- Reuben—Aquarius.
- Simeon and Levi—Gemini.
- Judah—Leo.
- Zebulun—Pisces.
- Issachar—Taurus.
- Dan—Virgo-Scorpio.
- Naphtali—Capricorn.
- Benjamin—Cancer.
- Asher—Libra.
- Joseph—Sagittarius.
- Dinah—Virgo separated from Scorpio.

### Ephraim in Blavatsky

On pp. 652–653 she reports, critically, a Christian-symbolist claim that Pisces was Ephraim’s sign and hence the Messiah’s constellation. She does not make Ephraim or Manasseh subsidiary parts of Joseph’s Sagittarius in a systematic table. Therefore:

- **Explicit Blavatsky assignment:** Joseph = Sagittarius.
- **Reported Christian claim:** Ephraim = Pisces.
- **No explicit independent Blavatsky sign found for Manasseh.**

### *Isis Unveiled* (1877)

No parallel passage has been located in which Blavatsky explicitly assigns Joseph to Sagittarius. *Isis Unveiled* does argue that twelvefold biblical structures and the high-priestly breastplate reflect older cosmic symbolism. It also treats Joseph as outwardly Egyptianized through marriage to a priest’s daughter. In volume I, p. 556, she writes that Joseph:

> “had, in appearance at least, changed his religion, married the daughter of a priest of the ‘idolatrous’ nation, and become himself an Egyptian.”

That is an esoteric-historical assertion, not evidence that Genesis says Joseph converted. [*Isis Unveiled*, I, chapter 14](https://www.theosociety.org/pasadena/isis/iu1-14.htm)

The requested “Joseph = Sagittarius” quotation belongs to *The Secret Doctrine*, not demonstrably to *Isis Unveiled*. Absence of a matching explicit assignment in *Isis* is a finding.

## Josephus and Philo

Josephus says the twelve breastplate stones symbolize the months or zodiacal circle (*Antiquities* III.7.7), but he does not give a tribe-by-sign table. He also says the tribal names were engraved by birth order. Any complete Josephus correspondence table is therefore reconstructed by later writers, not supplied by Josephus.

Philo repeatedly makes twelve cosmic:

- twelve months;
- zodiac signs;
- tribes;
- showbread loaves;
- breastplate stones.

In *On Flight and Finding* (*De Fuga*) 110–112, he links the high-priestly arrangement with the zodiacal circle. He does not securely assign Joseph to Sagittarius.

## Comparison of major schemes

| Source/system | Date | Joseph/Ephraim/Manasseh assignment | Governing logic |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Josephus | c. 93–94 CE | No individual signs | Twelve stones = months/zodiac; names in birth order |
| Philo | 1st c. CE | No individual signs | Philosophical symbolism of twelve |
| Yalqut/camp-order tradition | medieval compilation from earlier materials | Ephraim Libra, Manasseh Scorpio, Benjamin Sagittarius | Zodiac placed sequentially around Numbers 2 camps |
| *Sefer Yetzirah* itself | late antique, recensions vary | No tribes in the core surviving text; later commentaries add them | Twelve letters = signs, months, bodily faculties |
| Later Raavad/Gra-influenced chart | medieval/early modern reception | Commonly Ephraim–Tishri–Libra; Manasseh–Marḥeshvan–Scorpio | Camp/calendar sequence |
| Blavatsky | 1888 | Joseph Sagittarius; reports Ephraim Pisces claim; no separate Manasseh | Genesis 49 bow |
| Rolleston, *Mazzaroth* | 1862 | Joseph Taurus | Deuteronomy’s bull |
| Golden Dawn/Mathers | late 19th c. | Ephraim Taurus; Manasseh Gemini | Camp arrangement beginning with Judah/Leo and assigning adjacent signs |
| Crowley, *777* | 1909 tables, revised later | Ephraim Taurus; Manasseh Gemini | Inherited Golden Dawn correspondences |
| Some later Golden Dawn-derived charts | 20th c. | Ephraim Aquarius, Manasseh Capricorn | Alternative reordering found in secondary occult tables |
| Bullinger, *Witness of the Stars* | 1893 | His introductory tribal order makes Joseph’s descendants part of a camp/zodiac system; Asher, not Joseph, receives Sagittarius in one table | Christian “primeval gospel” theory |
| Seiss, *Gospel in the Stars* | 1882 | No stable complete Joseph tribal table central to the work | Constellations interpreted as pre-Christian gospel |
| Manly P. Hall | 1920s onward | Repeats broad zodiac/breastplate parallel; no consistently documented original Joseph table | Comparative occult symbolism |
| Modern popular Jewish astrology | 20th–21st c. | Often Ephraim Libra/Tishri, Manasseh Scorpio/Cheshvan | Later *Sefer Yetzirah* commentary plus camp order |
| Modern Christian “tribes and signs” charts | 20th–21st c. | Joseph varies among Sagittarius, Taurus, or Pisces | Choice of bow, bull, or fruitful/fish symbolism |

### Why the schemes conflict

1. There are thirteen available names when Levi, Joseph, Ephraim, and Manasseh are all counted.
2. Birth order, camp order, blessing order, geographical order, and Revelation order differ.
3. Joseph has several strong symbols: bow, bull, horns, fertility, water, vine.
4. Dan can be serpent/Scorpio or eagle, the “higher” fixed sign paired with Scorpio.
5. Judah’s lion makes Leo unusually stable, but fixing Judah at Leo changes every sequential assignment.
6. Ephraim’s bull makes Taurus attractive; Manasseh then becomes Gemini in Golden Dawn systems because the “half-tribe” is rhetorically doubled.
7. Sagittarius fits Joseph’s bow but conflicts with systems assigning Sagittarius sequentially to Benjamin or Asher.
8. Ancient authors established numerical analogies; later authors often mistook these for complete ancient tables.

### The emblem anchors

- Reuben—unstable water → Aquarius.
- Judah—lion → Leo.
- Zebulun—ships/sea → Pisces, Cancer, or occasionally Sagittarius through alternative sequences.
- Issachar—ass → Taurus or Cancer.
- Dan—serpent → Scorpio; eagle in four-standard systems.
- Naphtali—hind → Capricorn or Sagittarius.
- Joseph—bow → Sagittarius; bull → Taurus; fertility/waters → Pisces.
- Benjamin—wolf → Cancer in Blavatsky, Gemini/Sagittarius in other sequential systems.

## Kircher

Athanasius Kircher’s enormous *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (Rome, 1652–1654) joins Egyptian, Hebrew, planetary, zodiacal, and hieroglyphic systems. Reproductions of his tables circulated widely among later Christian antiquarians. However, the commonly reproduced modern “Kircher tribes-signs table” is frequently detached from volume, folio, and column, and different derivative charts disagree.

A secure finding is that Kircher helped popularize the universal-correspondence framework. A unique “Kircher says Joseph = X” should not be asserted without specifying the exact table and edition. The available digitized materials did not yield a sufficiently legible primary table to settle that attribution. [Gallica digitization](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k1180769)

## Rolleston, Seiss, and Bullinger

Frances Rolleston explicitly assigns Joseph **Taurus**, citing “his glory like the firstling of his bullock.” Her work became a major source for later “gospel in the stars” literature. [Rolleston table](https://www.friendsofsabbath.org/Further_Research/Bible/mazzaroth/205.htm)

Joseph A. Seiss adopts Rolleston’s larger thesis that the constellations once encoded a primeval revelation of redemption, but his book principally organizes the gospel by signs rather than securely matching every tribe.

E. W. Bullinger popularizes the same Christian theory. He cites Joseph’s twelve-star dream as evidence of zodiacal knowledge, yet Sagittarius in his main exposition symbolizes the conquering Redeemer, and his tribal order does not simply reproduce Blavatsky’s Joseph-Sagittarius. [Bullinger](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/49018/49018-h/49018-h.html)

## Golden Dawn and Crowley

Crowley’s *777*, table V, column CXLI gives:

- Aries—Gad
- Taurus—Ephraim
- Gemini—Manasseh
- Cancer—Issachar
- Leo—Judah
- Virgo—Naphtali
- Libra—Asher
- Scorpio—Dan
- Sagittarius—Benjamin
- Capricorn—Zebulun
- Aquarius—Reuben
- Pisces—Simeon

Joseph is divided into his sons. The bull secures Ephraim to Taurus; Manasseh follows as Gemini. [*777* table](https://www.astrumargenteum.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/777-by-Aleister-Crowley.pdf)

This is an occult correspondence system inherited from Golden Dawn teaching, not an ancient Jewish consensus.

## Godfrey Higgins, Gerald Massey, Thomas Inman, and Hall

These comparative-mythological writers variously identify Israel’s twelvefold structures with the zodiac, often citing one another, Dupuis, Kircher, or earlier antiquarians. Their contribution is chiefly diffusion of the general thesis. A uniform Joseph assignment cannot be responsibly extracted from the entire group:

- Higgins argues for shared ancient astro-theological origins.
- Massey reads Hebrew and Christian imagery through Egyptian astronomical religion.
- Inman treats biblical symbols as comparative pagan/phallic emblems.
- Hall presents the breastplate, zodiac, tribes, and cosmic man as related symbolic orders.

Where modern charts attribute a specific Joseph sign to one of these writers without edition and page, the attribution should be treated as unverified.

Because “Joseph” divides into two tribes, figures are listed by explicit or probable affiliation.

## Joseph himself and immediate family

- Joseph.
- Rachel, his mother.
- Asenath, his wife.
- Manasseh and Ephraim, his sons.
- Machir son of Manasseh.
- Gilead son of Machir.
- Shuthelah, Becher, and Tahan, Ephraimite clan ancestors.
- Zelophehad and daughters Mahlah, Noah, Hoglah, Milcah, Tirzah.

## Ephraimites

- **Elishama son of Ammihud:** wilderness prince.

## Manassites and Gileadites

- **Gamaliel son of Pedahzur:** wilderness prince.
- **Gaddi son of Susi:** scout.
- **Hanniel son of Ephod:** allotment representative.
- **Moses’ Machirite commanders and settlers:** Machir, Jair, Nobah.
- **Gideon/Jerubbaal:** judge and military leader, Abiezrite of Manasseh.

- **Jehu son of Hanani:** a prophet associated with northern affairs; tribe unstated.
- **Micaiah son of Imlah:** northern prophet; tribe unstated.

## Northern kings and the danger of over-attribution

Only Jeroboam I is expressly called Ephraimite. Later kings—Baasha, Omri, Ahab, Jehu, and others—rule the kingdom frequently called Ephraim, but ruling “Ephraim” does not make each king an Ephraimite. Omri’s family origin is not stated; Jehu’s grandfather Nimshi does not establish a tribe.

No biblical priesthood belongs genealogically to Joseph: priests are Aaronide Levites. Northern sanctuaries nevertheless employed non-Levitical royal priests according to Kings.

# Features and character

## Biblical portrait

Joseph combines:

### Virtues

- administrative intelligence;
- dream interpretation attributed to God;
- sexual restraint;
- endurance under betrayal and false accusation;
- preservation of life;
- forgiveness;
- political and economic competence;
- loyalty to the ancestral promise through the command about his bones.

### Ambiguities and faults

- bringing a bad report;
- apparent youthful vanity or indiscretion in relating dreams;
- manipulating his brothers through detention, returned money, and the planted cup;
- participating in the enslavement and land concentration of famine-stricken Egyptians;
- deep assimilation into Egyptian elite life.

Genesis does not flatten these tensions. Joseph is both victim and powerful administrator, family reconciler and agent of Pharaoh.

## Rabbinic Joseph

### Joseph the righteous

Joseph’s resistance to Potiphar’s wife makes him the paradigmatic *tzaddiq*. In later mystical symbolism the sefirah Yesod, associated with sexual covenant and generative transmission, is frequently personified by Joseph.

### Measure for measure

Midrash relates Joseph’s later honors to his earlier acts:

- he refused improper touch, so royal ornaments are placed upon him;
- he fled sin, so he receives authority;
- he sustains others, so abundance flows through him.

### Criticism

Genesis Rabbah also portrays the young Joseph as vain, beautifying himself while his father mourned. Some traditions say he remained imprisoned longer because he relied twice on the cupbearer rather than solely on God.

### Pseudo-Jonathan

Pseudo-Jonathan amplifies Joseph’s piety, the brothers’ deliberations, Asenath’s Israelite ancestry, and the supernatural preservation and identification of his bones. These are interpretive expansions, not alternative manuscript readings of Genesis.

## Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs

The *Testament of Joseph*, in its surviving Christian-transmitted form, makes chastity, patience, fasting, and endurance the controlling virtues:

> “God loveth him who in a den of darkness fasteth with chastity, rather than him who in secret chambers liveth delicately without restraint.”

The work has Jewish foundations but Christian redactional problems; its final form cannot simply be cited as pre-Christian Jewish testimony. [Primary translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Ante-Nicene_Fathers/Volume_VIII/The_Testaments_of_the_Twelve_Patriarchs/The_Testament_of_Joseph_Concerning_Sobriety)

## Zohar

The Zohar calls Joseph righteous and connects him with covenantal sexual purity and Yesod. Zohar I, 189a–b develops the theme that the divine presence accompanies the righteous. Its Joseph is a channel through which blessing and fertility pass into the world.

## Messiah ben Joseph

**[Rabbinic-apocalyptic tradition]** Messiah son of Joseph/Ephraim is a warrior or preparatory messiah distinct from Messiah son of David. In Babylonian Talmud Sukkah 52a–b he is killed, prompting mourning and Messiah ben David’s request for life. Related materials connect him with the defeat of Israel’s enemies and restoration before final Davidic redemption.

The explicit figure first appears securely in late-antique rabbinic texts, though scholars debate earlier roots in postbiblical Joseph, Ephraim, Joshua, and suffering-messiah traditions. He is not named in the Hebrew Bible. [Study of the earliest texts](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/ryanszpiech/wp-content/uploads/sites/88/2024/12/2024.Szpiech.rabbinical.christian.two_.messiashs_189-214.finalprint-1.pdf)

## Christian typology

Joseph is read as a type of Jesus:

- beloved son;
- rejected by brothers;
- stripped of a garment;
- sold for silver;
- falsely accused;
- descends into pit/prison;
- raised to authority;
- provides bread;
- forgives persecutors;
- saves both family and nations.

This typology is theological analogy, not a claim made explicitly by Genesis. Patristic and medieval art especially favors Joseph sold, Joseph resisting temptation, and Joseph recognized by his brothers.

## Islamic tradition

Qur’an Sura 12, *Yūsuf*, calls Joseph’s story “the best of narratives.” It retells:

- the dream of eleven stars, sun, and moon;
- brothers’ jealousy;
- the well;
- sale in Egypt;
- attempted seduction;
- imprisonment;
- dream interpretation;
- famine administration;
- Benjamin and the cup;
- reunion and fulfillment of the dream.

Distinctive Qur’anic elements include women cutting their hands on seeing Joseph’s beauty, Joseph’s prayer for prison rather than sin, and a witness reasoning from the direction of the torn shirt. The woman is unnamed in the Qur’an; Zulaikha is later tradition. [Qur’an 12](https://quran.com/12)

The Qur’anic *asbāṭ*, “tribes/descendants,” occur in broader Israelite contexts. The twelve springs struck by Moses correspond to the twelve tribal divisions (Qur’an 2:60; 7:160), but the Qur’an does not provide a Joseph-tribe zodiac or banner.

## Art and literature

Major afterlives include:

- medieval illuminated Bibles and Qur’anic manuscripts;
- Persian *Yūsuf and Zulaykhā* romances, especially Jāmī;
- Rembrandt and other European paintings of Joseph and Potiphar’s wife;
- Thomas Mann’s tetralogy *Joseph and His Brothers*;
- Andrew Lloyd Webber and Tim Rice’s *Joseph and the Amazing Technicolor Dreamcoat*;
- Chagall’s biblical paintings and Hadassah window;
- Richard Strauss’s *Josephslegende*.

Each period emphasizes a different Joseph: dreamer, chaste youth, providential statesman, erotic beloved, exile, or reconciler.

# Afterlife and claimants

## Samaritans

**[Living communal tradition]** Samaritans identify themselves chiefly with Ephraim, Manasseh, and Levi—Israelites who remained in the land rather than foreigners imported by Assyria. Mount Gerizim is their chosen sanctuary.

Jewish polemical sources, including 2 Kings 17 and Josephus, emphasize foreign settlers and syncretism. Modern scholarship generally reconstructs a community emerging from surviving northern Israelites, regional inhabitants, and later separation from Judean Judaism during Persian and Hellenistic times.

Genetic studies find strong affinities between Samaritan paternal lineages and Jewish/Cohanic or wider Levantine populations, supporting substantial ancient Israelite/Levantine continuity. Genetics cannot identify a chromosome as specifically “Ephraim” or “Manasseh.” [Oefner et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25079122/), [full study](https://web.stanford.edu/group/rosenberglab/papers/OefnerEtAl2013-HumBiol.pdf)

This is the strongest living claim to continuity with the historical population of Joseph’s central territory, though exact tribal genealogy remains unprovable.

## Bnei Menashe

Kuki-Mizo-Chin communities in northeastern India and Myanmar developed or revived an Israelite identity in the twentieth century, particularly following a 1951 religious vision and later contact with Rabbi Eliyahu Avichail’s Amishav movement. Avichail popularized the name “Bnei Menashe.”

In 2005 Sephardi Chief Rabbi Shlomo Amar recognized their claimed Israelite connection sufficiently to require formal conversion rather than accepting uninterrupted halakhic Jewish status. Thousands subsequently migrated to Israel through conversion-and-immigration programs.

**Evidence:**

- Strong contemporary communal belief and reconstructed Jewish practice.
- Oral parallels invoked with Exodus and an ancestor called Manmasi.
- No ancient documentary chain connecting the community to Assyrian Manasseh.
- Genetic results are mixed in popular reporting; the principal Y-chromosome study found East/Southeast Asian lineages and no diagnostic Jewish paternal signature. Limited maternal similarities are neither tribe-specific nor proof of the migration narrative. [Academic identity study](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Ad86e09ea-a3a6-4795-bc33-4c10734d76c6/files/dhx11xg13q), [genetics and identity review](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/15036/1/173%20pdf.pdf)

Classification: **modern religious-ethnic claim with institutional recognition and conversion, not demonstrated biological descent from Manasseh.**

## British Israelism

John Wilson’s *Our Israelitish Origin* (1840) systematized the claim that Anglo-Saxon peoples descended from lost Israel. Edward Hine spread the movement internationally in the later nineteenth century and associated:

- Britain/Commonwealth with Ephraim, the “multitude of nations”;
- the United States with Manasseh, the single “great people.”

Advocates use Genesis 48, Deuteronomy 33, supposed Hebrew-English word similarities, heraldry, and migration legends.

Historical linguistics, archaeology, and population genetics provide no credible migration chain from Assyrian Israel to Anglo-Saxon Britain. The system later influenced Herbert W. Armstrong’s theology and, through radical branches, Christian Identity; British Israelism itself should not be automatically equated with every racist derivative. [Jewish Encyclopedia](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/1524-anglo-israelism), [historical study](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5149/9781469611112_barkun)

## Latter-day Saint lineages

Official LDS teaching says patriarchal blessings declare a person’s lineage in the house of Israel. Ephraim and Manasseh are especially common. The lineage may be understood as literal descent, covenantal adoption, or both; it is a religious declaration, not a genetic test.

LDS scripture places Book of Mormon peoples within Joseph’s lineage, commonly through Manasseh for Lehi and Ephraim in other gathering traditions. [Official LDS explanation](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/gospel-topics/patriarchal-blessings?lang=eng)

Classification: **sacramental/covenantal lineage claim, inaccessible to ordinary historical verification.**

## Bene Ephraim of Andhra Pradesh

The Bene Ephraim are primarily Madiga Dalits whose Israelite-Jewish identity arose publicly in the late 1980s. Their narrative interprets caste marginalization and inherited customs through the lost-tribe framework.

There is no ancient documentation or genetic demonstration of Ephraimite descent. Scholarship treats the movement seriously as modern identity formation, religious creativity, and resistance to caste without thereby validating a Bronze/Iron Age genealogy. [Yulia Egorova and Shahid Perwez](https://academic.oup.com/book/3133), [Durham study](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1522405/from-dalits-to-bene-ephraim-judaism-in-andhra-pradesh)

## Pashtun claims

The best-known written genealogy appears in Niʿmat Allah al-Harawi’s *Makhzan-i Afghani* around 1612, tracing Afghans to Israel through Saul and an ancestor Afghana. Some versions include Joseph’s tribes.

Pashtun languages are Eastern Iranian, not descended from Hebrew. Customs cited as Israelite—levirate marriage, circumcision, dietary rules, clan organization—are widespread and not diagnostic. Genetic research shows ordinary regional Central/South Asian and Iranian affinities, not a demonstrable descent from deported Israelite tribes. [Historical review](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/pub/israiliyat/article/668708), [Cambridge paper](https://www.cambridge.org/engage/api-gateway/coe/assets/orp/resource/item/656d6e765bc9fcb5c9fe037f/original/are-pashtuns-the-lost-tribe-of-israel.pdf)

## Igbo

Some Igbo communities and Jewish-identifying Igbo groups claim Israelite descent, sometimes specifying Gad, Manasseh, or other tribes. The claims became especially prominent through colonial-era biblical interpretation, missionary contact, and modern Judaizing movements.

Cultural analogies are real as analogies but do not establish common origin. No documentary migration chain or tribe-specific genetic evidence connects the Igbo to Joseph. The communities’ present Jewish religious identity is a separate question from ancient biological descent.

## Lemba

The Lemba of southern Africa preserve traditions of northern migration, male clans, dietary restrictions, and ritual practices. Genetic research has found some West Asian-associated paternal lineages, including attention to the Buba clan, but early claims that a particular “Cohen Modal Haplotype” proved priestly Jewish ancestry were overstated.

The evidence can support some male-mediated West Asian ancestry; it cannot identify Manasseh, Ephraim, or even uniquely Jewish rather than broader Arabian/Levantine ancestry. Joseph affiliation is not their central traditional claim.

## Beta Israel

Beta Israel’s principal lost-tribe attribution is **Dan**, not Joseph. Medieval Jewish authorities and modern advocates used Danite descent as one explanation of Ethiopian Jewish origins. Scholarship also examines indigenous Ethiopian, Aksumite, Agaw, Christian, and Judaizing contexts.

Their established Jewish communal identity and Israeli recognition do not supply proof of descent from biblical Dan. They belong in the wider lost-tribe history but not as Joseph claimants.

# Controversies and disputes

## Etymology

- Genesis explicitly explains Joseph as “may he add.”
- The preceding “taken away” creates a second pun.
- Whether the historical name arose exactly this way is unknowable.
- Both wordplays are literary facts; neither is a modern linguistic proof of an individual’s historicity.

## Historicity of Joseph

No Egyptian text securely names the biblical Joseph, Zaphenath-paneah, his famine, or Israel’s settlement through his office. Egyptian color—titles, Heliopolis, signet, linen, embalming—shows knowledgeable literary setting but not direct attestation.

Major proposals date the novella’s composition or final form anywhere from monarchic to Persian/Hellenistic periods. Egyptian loanwords and court motifs coexist with late Hebrew and wisdom-novella features. The prudent conclusion is:

- Joseph is securely a central literary and ancestral figure.
- A historical individual behind the traditions is possible.
- His biography cannot presently be independently reconstructed.

## Joseph story composition

Disputed features include:

- Ishmaelites versus Midianites.
- Reuben versus Judah as rescuer.
- multiple recognitions and journeys.
- Rachel’s apparent presence in Joseph’s second dream.
- duplicated naming and descent motifs.

Documentary-source critics find combined J/E traditions; literary critics emphasize deliberate repetition and character transformation. The final narrative is highly integrated, so compositional history remains contested. [Literary-complexity study](https://www.thetorah.com/article/the-historical-and-literary-complexity-of-the-joseph-story)

## Did twelve tribes exist simultaneously?

The varying lists, Machir/Gilead in Judges 5, Levi’s special status, and Joseph’s division make a permanently fixed league doubtful. “Twelve” likely has symbolic completeness as well as political use. This does not mean the named regional groups were imaginary.

## Conquest and territory

Joshua’s mapped allotments coexist with admissions that Canaanite cities remained unconquered. Archaeology supports gradual highland settlement and later territorial consolidation more readily than one short unified campaign. Boundaries may reflect later administrative memory projected into the conquest age.

## Ephraim versus Manasseh

Genesis 48 privileges Ephraim, while Numbers’ second census makes Manasseh much larger. The Song of Deborah names Machir rather than Manasseh. These differences may preserve shifting dominance, different source periods, or distinct counting conventions.

## Assyrian “lostness”

Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom and deported many people, but:

- not everyone was deported;
- deportees were not necessarily kept in tribal units;
- northern refugees entered Judah;
- Samarian populations persisted.

“Ten Lost Tribes” is therefore a post-conquest theological and legendary category, not an exact demographic description.

## Breastplate stone

The assignment depends on two uncertainties:

1. the order of tribal names;
2. the identity and ordering of ancient stones.

Joseph = *šōham*/onyx is traditional and supported by Josephus’ birth-order principle and Numbers Rabbah. It is not explicitly stated in Exodus, and “onyx” is not mineralogically certain.

## Banner

Numbers mentions standards but describes no colors or pictures. Black/Egypt/bull/*reʾēm* belongs to Numbers Rabbah; the three-colored western standard and young man belong to Pseudo-Jonathan. Claims that the wilderness Israelites demonstrably carried these later designs turn interpretation into history.

## Month

*Sefer Yetzirah* maps letters, months, zodiac signs, and organs/faculties, but the basic text does not name tribes. Tribe-month tables belong to commentaries and later syntheses. Thus “Joseph’s month” has no single ancient answer:

- Joseph as one name: none.
- Ephraim: commonly Tishri.
- Manasseh: commonly Marḥeshvan.
- Competing schemes exist.

## Zodiac

There is no single Jewish, Christian, or occult consensus:

- Joseph’s bow → Sagittarius.
- Joseph/Ephraim’s bull → Taurus.
- fruitful waters → Pisces.
- camp sequence → Libra/Scorpio for Ephraim/Manasseh.
- Golden Dawn → Taurus/Gemini.
- Blavatsky → Joseph Sagittarius.

Josephus and Philo establish twelvefold cosmic analogy but not the later detailed tables. Claims that their authority validates one precise modern assignment over another are overstated.

## “Every scheme”

No finite dossier can capture every privately published or internet-generated table. More importantly, many purported “ancient schemes” are copied without primary citations. This dossier includes the historically influential and textually recoverable systems and flags unverified attributions rather than manufacturing certainty.

# Open questions

1. Was Joseph originally a personal ancestor tradition, a name for a tribal league, or both?
2. Does *bānôt ṣāʿădāh ʿălê-šûr* in Genesis 49 describe branches, daughters, towns, or onlookers?
3. What exact animal was the biblical *reʾēm*?
4. What mineral did *šōham* denote in Iron Age priestly vocabulary?
5. How much of Genesis 37–50 is monarchic northern tradition, and how much is exilic or Persian-period wisdom literature?
6. Did “Machir” precede “Manasseh” as the normal name of the eastern group?
7. How closely do Joshua’s borders reflect premonarchic settlement rather than later administrative geography?
8. Which population captured Samaria—Shalmaneser V’s army or Sargon II’s—and how should their overlapping claims be apportioned?
9. What proportion of northern Israelites was deported, remained, or migrated to Judah?
10. How directly do today’s Samaritans descend from pre-722 northern populations, and how did their community crystallize?
11. Which recension and commentary first attached named tribes to *Sefer Yetzirah*’s months?
12. Can Kircher’s exact Joseph correspondence be securely recovered from a specified folio rather than later derivative charts?
13. How much of the *Testament of Joseph* is Jewish, and how much reflects Christian editing?
14. Did Messiah ben Joseph develop from Joshua/Ephraim restoration traditions, from a suffering-warrior expectation, or from later harmonization?
15. How should modern conversion-based Joseph identities be described without either dismissing living religious identity or treating it as genetic proof?
