# San Guinefort: Pantheon Dossier

> **Scope and evidentiary warning.** San Guinefort—more commonly *Saint Guinefort* or *Saint Guignefort*—is not securely attested as a “divine being” in the strict sense. The earliest source calls him a dead greyhound whom local people honored as a martyr and petitioned for aid. He was never an officially canonized saint, god, demon, or member of a formal pantheon. The same source’s Dominican author classified the cult as idolatrous superstition and interpreted its supernatural agents as demons. “Folk saint,” “holy greyhound,” and “child-healing intercessor” are therefore the least misleading classifications.
>
> The dossier’s evidence is unusually asymmetrical. Almost everything known about the thirteenth-century cult comes through one hostile clerical witness, Étienne de Bourbon. There is no medieval image, inscription, shrine dedication, liturgy, miracle book, or text written by Guinefort’s devotees. Later evidence consists of an 1877 edition, nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore reports, archaeology, oral-history work in the 1970s, and modern reinvention. Claims of uninterrupted, unchanged worship for seven centuries exceed what the record can prove.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identification

Guinefort is the name attached to a legendary hunting dog—specifically a greyhound—honored near Neuville in the medieval diocese of Lyon. According to Étienne de Bourbon’s Latin *Tractatus de diversis materiis praedicabilibus*, composed around 1250–1261, the dog killed a serpent threatening its master’s infant, was mistaken for the child’s killer, and was slain by its master. After the castle was abandoned, peasants reportedly visited the dog’s burial place, honored it “as a martyr,” and sought help there in sickness or need. Mothers of sick or weak children performed an elaborate healing or changeling-reversal ritual in the grove.

The documented Guinefort consequently occupies several historically distinct categories:

| Period or viewpoint | Classification |
|---|---|
| Local devotees, as reported c. 1250–1261 | *Sanctus* and *martyr*; posthumous healer or intercessor |
| Étienne de Bourbon | A dead *canis leporarius* wrongly treated as a saint; the associated cult was idolatry, superstition, divination, and demonic deception |
| Nineteenth-century antiquarians and folklorists | Legendary animal saint and survival of rural popular religion |
| Jean-Claude Schmitt and later historians | A folk saint whose dossier reveals interaction among oral narrative, unofficial ritual, clerical repression, landscape, and changing memory |
| Popular modern reception | “Dog saint,” patron of infants, protector of children and sometimes animals |
| Contemporary artistic or devotional reinvention | Occasionally treated as an unofficial patron of dogs or invoked in newly composed prayers; these uses are modern, not medieval inheritances |

### Names and spellings

- **Latin:** *sanctus Guinefortis* or inflected *sanctum Guinefortem*; the animal is a *canis leporarius*, literally a hare-hunting dog or greyhound. The frequently printed heading *De adoratione Guinefortis canis* (“On the worship of the dog Guinefort”) is a manuscript marginal title, not necessarily Étienne’s own title. The Latin text begins by placing the case among *superstitiones contumeliosae Deo*, superstitions insulting to God. The Latin and an English translation are reproduced in the Cambridge excerpt of Schmitt’s study and in the Internet Medieval Sourcebook. [Cambridge University Press excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805211/08805/excerpt/9780521108805_excerpt.pdf)
- **French:** *Guinefort*, *saint Guinefort*, and regionally *Guignefort* or *saint Guignefort*.
- **English:** Saint Guinefort, the Holy Greyhound, the Greyhound Saint.
- **Spanish:** San Guinefort, santo lebrel.
- **German:** der heilige Windhund.
- **Italian:** san Guinefort or Guinefort il levriero.
- **Portuguese:** São Guinefort.
- **Russian:** Гинфорт (*Ginfort*).

### Etymology

No securely demonstrated etymology of the dog’s individual name survives in the medieval source. Several competing explanations must be kept separate:

1. **Transfer from a human Saint Guinefort/Gunifort of Pavia.** This is the most historically productive scholarly proposal. A human martyr called Guinefort, Gunifort, Winifort, or a related form was venerated at Pavia, with a feast on 22 August. Jean-Claude Schmitt proposed that the saint’s name could have reached the Dombes through ecclesiastical or monastic circulation and become attached to an existing healing place or narrative. A later study emphasizes the spread of the Pavia saint’s cult through France. This is a hypothesis of cultic transfer, not proof that the dog was named after him. [Rebecca Rist, “The Papacy, Inquisition and Saint Guinefort the Holy Greyhound”](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/81816/)

2. **Germanic personal-name derivation.** Forms such as *Winifrid*, *Winefrid*, *Guinifort*, or *Gunifort* may involve Germanic *wini-* (“friend”) and a second element later assimilated to French *fort*. This is plausible for the human saint’s name but does not independently explain how it became the greyhound’s.

3. **Breton or Celtic-looking derivation.** Popular sources sometimes derive *Guinefort* from *guen/gwen* (“white, pure”) plus *frout* (“stream, torrent”), yielding “pure stream.” This is poorly demonstrated for the Dombes dog and appears to conflate later hagionymic or toponymic speculation with the medieval name.

4. **Popular French wordplay.** *Guignefort* has been associated with *guigner* (“to peer, squint, look sideways”) plus *fort*, helping connect human saints of the name with eye disorders. This is folk etymology or later rationalization, not an established medieval derivation.

5. **Greek *kynephoros* or similar constructions.** Internet sources occasionally claim an origin meaning “dog-bearer.” No reliable philological demonstration has been located; this should be treated as modern speculation.

### Epithets and cult titles

The primary record is sparse:

- **“Saint” — *sanctus Guinefortus/Guinefortis*.** Reported by Étienne as the devotees’ classification.
- **“Martyr” — *martyr*.** Étienne says peasants honored the innocently killed dog as a martyr.
- **“Dog” — *canis*.** Biological classification in the source.
- **“Greyhound” — *canis leporarius* / *leporarius*.** The earliest and only secure medieval description of its type.
- **“Healer of children” — *guérisseur d’enfants*.** A modern scholarly summary made canonical by Schmitt’s 1979 title, although it accurately describes the function of the recorded rites.
- **“Holy Greyhound” / “Saint Lévrier.”** Modern scholarly and popular labels, not attested medieval cult titles.
- **“Patron of infants,” “patron of dogs,” or “protector of animals.”** The first is a reasonable modern summary of the child-healing function; the latter two are modern extrapolations and are not in Étienne’s account.

### Distinguishing the dog from human saints

The greyhound must not be silently identified with officially or semi-officially venerated human saints bearing similar names:

- **Guinefort/Gunifort of Pavia:** a human pilgrim or martyr, traditionally associated with Pavia and 22 August.
- **French Guinefort traditions:** later notices speak of a Scottish-born bishop, an abbot of Saint-Ursin, or a martyr at Abbeville. Their historical relationships and even identities are unstable.
- Churches, chapels, statues, relics, and feast days dedicated to a human Guinefort cannot automatically be counted as evidence for worship of the dog. The possibility of confusion or substitution is itself part of the history.

The now-popular **22 August feast day for the greyhound** almost certainly derives from the human Pavia saint’s calendar date. Étienne supplies no feast day.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest secure evidence

The earliest secure attestation is Étienne de Bourbon’s Latin exemplum in the *Tractatus de diversis materiis praedicabilibus*, composed during the final part of his life, conventionally c. 1250–1261. FAMA dates the work approximately 1250–1256 and records more than twenty manuscripts for the larger *Tractatus*. [FAMA manuscript-work record](https://fama.irht.cnrs.fr/oeuvre/326206)

Étienne says the events occurred “recently” (*nuper*) while he was preaching against divination and hearing confessions in the diocese of Lyon. Numerous women confessed that they had taken their children “to Saint Guinefort.” His account therefore contains several evidentiary layers:

1. Étienne’s claimed personal activity as preacher and confessor.
2. Confessional reports from unnamed women.
3. His inquiry into Guinefort’s identity.
4. An oral etiological legend about the dog, household, serpent, and grave.
5. Reports about the ritual, some apparently supplied by multiple witnesses.
6. His claimed visit to the place and destruction of the bones and grove.
7. A prohibition allegedly issued by the local lords.

What it **does establish** is that by the middle of the thirteenth century a Dominican writer believed that women in the Lyonnais/Dombes were taking children to a place called Saint Guinefort, that a canine martyr legend explained the site, and that he intervened against it.

What it **does not establish** is:

- that a historical dog performed the rescue;
- when the legend originated;
- how long the cult had existed before Étienne;
- whether every ritual detail was universal;
- whether the exhumed bones were canine;
- the exact words used by practitioners in their own language;
- continuous practice in every intervening century;
- official canonization or institutional Church recognition.

### Physical and manuscript transmission

The lost authorial original was a Latin prose manuscript. The earliest important witness cited in the modern Guinefort literature is:

- **Paris, Bibliothèque nationale de France, MS Latin 15970, fols. 413va–414ra**, a thirteenth-century manuscript preserving the Guinefort exemplum.

The larger *Tractatus* survives in numerous medieval witnesses and derivative arrangements. Biblissima identifies, among others, BnF Latin 14598–14601, formerly belonging to the Abbey of Saint-Victor, as witnesses to the *Tractatus* tradition. Those volumes range in date from the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries; this broad manuscript record must not be confused with multiple independent eyewitness accounts of the cult. [Biblissima manuscript record](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark%3A/43093/gdata4d2f4fab7209f6cf6daae4fbb641c1fcfa30f431)

No illustrated medieval Guinefort manuscript has been securely identified in the sources consulted. The physical support is parchment manuscript, not inscription, papyrus, monumental art, or cult statue.

### Principal primary text: contents

Étienne’s account may be summarized closely as follows:

- Near the village of enclosed nuns called Neuville, on the land of the lord of Villars, stood a castle.
- Its lord and lady had an infant son and a greyhound.
- The lord, lady, and nurse left the house, leaving the infant in its cradle.
- A great serpent entered and approached the cradle.
- The greyhound pursued it, overturned the cradle in the fight, suffered bites, killed the serpent, and threw it away from the child.
- The dog remained beside the cradle, bloody from the combat.
- The nurse returned, saw the disorder and blood, assumed the dog had killed the infant, and cried out.
- The mother made the same inference.
- The lord drew his sword and killed the dog.
- They then found the child unharmed and the serpent dead.
- Realizing the dog’s innocence, they cast its body into a well, covered the well with stones, and planted trees around it as a memorial.
- The castle was later destroyed and the land deserted.
- Peasants who heard the story visited the place, honored the dog as a martyr, and prayed there in sickness or need.
- Étienne interpreted apparent responses as demonic deception.

The healing ritual, according to Étienne, was directed especially toward sick or weak children:

1. A mother consulted an old woman in a fortified settlement about one league away.
2. The old woman instructed or accompanied her.
3. Salt and other offerings were made.
4. The child’s swaddling clothes were hung on surrounding bushes.
5. Nails were driven into the trees.
6. The naked child was passed or thrown nine times between the trunks of two trees, from the mother to the old woman.
7. They invoked the “fauns” of the forest of *Rimite/Rimita*, telling them to take back the sick child—understood as theirs—and return the mother’s own child, fat, healthy, safe, and sound.
8. The child was laid naked on straw from its cradle beneath a tree.
9. Two short candles were fixed or placed near its head and lit.
10. The women withdrew so that they could neither see the infant nor hear its cries.
11. Étienne says several witnesses reported that candles burned and killed some children.
12. If the child was still alive, it was taken to a nearby river and immersed nine times.
13. Étienne says robust children survived, whereas frail children died or nearly died.

Étienne then states that he went to the site, summoned the people, preached against the practice, had the dead dog exhumed, had the grove cut down, burned the bones with the wood, and obtained an edict from the local lords threatening confiscation or financial penalty against future visitors.

The full Latin opening is available in the Cambridge excerpt; the complete episode appeared in Albert Lecoy de La Marche’s 1877 edition at pp. 325–329. [Cambridge University Press excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805211/08805/excerpt/9780521108805_excerpt.pdf) [Internet Medieval Sourcebook translation](https://www.historyclass.org/2PM/04apr-may/guinefort.htm)

### Printed editions and translations

- **1877 — Albert Lecoy de La Marche, ed., *Anecdotes historiques, légendes et apologues tirés du recueil inédit d’Étienne de Bourbon*.** First influential printed presentation and attribution of the collection to Étienne. It is selective rather than a full critical edition.
- **1979 — Jean-Claude Schmitt, *Le Saint Lévrier: Guinefort, guérisseur d’enfants depuis le XIIIe siècle*.** Prints the exemplum and a translation, then places it within narrative, social, ritual, archaeological, and ethnographic contexts.
- **1982 onward — translations of Schmitt.** German, Spanish, Italian, English, Portuguese, and other translations made the dossier internationally known.
- **2002–2015 — Jacques Berlioz and Jean-Luc Eichenlaub, critical edition of the *Tractatus*, Corpus Christianorum, Continuatio Mediaevalis 124 and following volumes.** This is the modern critical framework for Étienne’s work. The Brepols edition describes the *Tractatus* as an exceptionally large exemplum collection shaped by Étienne’s preaching and inquisitorial experience. [Brepols edition](https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503042411-1)

### Other medieval narrative parallels—not Guinefort attestations

The “faithful animal rashly killed” story circulated independently of the Dombes cult:

- Sanskrit *Pañcatantra*: a mongoose kills a snake threatening a child but is killed by the child’s mother.
- Arabic and Persian *Kalīla wa-Dimna* traditions: a weasel or comparable animal fills the role.
- *Dolopathos* / *Seven Sages of Rome*: European dog-and-child versions.
- Later *Gesta Romanorum* versions.
- Welsh Gelert tradition: a dog killed after saving a child from a wolf; its familiar localization at Beddgelert is much later than Étienne.

These are narrative analogues, not independent evidence that Guinefort himself was worshipped outside the Dombes. The tale family is classified as **ATU 178A**, “The Faithful Mongoose,” and also indexed through more granular faithful-dog motifs. The Seven Sages database supplies a broad list of textual analogues. [Seven Sages of Rome database](https://db.seven-sages-of-rome.org/Canis)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and functions

The sources support the following functions, in descending order of security:

- **Healing or adjudicating the fate of sick and weak children:** securely reported by Étienne.
- **Recovering children believed exchanged by woodland beings:** securely part of the reported ritual.
- **Aid in general sickness or need:** Étienne says peasants prayed at the site when ill or in need of something.
- **Protection of an infant from a serpent:** narrative role in the etiological legend.
- **Martyr-like innocence and fidelity:** the reason given for local honor.
- **Protection of dogs or all animals:** modern extrapolation only.
- **Patronage of greyhounds:** modern commemorative usage, not medieval evidence.

The rite is not simply a prayer for cure. Its wording presupposes a **changeling diagnosis**: the weak infant is treated as a being belonging to the woodland “fauns,” who are asked to return the mother’s true, healthy child. The outcome could be read retrospectively as judgment: a child who survived the abandonment, candle exposure, and nine immersions was confirmed or restored; a frail child might die. Étienne calls the mothers child-killers, but that description is his prosecutorial and theological judgment.

### Physical form

The only secure medieval physical classification is *canis leporarius*, a greyhound or coursing hound. The source does not record:

- coat color;
- sex in an explicit anatomical statement, although Latin grammar and later retellings treat the dog as male;
- age;
- breed in the modern kennel-club sense;
- collar, armor, halo, or other regalia.

Modern reddish, white, black, or brindled depictions are artistic choices.

### Iconography

There is no securely attested medieval cult image of the greyhound Guinefort. No medieval statue, panel, fresco, seal, reliquary, pilgrim badge, or illuminated scene can presently be assigned to his Dombes cult.

Modern iconography commonly combines:

- a slender greyhound;
- halo or nimbus;
- infant or overturned cradle;
- dead or attacked serpent;
- well, cairn, or grove;
- candles, trees, hanging cloths, and nails;
- martyr’s palm or saintly frontal pose.

These elements are visual reconstructions from Étienne’s prose, not continuations of a documented medieval image type. A frequently reproduced “Saint Guinefort” image associated online with Jeanne-Élisabeth Chaudet is especially hazardous: Chaudet painted children and dogs, but an attractive historical dog painting is not thereby a medieval or cult image of Guinefort. Modern art pages explicitly label their images contemporary. [Wikimedia Commons contemporary illustration](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ASt_Guinefort.jpg)

Claims that a Guinefort scene occurs in the *Hours of Henry VIII* are modern artistic invention or pastiche unless supported by a manuscript catalogue and folio identification. A contemporary artist’s “Miracle of Saint Guinefort” openly borrows a Veronese greyhound as its model; it is not an image from that Book of Hours.

### Symbols and associated objects

Securely attested through Étienne:

- greyhound;
- serpent;
- cradle and cradle straw;
- well;
- stones;
- grove and paired trees;
- swaddling clothes;
- nails;
- salt and unspecified additional offerings;
- two short candles;
- nearby river;
- the number nine;
- sword.

No medieval source assigns Guinefort a sacred color, plant species, celestial body, weekday, month, or formal liturgical number. Identification of the trees as elder, hawthorn, or honeysuckle varies in translations and comparative folklore; Étienne’s introductory discussion mentions women worshipping *sambucae*, usually elder trees, but the Guinefort narrative itself should not be forced into a single botanical reconstruction without manuscript-level analysis.

### Powers and limitations

The cultic logic attributed efficacy to Guinefort’s place and name, but the ritual’s spoken invocation was directed to the woodland *fauni*. Guinefort’s precise agency is therefore ambiguous: healer, martyr-intercessor, owner of the shrine, or legitimating story for a changeling rite.

The being was neither omnipotent nor institutionally guaranteed. The source’s own outcome model admits death. Moreover, Guinefort’s material remains and grove could be destroyed by human authority. No resurrection, speech, apparition, oracle, or direct miracle of the dog is narrated.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No parentage, birth story, mate, offspring, siblings, breed-line, or divine genealogy is attested. Guinefort belongs to a lord’s household and serves as guardian of its infant, but these are social—not genealogical—relations.

### Human and supernatural relations

- **Unnamed lord/master:** owns the dog and kills it with a sword after misreading the bloody scene.
- **Unnamed lady:** mother of the infant; initially concludes that the dog killed the child.
- **Unnamed nurse:** first discovers the bloody scene and raises the alarm.
- **Unnamed infant boy:** saved from the serpent.
- **Great serpent:** Guinefort’s sole narrative adversary; killed in combat.
- **Peasants:** transform memory of the dog’s unjust death into martyr veneration.
- **Mothers of sick children:** principal ritual petitioners.
- **Old woman:** ritual specialist who teaches and conducts the procedure.
- **Étienne de Bourbon:** hostile investigator and suppressor.
- **Lords of the land:** issue the reported penal edict.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

**None is securely attested.** No other named god, saint, angel, demon, fairy, or supernatural person appears in a scene with Guinefort.

The *fauni* of the forest of Rimite are a collective category, not individually named beings, and Étienne never narrates Guinefort meeting them. He interprets the cult generally as the work of the Devil and demons, but no discrete meeting, combat, alliance, judgment, or conversation between Guinefort and Satan occurs.

| Other being | Interaction | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Woodland *fauni* | Mothers invoke them at Guinefort’s site to exchange a weak child for the healthy child allegedly taken by them; Guinefort does not directly interact with them | Étienne de Bourbon, c. 1250–1261 | Securely attested ritual report |
| Devil/demons | Étienne attributes apparent cultic effects to demonic enticements and illusions; no narrative encounter with Guinefort | Étienne de Bourbon, c. 1250–1261 | Clerical interpretation |

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The faithful greyhound and the serpent

**Earliest Guinefort form:** Étienne de Bourbon, c. 1250–1261.

A household leaves an infant unattended in its cradle. A great serpent enters. The greyhound attacks beneath the cradle, overturning it, and fights until it kills and throws aside the serpent. The dog, bloodied and wounded, remains near the infant. Returning adults mistake the blood and disorder for evidence that the dog has killed the child. The lord kills the animal with his sword. The infant is then found alive beside the dead serpent. The family recognizes its error.

This is Guinefort’s sole medieval narrative cycle. Its thematic structure is:

1. loyal animal entrusted with vulnerable child;
2. hidden combat;
3. misleading physical evidence;
4. rash human judgment;
5. killing of innocent protector;
6. discovery and remorse;
7. memorial burial;
8. posthumous cult.

### B. Burial, memorial landscape, and popular canonization

The lord casts the dog into a well, fills or covers it with stones, and plants trees around it. The subsequent destruction or abandonment of the castle separates the cult from the household that generated the story. Peasants who hear of the dog’s innocence visit the place, treat it as a martyr, and pray for help.

The well, cairn, trees, and ruined habitation convert an animal story into sacred geography. Étienne’s text gives no clerical translation, relic authentication, altar, church, priest, or episcopal approval. “Canonization” here is metaphorical popular recognition, not a canon-law process.

### C. Guinefort and the changeling-healing ordeal

This ritual cycle is not a sequel performed by Guinefort in narrative time; it is what devotees reportedly did at his place:

- offerings and the deposition of clothes externalize illness;
- nails attach the petition to trees;
- nine passages between trunks move the infant across a boundary;
- the mother and ritual specialist invoke woodland beings to reverse an exchange;
- naked abandonment and candles expose the child to the supernatural;
- nine immersions complete the ordeal.

The rite’s structure is consistent with widespread European tree-passage, water-healing, votive-cloth, nail, and changeling traditions. Similarity does not prove that it is a direct survival of a particular pre-Christian cult.

### D. Comparative faithful-animal traditions

The Guinefort story belongs to a broad Eurasian tale family. Earlier or parallel forms include the mongoose and snake in the *Pañcatantra*, Arabic-Persian weasel forms, and dog versions in *Dolopathos* and the *Seven Sages of Rome*. Later analogues include Gelert.

Three historical possibilities remain distinct:

1. The Dombes legend was directly adapted from a circulating literary tale.
2. A preacher’s or local narrator’s oral version derived indirectly from the same international tale family.
3. Étienne shaped local information into a familiar exemplum structure.

No surviving source proves the precise line of transmission. The *Seven Sages* dog narrative was circulating in Europe by around 1200 and is a credible proximate analogue. It does not contain the Guinefort name or Dombes cult.

### E. Later narrative alterations

Common modern additions not found in Étienne include:

- precise birth and death dates for the dog;
- a date of death on 22 August;
- a personal name for the knight, lady, nurse, or infant;
- a claim that the knight deliberately built a formal shrine;
- a standard breed color;
- Guinefort voluntarily sacrificing his life;
- official canonization followed by “decanonization”;
- a formal Catholic feast for the dog;
- an institution called the “Church of Saint Guinefort”;
- repeated papal decrees specifically naming the dog.

These should be labeled modern elaborations unless independently sourced.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult center

The primary source places the site:

- in the medieval diocese of Lyon;
- near a village of enclosed nuns called *Nova Villa* or Neuville;
- on the estate of the lord of Villars;
- at an abandoned castle and grove;
- near the forest called Rimite;
- near water used for immersion.

Modern historical and archaeological work identifies the cult landscape with the **Bois de Saint-Guignefort in the commune of Sandrans**, near Neuville-les-Dames and Châtillon-sur-Chalaronne in the Dombes, département of Ain.

The exact equation is a historical reconstruction. Étienne supplies no modern coordinates, and local arguments have sometimes proposed other ruined sites.

### Temple or shrine

There is no evidence for a constructed temple. The shrine comprised natural and memorial features:

- the alleged burial well;
- piled stones;
- trees planted around it;
- the surrounding grove;
- a nearby stream or river.

Terms such as “sanctuary” and “shrine” are analytically useful but should not imply a building.

### Archaeology

Jean-Michel Poisson’s 1980 investigation at the Bois de Saint-Guinefort found an earthwork site on a spur cut off by a ditch, with traces of eleventh- and twelfth-century habitation. Six test trenches revealed postholes and a shallow stratigraphy: humus, a leveling deposit with occupation traces, and moraine substrate. [Poisson excavation notice](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcme_0153-9337_1981_num_11_1_1076_t1_0308_0000_2)

The archaeology supports the existence of a medieval habitation or defended site later abandoned, broadly compatible with part of Étienne’s setting. It does **not** prove:

- that the excavated site was the precise castle in the legend;
- that the dog existed;
- that a particular well contained a canine burial;
- that the full ritual continued without interruption.

A municipal archaeological inventory records the Bois de Saint-Guignefort as site 01 393 0001, with postholes and ditch, cautiously dated to the central Middle Ages. [Sandrans heritage report](https://sandrans.fr/wp-content/uploads/sites/618/2021/09/01393_rapport_1_20180313.pdf)

### Ritual personnel

No priesthood is documented.

- The principal clients were mothers of sick or weak infants.
- An unnamed “old woman” from a fortified settlement served as ritual expert.
- Her office was informal and probably based on local knowledge rather than hereditary or institutional ordination.
- Étienne labels women associated with such practices *sortilegae*, diviners or practitioners of lots, but that is a clerical classification.
- Later oral history recorded memories of women who undertook pilgrimages, lit candles, or acted on behalf of others. Whether they inherited the medieval specialist’s role cannot be proven.

### Offerings and actions

Secure thirteenth-century evidence includes:

- salt;
- unspecified other offerings;
- hanging swaddling clothes on bushes;
- driving nails into trees;
- passing the child nine times between tree trunks;
- invocation of woodland *fauni*;
- abandonment with two candles;
- nine immersions in water;
- prayer at the site in sickness and need.

No animal sacrifice, Eucharistic rite, Mass, hymn, procession, incense, clerical blessing, or divinatory oracle is specifically recorded. Nor does Étienne report sacrificing children as offerings to Guinefort. Children died in procedures that he condemned as homicidal; describing that as a formal doctrine of “human sacrifice” would go beyond the text.

### Festivals and calendar

No medieval annual festival or fixed calendar date is recorded. The modern observance of **22 August** appears borrowed from the human Saint Gunifort of Pavia. It cannot be projected into the thirteenth-century dog cult.

### Votives and inscriptions

No medieval inscription or securely proven votive object has survived. Nails, cloth, salt, and candles are known only from the text; archaeological recovery of an individual nail would not by itself establish a Guinefort votive.

No theophoric human names—such as children named after the dog as fulfillment of vows—are documented.

### Economics and politics

The cult was not attached to an institution capable of collecting regular revenue. The old woman’s authority and any gifts she received are not quantified.

Étienne’s intervention enlisted secular lordship: the landholders issued an edict threatening *spoliatio* and *redemptio*, confiscation or a redeeming fine, against future visitors. Suppression therefore joined preaching, destruction of ritual matter, and economic coercion.

The report is not evidence of a papal trial or centralized papal campaign specifically against Guinefort. Rebecca Rist places the episode within wider papal, inquisitorial, and pastoral attempts to define legitimate sanctity and control dangerous healing practices. [Rist 2019](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/81816/)

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before c. 1250: unknowable beginnings

No pre-thirteenth-century source names the dog or its cult. Earlier faithful-animal stories existed, and a human Saint Guinefort was known, but neither fact proves a pre-Christian or early-medieval canine cult in the Dombes.

The cult’s formation may have involved:

- a ruined aristocratic site;
- a well and grove already associated with healing;
- a circulating faithful-dog tale;
- transfer of a human saint’s name;
- local infant illness and changeling explanations.

This is a reconstruction, not a documented foundation narrative.

### c. 1250–1261: first documentation and suppression

Étienne encounters women confessing visits to Saint Guinefort, investigates, records the legend and rite, visits the location, preaches against it, destroys bones and trees, and secures a secular prohibition.

This is the sole securely documented medieval intervention. “The Church repeatedly prohibited the cult throughout the Middle Ages” is not established by a comparable series of named decrees.

### Later Middle Ages: textual survival, historical silence

The account survived as an exemplum in manuscripts of the *Tractatus*. That manuscript transmission demonstrates clerical preservation of Étienne’s anti-superstition narrative, not continued practice at Sandrans.

No continuous chain of fourteenth-, fifteenth-, sixteenth-, seventeenth-, and eighteenth-century reports has been demonstrated. Any claim of continuous cult must acknowledge this documentary gap.

Human saints named Guinefort continued to receive cult in France and Italy. Substitution or confusion may have allowed functions such as healing children or eyes to migrate between canine and human figures, but individual cases require local proof.

### Nineteenth century: antiquarian rediscovery and folklore testimony

Albert Lecoy de La Marche published Étienne’s Latin account in 1877. He added a notice that devotion to Saint Guinefort still existed at Romans, where women invoked the saint for husbands and children. Whether those worshippers understood Guinefort as a dog is unclear.

In 1879, A. Vayssières reported persistence of the Dombes pilgrimage. Jean-Claude Schmitt later used this report as a bridge between the medieval text and his own fieldwork. Patrick Geary and other reviewers cautioned that Vayssières’s assertion should not automatically be accepted as proof of seamless continuity.

This period transformed Guinefort from a local remembered healer into an object of folklore collection and national antiquarian curiosity.

### Early twentieth century: fading practice and changing identity

Local reports and memories collected later indicated that people continued to visit the wood for sick or weak children into the early twentieth century, perhaps as late as the 1930s or around the Second World War. Abbé Paul Armand discussed the pilgrimage in 1943.

The crucial change is that Schmitt’s informants remembered healing practices and the place but no longer necessarily remembered that the saint had been a dog. Thus continuity of location or rite is not the same as continuity of theological identity.

A 2005 retrospective on Schmitt’s work summarizes the evidentiary result: archaeology and ethnography supported continued child-healing practices at the site into the early twentieth century, while memory of a saint explicitly identified as a dog had disappeared. [Archives de sciences sociales des religions review](https://journals.openedition.org/assr/pdf/2848)

### 1970s–1980s: scholarly reconstruction

Jean-Claude Schmitt:

- re-read Étienne’s text;
- compared it with international narrative types;
- investigated human saints named Guinefort;
- examined medieval clerical attitudes to animals and sainthood;
- conducted oral-history fieldwork in the Dombes;
- helped identify the Bois de Saint-Guignefort;
- incorporated Jean-Michel Poisson’s archaeological work;
- analyzed the rite structurally through oppositions involving domestic/wild, healthy/sick, human/nonhuman, and mother/other woman.

His 1979 book made Guinefort a major case in historical anthropology and the history of popular religion. Reviews recognized its importance while questioning whether structural analysis and “long duration” risked overstating continuity. [Paul Jorion review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/hom_0439-4216_1980_num_20_3_368117)

### Late twentieth century to present: heritage, media, and diffuse revival

The site and legend became regional heritage. The Association Saint-Guignefort in Châtillon-sur-Chalaronne promotes historical and archaeological knowledge of the legend. [CTHS association record](https://cths.fr/an/societe.php?id=3083)

There is no reliable evidence of a large, organized, continuous religious community worshipping Guinefort today. What exists is diffuse:

- heritage commemoration;
- personal or internet devotion;
- greyhound-owner observances;
- contemporary icons and prayers;
- neopagan or folk-Catholic appropriation;
- animal-soul discussions;
- fiction and film.

These constitute a modern reception network, not necessarily survival of the medieval cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship as cultural reinvention

Schmitt’s *Le Saint Lévrier* did more than document Guinefort: it supplied the coherent modern image of “Guinefort, healer of children since the thirteenth century.” Translations and reviews moved the story from French medieval studies into folklore, animal studies, religious studies, and popular culture.

The modern title “Holy Greyhound” has consequently become more stable than any medieval cult title.

### Film: *Le Moine et la Sorcière* / *Sorceress*

Suzanne Schiffman’s Franco-Swiss film was produced in 1986 and released in 1987. Its screenplay was written with Pamela Berger. Tchéky Karyo plays Étienne; Christine Boisson plays Elda, a young herbal healer.

The film draws from *De adoratione Guinefortis canis* but invents characters, motives, gender politics, village conflict, and a dramatic confrontation between clerical authority and women’s healing knowledge. The medieval source’s “old woman” becomes a young and sympathetic healer. The film’s social world is a researched imaginative reconstruction, not a visualization of a complete medieval transcript.

A contemporary scholarly review explicitly notes that the original exemplum is thin and that the filmmakers had to personalize and reconstruct its context. [Persée film review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/heres_0758-3737_1988_num_10_1_1042_t1_0107_0000_1) Recent scholarship reads the film through second-wave feminism and the opposition it constructs between feminine community care and masculine institutional power. [Oxford Academic chapter](https://academic.oup.com/fordham-scholarship-online/book/61950/chapter-abstract/548439461)

### Fiction

Roger Bevand’s 2019 historical novel *L’horrible secret de Saint Guignefort* builds a mystery around Étienne’s investigation. Its plot and “secret” are explicitly modern fiction, not recovered medieval evidence. [Publisher’s record](https://www.editions-harmattan.fr/catalogue/livre/lhorrible-secret-de-saint-guignefort/14456)

Numerous shorter stories, poems, blogs, and role-playing adaptations now portray Guinefort as:

- the first or only canine saint;
- a divine protector of pets;
- a symbol of loyalty betrayed by human haste;
- an opponent of authoritarian religion;
- an emblem of animal moral worth.

All expand beyond the medieval dossier.

### Contemporary visual art

Modern icons typically portray a haloed greyhound with child and serpent. These images create a recognizable sacred portrait that the medieval cult is not known to have possessed.

Some contemporary artists compose pseudo-medieval illuminations or prayers. Unless clearly labeled, these can circulate detached from their modern authorship and be mistaken for medieval evidence. The most reliable diagnostic is provenance: no catalogued medieval manuscript, folio, church, or museum accession means the “ancient icon” claim is unestablished.

### Modern devotion

Online observance on 22 August, petitions for sick pets or children, candles, dog blessings, and private altars are documented as modern practices. Their date derives largely from the human Pavia saint’s feast.

A modern U.S. Catholic calendar feature calls Guinefort “not a traditional saint” and acknowledges that the Catholic Church does not recognize the dog as canonized. Its playful language demonstrates contemporary popular-Catholic reception, not official liturgical authorization. [U.S. Catholic modern calendar entry](https://uscatholic.org/calendar/st-guinefort-the-dog/)

No evidence was found for:

- a recognized Catholic religious order dedicated to the dog;
- inclusion of the greyhound in the Roman Martyrology;
- a substantial modern reconstructionist denomination;
- an established Golden Dawn, Theosophical, Renaissance Hermetic, or classical grimoire tradition involving Guinefort;
- historical chaos-magic texts centered on him;
- nationalist or extremist appropriation of demonstrable significance.

Isolated modern practitioners may incorporate him into occult or neopagan work, but that is individualized reinvention.

### Modern misinformation

The following circulating claims are unsupported or demonstrably late:

- **Birth on 27 May 1242 and death on 22 August 1250:** absent from Étienne; likely internet invention.
- **Official feast instituted for the dog:** confusion with the Pavia martyr.
- **Canonized and later suppressed/decanonized:** canonization never occurred.
- **A surviving medieval portrait:** none securely documented.
- **Continuous unchanged worship from 1250 to today:** documentary gaps and loss of the canine identity contradict this smooth narrative.
- **The Church repeatedly destroyed multiple dog shrines across France:** only Étienne’s specific intervention is securely recorded.
- **Guinefort was a pre-Christian Celtic god in canine form:** no ancient inscription, image, name, or text supports this.
- **The Dombes rites prove survival of a specific pagan deity:** comparative similarity is not genealogical proof.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Étienne de Bourbon as witness

Étienne is indispensable and problematic. He was:

- a Dominican preacher;
- a confessor;
- an inquisitorial investigator;
- a compiler of exempla for pastoral use;
- an opponent of divination and unofficial ritual.

He distinguishes some personal observation from things heard, but his text collapses several voices into a moralized clerical narrative. The devotees do not speak independently. His words “demons,” “idolatry,” “child-killing mothers,” and “superstition” are classifications made within Latin Christian pastoral theology.

The account should neither be rejected wholesale as fantasy nor treated as transparent ethnographic transcription.

### Was Guinefort a historical dog?

The evidence cannot decide. Three positions are possible:

1. A real dog’s death generated the cult.
2. A widely circulating faithful-animal tale became localized at an existing well or ruin.
3. An existing healing place and human saint-name attracted a canine etiological story.

The international tale parallels and the absence of independent biographical evidence favor caution. Historically, the cult and story are attested; the dog’s zoological existence is not.

### Was the cult pre-Christian?

Older folklore scholarship often interpreted trees, wells, “fauns,” numerical repetition, and changelings as survivals of paganism. Modern historians are more cautious:

- Natural-place ritual could persist or re-emerge within medieval Christianity.
- The term *fauni* is Étienne’s Latin translation or demonological classification, not necessarily the practitioners’ exact word.
- An unofficial rite need not descend unchanged from antiquity.
- No ancient Dombes deity named Guinefort is attested.

“Pre-Christian survival” is therefore an unproven origin theory.

### Was it really a cult of the dog?

In Étienne’s etiological explanation, yes: peasants honored the dog as a martyr and prayed at its site. But in the ritual action he describes, the named supernatural addressees are woodland *fauni*. The cult may have combined:

- a saint-name;
- a canine martyr legend;
- a healing site;
- changeling beliefs;
- ritual specialists;
- Christian and non-clerical practices.

Calling it simply “dog worship” reproduces Étienne’s polemical compression.

### Continuity controversy

Schmitt’s subtitle, “healer of children since the thirteenth century,” and his long-duration method encouraged a continuity model. The evidence contains:

- thirteenth-century testimony;
- a long documentary silence;
- nineteenth-century reports;
- twentieth-century memories;
- archaeological compatibility of place.

Patrick Geary criticized unreserved acceptance of Vayssières’s 1879 testimony. Later reflection emphasized that practices at the place may have persisted while knowledge that the saint was a dog disappeared. The safest conclusion is **recurrence or persistence of a healing tradition at the same landscape**, not proven unmodified continuity.

### Structuralist interpretation

Schmitt’s analysis belongs to the historical anthropology associated with Jacques Le Goff and, more broadly, the structural analysis developed around Claude Lévi-Strauss and Jean-Pierre Vernant. It treated the ritual’s oppositions as a meaningful system rather than a heap of irrational survivals.

Later historians and ethnologists have criticized strong structuralism for risking timelessness and underplaying local contingency. A retrospective review nonetheless calls Schmitt’s work methodologically formative. [ASSR retrospective](https://journals.openedition.org/assr/pdf/2848)

### Substitution by a human martyr

Henri Platelle’s study, “Saint Guinefort vénéré des Dombes: comment un martyr inconnu fut substitué à un chien-martyr,” emphasized movement between the dog and a human saint of the same name. This addresses a historical mechanism by which clerical culture could domesticate an unacceptable animal cult: retain the efficacious name and patronage while replacing the animal with a human martyr. [Platelle article](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jds_0021-8103_1980_num_1_1_1408)

The hypothesis is important but should be tested location by location. A church dedicated to human Guinefort is not automatically evidence of conscious replacement of the dog.

### The Church, animals, and sainthood

Medieval Latin theology ordinarily denied animals the rational moral agency associated with human sanctity. Rist argues, however, that Étienne’s intervention cannot be reduced to the proposition “animals cannot be saints.” His concern also involved:

- honor given to a creature as divine;
- demonic invocation;
- divination;
- rejection of churches and relics;
- physical danger to children;
- unauthorized ritual authority exercised by women.

This broader interpretation better fits the full text. [Rist 2019](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/81816/)

### State of the evidence

Survives:

- medieval manuscripts of the *Tractatus*, principally in European libraries;
- the thirteenth-century Guinefort text;
- nineteenth-century printed editions and folklore reports;
- twentieth-century articles and oral testimony;
- archaeological records of the Sandrans site;
- modern literary, filmic, artistic, and online reception.

Does not survive or has not been identified:

- Étienne’s autograph;
- writings by devotees;
- a medieval liturgy;
- cult inscriptions;
- authenticated relics;
- a securely identified canine burial;
- medieval Guinefort images;
- administrative records of a cult institution;
- a continuous chronological register of pilgrims.

### Source-critical conclusions

1. **Secure early core:** a mid-thirteenth-century Latin exemplum reports a local greyhound-martyr legend, child-healing/changeling rites, and Étienne’s suppression.
2. **Secure material context:** the identified Sandrans site contains central-medieval habitation features compatible with an abandoned settlement.
3. **Probable but discontinuously documented:** later child-healing practice persisted or recurred at the site into the early twentieth century.
4. **Uncertain:** actual historicity of the dog; exact origin of the name; direct transmission from a particular faithful-animal text; unbroken ritual continuity.
5. **Unsupported:** pre-Christian dog god, formal canonization, medieval feast on 22 August, surviving medieval iconography, exact canine birth/death dates.
6. **Modern constructions:** haloed greyhound icons, patronage of pets, standardized feast-day observance, personalized fiction, feminist cinematic reinterpretation, and internet devotional prayers.
