# El: Pantheon Dossier

> **Scope and method.** “El” presents an unusual documentary problem because Northwest Semitic *ʾil* can be both the common noun “god” and the proper name of a particular divine patriarch. This dossier treats an occurrence as the proper god El only where grammar, epithet, narrative role, divine list, or context supports that identification. Amorite and Eblaite personal names containing *il/ila* are therefore evidence for the word and perhaps for the god, but not automatically secure attestations of the Ugaritic personality. Dates assigned to compositions are distinguished from dates of surviving copies. “Canaanite” is used as a practical linguistic-historical umbrella, not as the name of one uniform religion.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

El is the senior male god of the Late Bronze Age Ugaritic pantheon: king, father of gods and humanity, source of royal authority, judge, wise counselor, and patriarch of the divine household. Ugaritic poems locate him remotely “at the source of the rivers, amid the springs of the two deeps,” usually in a tent or pavilion. He is repeatedly imagined as aged and bearded, yet sexually generative. He authorizes or ratifies the rule of younger gods rather than ordinarily fighting their battles himself. Athirat is his regular consort; the “sons of El,” the “assembly of the gods,” and the “circle/family of El” constitute his court.

This profile is secure for thirteenth-century BCE Ugarit. It must not be projected unchanged into earlier Amorite, Iron Age Phoenician, Israelite, Punic, or Hellenistic evidence. In Israel and Judah, *El* and El-titles were increasingly identified with Yahweh; in Phoenician interpretatio graeca, El was equated with Kronos, later Saturn. Those are historical transformations, not proof that Yahweh, Kronos, Saturn, and Ugaritic El were timelessly identical.

### Names and scripts

| Form | Script/language | Meaning and status |
|---|---|---|

| *ilum*, *ilu* | Akkadian | Common noun “god.” Claims that a Mesopotamian deity *Ilu* is automatically Ugaritic El are disputed. |
| *ila/ilā* | Amorite onomastics in Akkadian writing | Theophoric or divine element. Whether it always names the specific El remains unsettled. |
| אל, *ʾēl* | Phoenician, Hebrew, Aramaic consonantal writing | “god,” and sometimes a proper divine name or component of a title. |
| אֵל, *ʾĒl* | vocalized Biblical Hebrew | “God/god,” used for Israel’s deity and in archaic or inherited El-formulas. |
| אֱלוֹהַּ, *ʾĕlōah*; אֱלֹהִים, *ʾĕlōhîm* | Biblical Hebrew | Related singular and morphologically plural formations. *Elohim* often governs singular grammar when designating Israel’s God. It is not simply “El’s children.” |
| Ἦλος/*Ēlos*, Ἰλοῦς/*Ilous*, Ἔλους/*Elous* | Greek, especially Philo of Byblos as quoted by Eusebius | Greek transcriptions of Phoenician El; explicitly equated with Kronos. |

| *Elus/Kronos/Saturnus* | Greek and Latin reception | Interpretive equations, not native Bronze Age names. |
| إله, *ʾilāh* | Arabic | Cognate common noun “god/deity.” It is linguistically related but is not evidence that the historical Ugaritic cult continued unchanged into Islam. |

The underlying Proto-Semitic form is conventionally reconstructed as *\*ʾil-* “god.” An old proposal derives it from a root meaning “be strong” or “be foremost”; other proposals regard the noun as primary or the verbal comparison as uncertain. No etymology securely reconstructs the personality of the god.

### Principal Ugaritic titles

The following are attested principally in the thirteenth-century BCE Ugaritic literary tablets; translations remain debated at points. The foundational collection remains Marvin H. Pope’s *El in the Ugaritic Texts*, supplemented by Aïcha Rahmouni’s systematic study of Ugaritic epithets.

- **ṯr ʾil**, “Bull El”: among his most frequent titles, including the Baal Cycle. “Bull” signifies virility, seniority, and sovereignty; it does not prove that El was normally represented with a bull’s body.

- **ʾil mlk**, “El the King”: marks his royal presidency.

- **mlk ʾab šnm**, usually “King, Father of Years”: a title of age and duration. “Father of years” is preferable to turning El into a god of the planet Saturn; the Saturn equation is much later.

- **ʾab ʾadm**, “Father of humanity/man”: especially in the Kirta tradition.

- **ʾab bn ʾil / ʾab bny ʾil**, “father of the sons of El/gods”: formulae vary with textual restoration.

- **bny bnwt**, conventionally “Creator of creatures” or “Begetter of created things”: *bny* can emphasize begetting/building rather than creation from nothing.

- **lṭpn ʾil d pʾid**, often “the Gracious/Benevolent El, the Compassionate” or “the sagacious and discerning god El”: both halves are philologically contested. It is safer to retain *Lṭpn* as an epithet than to build theology on one English rendering.

- **ḥkm / wise El**: wisdom is conveyed more often through action and description than through one fixed title. In the Aqhat epic he understands Anat’s intention before she states it.

- **ʾil qn ʾarṣ**, “El, creator/possessor of Earth”: best known from first-millennium Phoenician and related evidence and reflected in Hittite *Elkunirša*. The root *qny* can mean acquire, possess, beget, or create; “creator” is not the only possible translation.

- **ʾil ʿly / ʾil ʿlywn**, “El Most High”: relevant to later Northwest Semitic and biblical evidence, but no simple, universally accepted Ugaritic title *El Elyon* should be retrojected from Genesis 14. In the Sefire treaty, *ʾl wʿlyn* can be read either “El and Elyon” or as a coordinated title; the syntax is disputed.

### Biblical El-titles

The Hebrew Bible preserves or constructs several El-formulas:

- **El Elyon**, “El/God Most High,” Genesis 14:18–22. Melchizedek is priest of *El Elyon*, “maker/possessor of heaven and earth.” The narrative identifies or associates this title with Yahweh in Abram’s oath. The passage is literary evidence for Israelite reception, not a contemporary archive of Middle Bronze Jerusalem.

- **El Shaddai**, traditionally “God Almighty,” Genesis 17:1; 28:3; 35:11; 43:14; 48:3; 49:25; Exodus 6:3. The etymology of *šadday* is unresolved; connections with “mountain,” Akkadian *šadû*, fertility, or “destroyer” are proposals, not facts. Exodus 6:2–3 explicitly systematizes El Shaddai as the name under which the patriarchs knew the same deity later revealed as Yahweh. W. Randall Garr analyzes this as Priestly theological and grammatical identification.

- **El Olam**, “El/God Everlasting,” Genesis 21:33 at Beersheba.

- **El Bethel**, “El/God of Bethel” or “the god Bethel,” Genesis 31:13; 35:7. Whether Bethel is a place-title, an independent deity, or both in different settings must be decided passage by passage.

- **El Roi**, “El/God who sees me,” Genesis 16:13, a literary naming episode involving Hagar.

- **El Elohe Israel**, “El, God of Israel” or “the god of Israel,” Genesis 33:20 at Shechem.

These titles belong to the transmitted Hebrew literary corpus, much of which reached its present form in the first millennium BCE. They are not direct transcripts of Ugaritic cult.

### Cross-cultural identifications

1. **El–Kronos:** Explicit in Philo of Byblos’ Greek *Phoenician History*, composed probably in the late first or early second century CE and preserved in Eusebius, *Praeparatio evangelica* 1.9–10, early fourth century CE. This is a Phoenician-Hellenistic learned equation.

2. **El–Saturn:** A Roman extension of the Kronos equation. It should not be used to infer that Bronze Age El governed the planet Saturn.

3. **El–Yahweh:** Not a foreign interpretatio but an Israelite historical identification or absorption. Exodus 6:2–3, Genesis’s El-titles, and passages where Yahweh bears El’s patriarchal and council attributes document the completed literary identification. Its precise date and mechanism remain debated.

4. **El–Baal Hammon:** Similarities between enthroned senior-god iconography and later Punic Baal Hammon have prompted theories of continuity or absorption. Direct identity is not established by an uninscribed image alone.

5. **El–Amurru, Dagan, Kumarbi, or Enlil:** Functional comparisons and proposed historical connections occur in scholarship. None is a securely documented timeless equation comparable to Philo’s “Elus is Kronos.”

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

The earliest *linguistic* evidence is the Semitic noun *il/ilu* and its occurrence in third- and early second-millennium personal names. Ebla, Mari, and other archives preserve names containing *il*. Their clay tablets are real early evidence for the word and for theophoric language, but they often cannot distinguish “the god,” “my god,” or a particular god named El. Alfonso Archi and others consequently resist treating every Eblaite *il* as the Ugaritic El; some scholarship goes as far as regarding the fully individualized El as a later West Semitic or Ugaritic development.

The safest formulation is:

- **Third millennium BCE, Ebla:** *il* occurs in names and lexical material; the specific Ugaritic patriarch is not securely described.

- **Early second millennium BCE, Amorite/Mari onomastics:** names with *ila/il* may invoke El, but the proper-name interpretation remains debated because Amorite is known largely through names written by Akkadian scribes.

- **Fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE, Ugarit:** the first extensive, indisputable corpus in which El is a fully characterized proper deity. The surviving tablets come from the final centuries of Ugarit and were buried by the city’s destruction around 1190 BCE.

Ugarit was rediscovered after the accidental discovery of a tomb at Ras Shamra in 1928; formal excavations began in 1929. Major myth tablets came chiefly from the House of the High Priest on the acropolis. The Göttingen edition project’s catalogue records findspots, excavation numbers, museum numbers, and KTU correspondences.

### Primary corpus

#### Ugaritic myth and epic

- **Baal Cycle, KTU 1.1–1.6**  
  **Language/support/date:** Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform on six principal clay tablets, thirteenth century BCE copies; written by the scribe Ilimilku, according to colophons. Most were found in the House of the High Priest.  
  **El’s role:** divine king and judge; initially favors Yamm’s kingship; orders Kothar-wa-Hasis to build Yamm’s palace; receives Athirat and Anat when they seek permission for Baal’s house; dreams of Baal’s return; grieves violently when Baal is reported dead; authorizes Anat to select a replacement; finally supports Baal against Mot.  
  **Critical editions/translations:** KTU/CAT; Dennis Pardee; Mark S. Smith and Wayne Pitard; Nicolas Wyatt; the developing Göttingen *Edition des ugaritischen poetischen Textkorpus*.

- **Kirta/Keret Epic, KTU 1.14–1.16**  
  **Date:** thirteenth-century BCE tablets; narrative may incorporate older oral material, which cannot be independently dated.  
  **Role:** El appears to the childless, bereaved King Kirta in a dream, tells him how to obtain Hurriya as wife, blesses him with offspring, later convenes the gods to heal him, and creates the female healer Šaʿtiqatu when the gods do not answer.

- **Aqhat Epic, KTU 1.17–1.19**  
  **Role:** El grants Danel a son after ritual supplication; receives Anat’s request to threaten Aqhat; understands and authorizes her action in ambiguous language; later participates in the divine background to Aqhat’s death and its consequences. The ending is lost.

- **Birth of the Gracious Gods, KTU 1.23**  
  **Role:** El encounters two women by the sea, engages in a repeated sexual-ritual episode, and fathers Shahar and Shalim, Dawn and Dusk. The text combines myth, liturgical directions, and fertility imagery. Whether the women are Athirat and Rahmay, two manifestations of one goddess, or unnamed human/divine females is debated.

- **El’s Banquet or the Drunken El, KTU 1.114**  
  **Role:** El hosts a feast, becomes extremely drunk, is helped home by younger gods, encounters a figure described like a horned-and-tailed creature, collapses in his own excrement or urine, and is treated with a remedy. The text does not support an idealized picture of El as always solemn.

- **Fragmentary myths, including KTU 1.12 and related texts**  
  El appears as father and authority in damaged episodes involving divine offspring or destructive beings. Reconstruction varies too much to create a continuous story.

#### Ugaritic ritual, cult, and administrative texts

- **Pantheon lists KTU 1.47, KTU 1.118, and Akkadian RS 20.24:** El occurs near the head of formally ordered divine lists. Comparison between Ugaritic and Akkadian versions is central to identifying logographic and local divine names.

- **Sacrifice and offering texts KTU 1.39, 1.40, 1.41/1.87, 1.109, 1.148 and others:** El receives animals, wine, and other offerings among the gods. These are stronger evidence for actual institutional cult than mythic prominence alone.

- **KTU 1.161, royal funerary liturgy:** invokes royal ancestors/Rephaim and divine powers in a dynastic ritual setting; its precise relation to El and the dead remains debated.

- **Theophoric names:** Ugaritic persons bear names compounded with *il*, although some occurrences may mean “god” generically.

#### Hittite transmission

- **Elkunirša and Ašertu, CTH 342**  
  **Language/support/date:** fragmentary Hittite cuneiform tablets from Hattusa, copies generally assigned to the Hittite imperial period, especially the thirteenth century BCE. The narrative is widely judged to derive from a Northwest Semitic Vorlage because of its divine names and translated parallelism.  
  **Role:** Elkunirša is Ašertu’s husband. The Storm-god reports her attempted seduction; Elkunirša instructs him to humiliate her. Later Ašertu and Elkunirša plot against the Storm-god; a goddess written with the IŠTAR logogram warns him. The end is lost.  
  **Caution:** identification of the warning goddess as Anat, Astarte, or an Anat-Astarte compound is not secure.

#### Egyptian and Levantine comparanda

Egyptian magical and mythological texts of the New Kingdom preserve West Semitic gods and narratives, especially Baal, Anat, Astarte, Resheph, and Horon. El is much less securely identifiable in these texts. Claims that any elderly Egyptianized deity is El require an inscription or unusually specific context.

#### Iron Age Northwest Semitic inscriptions

- **Karatepe/Azatiwada bilingual, KAI 26, eighth century BCE:** Phoenician and Hieroglyphic Luwian monumental inscription from Cilicia. Its curse invokes major cosmic gods; related Northwest Semitic *qny ʾrṣ* language is important to “creator/possessor of earth” traditions, though individual restorations and equations require care.

- **Sefire treaties, mid-eighth century BCE:** Aramaic treaty inscriptions invoke *ʾl wʿlyn*. Scholars divide over “El and Elyon” as two gods versus “El-and-Most-High” or a coordinated epithet.

- **Arslan Tash amulets, usually seventh century BCE:** Phoenician/Aramaic incantatory plaques from Hadatu invoke divine collectives, Baal, Horon, and covenant/oath powers. The wording “sons of El” has been proposed in interpretation, but readings, authenticity questions, and the relation of *ʾl* to the proper god must be stated cautiously.

- **Phoenician and Punic inscriptions:** contain *El* in divine names, personal names, and titles, but a distinct public cult of El is much less visible than those of Baal Shamem, Melqart, Eshmun, Astarte, Tanit, or Baal Hammon.

#### Hebrew Bible

The Hebrew Bible is a composite corpus written and edited during the first millennium BCE, with manuscripts much later than many proposed composition dates. El-related evidence includes:

- Genesis 14:18–22: El Elyon, priest Melchizedek, creator/possessor of heaven and earth.
- Genesis 16:13: El Roi.
- Genesis 17:1 and patriarchal passages: El Shaddai.
- Genesis 21:33: El Olam at Beersheba.
- Genesis 28 and 35: Bethel traditions.
- Genesis 33:20: El Elohe Israel at Shechem.
- Genesis 49:24–25: poetic El/Shaddai titles alongside the “Mighty One of Jacob.”
- Exodus 6:2–3: explicit identification of the patriarchal El Shaddai with Yahweh.
- Numbers 23–24: Balaam poetry repeatedly uses El, Elyon, and Shaddai.
- Deuteronomy 32:8–9: a textual crux. Qumran witnesses and the Septuagint support “sons of God/El” or “angels of God,” whereas the Masoretic Text reads “sons of Israel.” In one historical interpretation, Elyon apportions nations among junior gods and Yahweh receives Israel; in the final monotheizing context, Elyon and Yahweh can be read as the same deity. The passage cannot establish with certainty that “Yahweh was literally El’s son” at every textual stage.
- Psalms 29, 74, 82, 89, and related poems: divine-council, kingship, creation, and combat language comparable to Ugaritic traditions. Psalm 82 calls the presiding deity *Elohim* in the assembly of El; later Jewish and Christian readings identify all supreme titles with one God.
- Isaiah 14:13: “mount of assembly” and “Most High” imagery has Ugaritic comparanda but is not a direct El narrative.

#### Philo of Byblos and Eusebius

Philo’s *Phoenician History* was written in Greek around the late first or early second century CE. It claimed to translate a primeval Phoenician author, Sanchuniathon. Only excerpts survive, principally in Eusebius’s early-fourth-century *Preparation for the Gospel*.

Its Elus/Kronos is son of Ouranos and Ge, overthrows and castrates his father, distributes cities and kingdoms, gives Athena rule over Attica, kills or sacrifices family members, and sacrifices an only son in wartime. The work contains genuine Northwest Semitic names and motifs but has passed through Phoenician, Hellenistic, euhemeristic, and Christian-polemical filters. It is a primary source for Roman-period Phoenician antiquarianism—not a verbatim Bronze Age scripture.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and powers

At Ugarit El’s securely attested domains are:

- kingship over the divine assembly;
- fatherhood and dynastic fertility;
- authorization of other gods’ rule;
- judgment and dispute resolution;
- wisdom, foresight, and dream revelation;
- bestowal of human offspring and healing agents;
- continuity, age, and ancestral legitimacy;
- convivial feasting and sexual generation.

El is not primarily the active rain-and-thunder god; that is Baal/Hadad. He is not securely the sun, moon, sea, or underworld god. He can authorize conflict but rarely fights in the surviving Ugaritic poems.

### Dwelling

The Baal Cycle places El at or toward:

> *mbk nhrm, qrb ʾapq thmtm*

commonly rendered “the source of the two rivers, amid the springs of the two deeps.” Messengers cross cosmic mountains to reach him. This is mythic geography. Attempts to locate the dwelling at a particular river source—Lebanon, the Euphrates headwaters, Mount Hermon, or elsewhere—are scholarly hypotheses.

He inhabits tents or pavilions rather than Baal’s newly constructed palace. This contrast has been read as ancestral pastoral kingship versus younger urban kingship, but the sociology is an interpretation, not an explicit Ugaritic explanation.

### Appearance

Texts describe El as old and bearded:

- he possesses a gray beard;
- he sits or reclines as patriarch and king;
- in KTU 1.114 he is physically vulnerable to drunkenness;
- KTU 1.23 presents him as sexually potent.

The tension between advanced age and fertility is part of the evidence and should not be harmonized away.

### Iconography

No surviving image bears an inscription saying unequivocally “El.” Identification is therefore probabilistic.

The standard proposed visual type is an elderly bearded male:

- seated on a throne;
- wearing a long garment;
- raising one hand in blessing, greeting, or audience;
- sometimes wearing a horned or Egyptianizing crown;
- receiving a worshiper or ruler.

The Metropolitan Museum’s Late Bronze Age Canaanite enthroned bronze-and-gold deity is described by the museum as **likely** El because seated senior gods contrast with dynamic “smiting gods,” usually associated with Baal. “Likely” must remain in the label.

The celebrated “El stele” from Ugarit shows a worshiper before an enthroned, bearded, horn-crowned deity. Its identification as El is influential but disputed; Guy Bunnens and others have challenged the certainty of the conventional label.

A gilded seated bronze from Ugarit wearing an Egyptianizing *atef*-like crown has also been called El. Its divine status is clear from type and regalia; the personal identity is not.

### Symbols and associations

- **Bull:** securely textual through “Bull El.” The bull connotes authority and procreative force.
- **Throne:** strong iconographic and functional association, though not exclusive to El.
- **Beard and old age:** securely textual; common in proposed images.
- **Cup/wine:** prominent in KTU 1.114 and banquet settings.
- **Rivers/deeps:** belong to his dwelling, not necessarily a domain of water.
- **Tent/pavilion:** recurrent residence.
- **Planet Saturn:** late interpretive consequence of the Kronos equation, not a demonstrated Ugaritic attribute.
- **Color, weekday, magical seal, tarot correspondence, crystal, incense recipe, or fixed number:** no Bronze Age source establishes the standardized correspondences found on modern occult websites.
- **Seventy:** Athirat’s “seventy sons” in KTU 1.4 VI is important, but “70 is El’s sacred number” is a modern abstraction rather than an attested cult rule.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical systems

#### Ugaritic system

El and Athirat are the royal parental pair. The pantheon is “the sons of El,” “the assembly of the gods,” or “the circle/family of El.” Athirat is mother of seventy sons in KTU 1.4 VI. Not every god called a “son of El” is thereby the biological offspring of one recorded sexual union; divine kinship also expresses rank and corporate membership.

Baal presents a special case. He belongs to El’s divine order and addresses El as father, but Ugaritic formulae also call Baal “son of Dagan.” The evidence supports overlapping theological kinship, not a single modern-style pedigree.

Shahar and Shalim are explicitly born from El’s union with two women in KTU 1.23. Other gods may be called his children collectively without surviving birth myths.

#### Hittite-West Semitic system

In CTH 342 Elkunirša is husband of Ašertu. No complete genealogy survives. The Storm-god is subordinate enough to address Elkunirša as father, but the tablet does not furnish a secure biological pedigree.

#### Philo’s Phoenician-Hellenistic system

Philo/Eusebius gives a radically different succession genealogy:

- Elyon and Berouth precede Ouranos and Ge.
- Ouranos and Ge beget Elus/Kronos, Baetylus, Dagon/Siton, and Atlas.
- Elus/Kronos marries or associates with Astarte, Rhea, and Dione.
- He fathers Persephone and Athena in the Greek rendering and sacrifices an only-begotten son by Anobret.
- He fights and mutilates Ouranos.

This is Roman-period learned systematization containing Phoenician material, Greek genealogical structure, and euhemerism. It is not a missing tablet of the Ugaritic canon.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Baal Cycle

**Early attested form:** KTU 1.1–1.6, thirteenth-century BCE Ugaritic tablets.

The cycle opens amid a contest over divine kingship. Yamm, Sea/River, is favored by El and receives royal titles including “Beloved of El.” Kothar-wa-Hasis is directed to construct a palace for Yamm. Yamm’s messengers appear before the assembled gods and demand the surrender of Baal. The gods lower their heads; Baal rebukes them. El is willing to deliver Baal, but Baal battles Yamm with Kothar’s weapons and defeats him.

Baal remains without a palace, the architectural sign of recognized kingship. Anat advocates violently for him; Athirat initially fears a renewed attack. Baal and Anat honor Athirat and send gifts made by Kothar. Athirat travels to El’s waterside dwelling. El responds to her appearance with sexual humor, then receives her petition. He authorizes a palace for Baal. Kothar builds it on Mount Ṣapanu.

After attaining his house, Baal confronts Mot, Death. Baal descends or is swallowed; messengers report his death. El comes down from his throne, sits in dust, cuts himself, and laments: “Baal is dead—what becomes of the people?” Athirat proposes Athtar as replacement. Athtar sits on Baal’s throne but is too small for it and relinquishes the position.

Anat searches for Baal, attacks Mot, and scatters him like grain. El later dreams that the heavens rain oil and wadis run with honey, proof that Baal lives. Baal and Mot renew their conflict; Shapash finally warns Mot of El’s support for Baal. Mot submits.

**El’s narrative function:** He is neither simply the defeated “old god” nor the unchallenged mover of every event. His authorization remains constitutionally decisive, but his original candidate Yamm loses, Baal’s advocates pressure him, and the active younger gods determine events by combat.

**Development:** Parallels with Mesopotamian, Hurro-Hittite, Egyptian, and biblical combat myths show shared ancient Near Eastern narrative structures, but no surviving source permits reconstruction of one original version. The poem’s relation to an annual seasonal ritual is debated; a rigid “dying-and-rising vegetation god” script is no longer a safe default.

### Kirta

Kirta loses wives and children and is left without an heir. He performs a ritual and sleeps; El appears in a dream. El asks why he weeps and rejects the assumption that the king wants wealth. Kirta wants offspring. El prescribes a campaign to Udum, where Kirta is to demand King Pabil’s daughter Hurriya rather than treasure. Kirta follows the revelation, marries Hurriya, and receives children through El’s blessing.

Kirta later falls gravely ill, apparently after violating a vow to Athirat. His son wonders whether a king, “son of El,” can die like an ordinary person. El assembles the gods and repeatedly asks who can remove the sickness. When none responds, El creates Šaʿtiqatu, who defeats or draws out the disease. Kirta recovers, but the epic’s damaged final tablet presents a succession conflict with his son Yassib.

This cycle documents El as dream-giver, guarantor of dynasty, and healer by creation or magical craftsmanship. It does not establish that Ugaritic kings were universally believed to be biologically divine.

### Aqhat

The righteous Danel performs rites for a son. Baal intercedes or calls attention to his lack; El blesses Danel, promising Aqhat. When Aqhat grows, Kothar gives him a marvelous bow. Anat desires it. She offers wealth and immortality; Aqhat refuses and insults her capacity to use a warrior’s bow.

Anat complains to El and threatens cosmic violence. El recognizes her intention and permits action while warning that she will bear responsibility. Anat recruits Yatpan, who kills Aqhat, but the bow is lost. Drought follows, Danel curses the places connected with the death, and Aqhat’s sister Pughat begins revenge. The preserved text breaks off.

El’s part is morally and grammatically difficult: he does not kill Aqhat, but his authorization enables Anat. Translations that turn his answer into unambiguous approval or total refusal exceed the damaged, idiomatic evidence.

### Birth of Shahar and Shalim

KTU 1.23 begins with ritual instructions and an invocation of gracious gods. El goes to the seashore, where two women crouch or bob. He roasts or hunts a bird and engages them in a repeated exchange testing whether they will call him father or husband. They choose husband; El’s penis lengthens “like the sea,” and he mates with them. After gestation, they bear Shahar and Shalim. The offspring have enormous appetites and are sent into the steppe.

The text is often called a fertility myth, sacred-marriage rite, or ritual drama. None of those genre labels is certain. It securely establishes El’s sexual generation of Dawn and Dusk; it does not securely identify both mothers or prove that worshipers reenacted intercourse.

### Drunken El

In KTU 1.114 El invites gods to a feast. Yarih and possibly other deities behave like dogs beneath the tables. El drinks heavily, staggers, and is supported by Thukamuna-and-Shunama. A creature with horns and tail confronts him; El falls in his bodily waste. Athirat and Anat seek or administer a remedy involving plant and animal products.

The tablet may combine myth, humor, cautionary tale, and medical incantation. It is direct evidence that divine dignity and comic degradation could coexist in Ugaritic religious literature.

### Elkunirša and Ašertu

Ašertu propositions the Storm-god. He refuses and reports her to her husband Elkunirša, addressing him as father. Elkunirša tells him to humble her. The Storm-god tells Ašertu that he has killed seventy-seven or eighty-eight of her children; she mourns. Later she and Elkunirša plot against him. A goddess written IŠTAR overhears and warns the Storm-god. The ending is lost, and an attached ritual indicates that the myth could be deployed in healing or purification.

This story is a regional transmitted variant, not part of the extant Ugaritic Baal Cycle. Its hostile or compromised El differs from simplified portraits of the universally benevolent patriarch.

### Philo’s succession myth

Ouranos repeatedly attempts to destroy his children. Elus/Kronos opposes him, rules with allies, ambushes and castrates him, and distributes territories. Astarte participates in his dynasty. Kronos sacrifices an only son in a national emergency and is said to circumcise himself and compel allies to do likewise.

The resemblance to Hesiod’s Ouranos–Kronos succession is obvious; so are West Semitic names and child-sacrifice traditions. The surviving form is a Hellenistic/Roman Phoenician intellectual construction. It cannot be silently inserted before the Baal Cycle as El’s “original childhood.”

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ugarit

El’s cult is securely attested by offering lists, pantheon lists, myths preserved in priestly or palace-associated archives, theophoric names, and probably cult images.

Ugarit’s excavated acropolis contained large temples conventionally assigned to Baal and Dagan. No comparably secure monumental building bears an ancient label “Temple of El.” Claims that a particular sanctuary belonged to El depend on artifact interpretation and early excavation arguments. Claude Schaeffer argued for archaeological evidence of an El cult, but later iconographic scholarship is more cautious.

Ritual texts prescribe offerings to El among broader sequences:

- cattle, sheep, or other sacrificial animals;
- wine and libations;
- offerings associated with calendrical rites;
- royal participation in sacrifices to multiple gods;
- divine-list order that places El at or near the head.

The corpus establishes formal worship but does not permit a complete daily liturgy, theology of sacrifice, or priestly constitution devoted exclusively to El.

### Priesthood and offices

Ugarit had priests, diviners, lamentation specialists, singers, scribes, and royal ritual personnel. Tablets from the House of the High Priest show elite custody of myth and ritual literature. No surviving evidence supports a unique hereditary “Order of El” with a reconstructed initiation system.

### Festivals

El appears within multi-deity sacrificial calendars and banquets. It is hazardous to assign every New Year, vintage, royal accession, or funerary ceremony specifically to him. KTU 1.41/1.87 records elaborate rites often associated with the vintage or calendrical transition; El receives an offering within the sequence.

### Domestic and ordinary worship

Theophoric personal names and small divine images suggest that devotion was not confined to literary myth. Yet an uninscribed household figurine cannot normally be assigned to El. Female figurines, standing smiting gods, and seated gods belonged to a varied religious material culture.

### Divination, dreams, and healing

Kirta portrays El communicating by dream; this demonstrates a literary conception compatible with ancient divinatory practice but is not itself an administrative dream report. KTU 1.114’s remedy and CTH 342’s attached ritual demonstrate reuse of divine narrative in healing or purification.

### Human sacrifice

No surviving Ugaritic ritual text securely prescribes human sacrifice to El. Philo’s Roman-period Phoenician account says Elus/Kronos sacrificed an only-begotten son in wartime. This is evidence that such a tradition circulated in Philo’s milieu; it is not sufficient by itself to prove a standard Bronze Age Ugaritic rite.

Biblical narratives of child sacrifice, the *mlk*/*molk* problem, and Punic tophets belong to related but distinct evidentiary complexes. Equating every victim of Levantine child sacrifice with an offering “to El/Moloch/Kronos” collapses differences of period, language, and deity. Conversely, Philo’s testimony should not be erased merely because it is late.

### “Temple prostitution”

No secure Ugaritic text documents an institution of “sacred prostitution” dedicated to El. Older scholarship often inferred such institutions from ambiguous words such as Hebrew *qĕdēšâ*, Greek and Roman polemic, or generalized fertility theories. Sexual imagery in KTU 1.23 is mythic or ritual-literary evidence, not documentation that temple personnel sold sex. The claim must be marked **unestablished**.

### Cult violence and politics

The myths normalize divine threats, mutilation, and lethal conflict, while kingship narratives use El’s blessing to legitimate dynasty. That is political theology. There is no documentary basis for a separate historical campaign in which “priests of El” violently imposed his cult across Canaan.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before approximately 1500 BCE: linguistic and onomastic prehistory

The Semitic divine noun *ʾil-* is ancient. Eblaite and Amorite names show its productivity, but whether they invoke a single individualized El remains contested. Dagan, Hadad, Ishtar, and city gods are often more institutionally visible in early Syrian archives.

### Approximately 1400–1190 BCE: Ugaritic florescence

This is the peak of El’s **documented literary visibility**, not necessarily the beginning or numerical peak of his worship. Ugaritic scribes recorded a coherent divine patriarch who heads lists and narratives but shares effective power with Baal. El’s high rank and Baal’s dynamic royal power coexist.

Ugarit’s destruction around 1190 BCE ended the archival context that preserved the poems. There is no evidence that every oral or ritual tradition immediately vanished.

### Hittite imperial period

Northwest Semitic narrative crossed into Anatolia. CTH 342 translated or adapted El as Elkunirša, demonstrating that his title “creator/possessor of Earth” and his marriage to Ašertu could circulate outside Ugarit.

### Iron Age Phoenicia, Aram, Israel, and Judah

The proper god El becomes harder to isolate because *ʾel* is also the ordinary word “god.” Phoenician and Aramaic titles preserve El-language, but other named gods dominate inscriptions.

In Israel and Judah, Yahweh absorbed or was identified with El’s titles, council, fatherhood, creator status, and senior kingship. This was not a single documented decree. The evidence consists of:

- El in the name *Israel*;
- El-titles attached to ancestral shrines;
- poetic divine-council language;
- Yahweh addressed as El/Elohim/Elyon;
- Exodus 6’s explicit retrospective equation;
- later denunciation or subordination of other gods.

Some reconstructions propose that early Israelites first worshiped El and later introduced Yahweh from the south; others emphasize that the surviving evidence cannot resolve the process so neatly. Deuteronomy 32:8–9 is important but textually unstable.

### Persian and Hellenistic periods

Jewish monotheistic interpretation increasingly made *El*, *Elohim*, *Elyon*, and *Shaddai* titles of the one God. Lesser members of the old divine assembly were reclassified as angels, heavenly hosts, demons, or nonexistent gods.

Phoenician elites translated their gods into Greek categories. El’s closest explicit learned counterpart became Kronos. This comparison probably drew on age, senior kingship, and succession traditions rather than a one-to-one cultic identity.

### Roman period

Philo of Byblos presented Elus/Kronos in a euhemerizing national history. Phoenician and Punic cults continued under Greco-Roman names, but it is not possible to demonstrate an unchanged independent cult of Ugaritic El. Roman Saturn in North Africa inherited Punic associations, especially through Baal Hammon; direct descent from El remains a historical hypothesis.

### Christianization and Islamization

Christian and later Islamic dominance ended or transformed public polytheistic institutions in the Levant through legislation, conversion, temple closure, reuse, local negotiation, and sometimes violence. There is no documented continuous El priesthood surviving these changes.

The word-family did survive:

- Jewish, Christian, and biblical usage of *El* and *Elohim*;
- theophoric names such as Israel, Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Daniel, and Elijah;
- Syriac and Arabic cognate vocabulary;
- scriptural titles translated as “God.”

Linguistic survival is not the same as continuous worship of El as Athirat’s husband in the Ugaritic pantheon.

### Rediscovery and the present

Excavations beginning in 1929 transformed El from a figure reconstructed mainly from the Bible, classical fragments, and comparative Semitics into a directly readable Late Bronze Age literary deity.

Modern Canaanite reconstructionist and revivalist groups began forming especially in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Ethnographic research documents small Israeli communities drawing on Ugaritic texts, Hebrew culture, Wicca, and modern Pagan networks. This is genuine contemporary religion, but its liturgical details are necessarily modern reconstructions because no complete ancient ritual manual survives.

No reliable census isolates present-day worshipers of El. Claims of an unbroken secret lineage from Ugarit are undocumented.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship-driven rediscovery

Before Ras Shamra, El was known through biblical philology and Philo. Charles Virolleaud’s publication of tablets in the 1930s, followed by grammars, dictionaries, KTU concordances, and works by Pope, Cross, Smith, Pardee, Wyatt, del Olmo Lete, Sanmartín, Rahmouni, and others established the modern profile.

The now-familiar portrait—aged creator, husband of Asherah, father of seventy gods, dwelling at the rivers’ source—is chiefly a twentieth-century synthesis of separate Ugaritic passages.

### Occult and esoteric reception

Unlike Baal/Bael, Astaroth, Belial, and Beelzebub, El did not develop a stable demonological identity in medieval or early-modern grimoires. Medieval Jewish and Christian magic more commonly used *El* as a divine name or element in angelic names.

Modern ceremonial-magic lists sometimes assign El:

- Saturn;
- Thursday or Saturday;
- bulls;
- white, gold, or black;
- particular incense, stones, sigils, or tarot cards.

Unless tied to a documented modern author, these are recent correspondence systems. Saturn derives from the late El–Kronos equation, not a Ugaritic ritual calendar. Internet “sigils of El” are modern inventions unless copied from an explicitly modern occult source; no ancient Ugaritic sigil of El is known.

### Canaanite revival and Pagan reconstruction

Modern practitioners use names such as Canaanite Reconstructionism, Natib Qadish, or *ʾŌraḥ Qaḏmōnī*. Tess Dawson’s *Whisper of Stone: Natib Qadish* is influential in Anglophone practice. Contemporary rites combine translated Ugaritic hymnic language, reconstructed offerings, modern ethical and communal forms, and sometimes Wiccan or broader Pagan conventions.

Marianna Ruah-Midbar Shapiro and Shai Feraro’s study of Israeli Canaanite reconstructionism documents a modern movement negotiating land, Hebrew identity, archaeology, and Pagan globalization. It does not establish cultural continuity from Bronze Age worship.

### Israeli Canaanism and politics

The twentieth-century “Canaanite” movement associated with Yonatan Ratosh was principally a secular literary-nationalist project seeking a Hebrew territorial identity distinct from diaspora Judaism. Its use of Canaanite myth materially affected Israeli literature and art but should not be confused with organized worship of El.

Later religious Canaanite revivalists may adopt or reject nationalist interpretations. No single political program represents them. Claims that modern worship of El is inherently Zionist, anti-Zionist, extremist, or ethnonationalist are unsupported without reference to a particular organization or text.

### Literature, games, and entertainment

El appears less often as an individualized character than Baal, Anat, or Mot. Modern fiction and games frequently:

- use “El” as a generic ancient-god name;
- identify him directly with the biblical God;
- turn him into a dethroned father of Baal;
- give him Saturnian magic;
- portray a unified “Canaanite pantheon” assembled from sources separated by more than a millennium.

These are legitimate creative adaptations, but not ancient tradition. Role-playing supplements that provide alignment, armor, spell domains, numerical statistics, or a fixed holy symbol are modern inventions.

### Popular misinformation

The following recurrent claims are not established:

- “El is definitely attested as supreme god at Ebla”: the *il* evidence is ambiguous.
- “The Ugaritic tablets say Yahweh was one of El’s seventy sons”: no secure Ugaritic occurrence of Yahweh does so.
- “El’s wife was always Asherah throughout the Near East”: secure at Ugarit as Athirat and reflected in Elkunirša; not universal.
- “Baal overthrew El”: no surviving Ugaritic text narrates this.
- “El was the Canaanite Saturn”: this is a later Greek-Roman equation.
- “The seated Ugaritic stele is certainly a portrait of El”: probable or possible, not certain.
- “El demanded infant sacrifice at Ugarit”: not in the extant Ugaritic ritual corpus.
- “Sacred prostitutes served El”: unestablished.
- “Modern Abrahamic worship is simply continuous worship of the same Canaanite god”: a theological or polemical compression, not a sufficiently precise historical statement.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major discoveries and editions

The decisive event was Claude Schaeffer’s excavation of Ras Shamra/Ugarit beginning in 1929. Charles Virolleaud published major mythic tablets, including the death-of-Baal material. The alphabetic cuneiform script was rapidly deciphered through the work of several scholars.

Important reference systems include:

- **CTA**, Andrée Herdner’s *Corpus des tablettes en cunéiformes alphabétiques*;
- **KTU**, Manfried Dietrich, Oswald Loretz, and Joaquín Sanmartín, *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit*;
- **CAT**, the English-titled edition of the same corpus;
- Dennis Pardee’s ritual editions;
- Mark S. Smith and Wayne Pitard’s multi-volume Baal Cycle commentary;
- del Olmo Lete and Sanmartín’s Ugaritic dictionary;
- the Göttingen digital edition of Ugaritic poetic texts.

Tablets are divided principally among the Louvre and the National Museum of Damascus, with other pieces in collections including the British Museum and institutional holdings. War in Syria has restricted access and endangered archaeological heritage; exact present condition must be checked object by object.

### Pope and the classic reconstruction

Marvin H. Pope’s 1955 *El in the Ugaritic Texts* remains foundational for the name, epithets, dwelling, status, and comparative evidence. Some etymologies and identifications have since been revised, but the work defined the research agenda.

### El versus generic *il*

This is the central philological problem. In myth, “Bull El,” personal actions, and the divine court make the proper name clear. In personal names, short inscriptions, and god lists, the same consonants may mean:

- El;
- “a god”;
- “the god”;
- “my god” by implication;
- a generic divine determinative or category.

Historical claims must follow each occurrence’s grammar rather than capitalizing automatically.

### Was there a cult of El?

Mythic prominence does not automatically prove a major independent temple cult. Offering lists demonstrate worship, but El lacks a securely identified monumental Ugaritic temple comparable to the conventional Baal and Dagan temples. David Toshio Tsumura’s deliberately provocative question, “Was There a Cult of El in Ancient Canaan?”, reflects disputes over whether particular *il* occurrences designate El and how myth, ritual, and archaeology correlate.

The most defensible answer is: **El received cult at Ugarit; the institutional scale, dedicated sanctuary, and wider geographic uniformity are not fully reconstructable.**

### El and Baal: succession or complementary kingship?

Older evolutionary accounts often imagined peaceful old El being replaced by vigorous young Baal. The Baal Cycle shows tension, lobbying, and competing candidates, but El remains king and his authorization matters. Baal gains active kingship without narratively castrating, killing, or deposing El.

Philo’s later El/Kronos does overthrow his own father Ouranos. Combining that story with the Ugaritic cycle produces a modern composite unsupported by either source.

### El and Yahweh

Frank Moore Cross emphasized continuity between Canaanite El and Israel’s ancestral god; Mark S. Smith described convergence and differentiation among Yahweh, El, and Baal; John Day stressed the absorption of El’s features into Yahweh. Debate concerns:

- whether El was Israel’s original patron;
- whether Yahweh entered from Edom/Midian/Seir;
- when the identification occurred;
- whether Deuteronomy 32 once distinguished Elyon and Yahweh;
- how much biblical El-language is inherited cultic memory versus ordinary Hebrew vocabulary;
- whether Asherah’s association with Yahweh followed an earlier El–Athirat pairing.

The broad conclusion—that biblical Yahweh bears titles and structures associated with El—is stronger than any exact reconstruction of stages.

### Deuteronomy 32:8–9

Qumran’s “sons of God/El” reading is widely judged earlier than the Masoretic “sons of Israel.” Even so, two reconstructions remain possible:

1. Elyon/El apportions nations to subordinate gods, with Yahweh receiving Israel.
2. Yahweh-Elyon apportions nations and retains Israel as his own special portion.

Grammar, textual history, and the poem’s redaction do not compel unanimity. Presenting the passage as a transparent family tree is misleading.

### The “seventy sons”

KTU 1.4 VI refers to Athirat’s seventy sons. Deuteronomy’s table of nations traditionally numbers seventy, encouraging comparison. The connection is plausible and influential, but the texts do not explicitly cite one another.

### Philo/Sanchuniathon

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars ranged from accepting Sanchuniathon as a direct Bronze Age witness to dismissing him as Philo’s fiction. Ugaritic discoveries vindicated the presence of genuine West Semitic material—El, Baal, Dagan, Athirat/Astarte-type relations—but did not validate Philo’s claimed primeval date.

Current responsible use treats the fragments as layered:

- some inherited Phoenician tradition;
- Greek translation and equation;
- Philo’s Roman-period antiquarian and euhemeristic program;
- Eusebius’s selective Christian quotation.

### Iconographic circularity

Scholars have often identified a seated figure as El because texts call El senior, then used the figure to prove El’s appearance. Recent iconographic work emphasizes posture, crown, gesture, archaeological context, and regional visual types while retaining uncertainty. “Enthroned senior deity, plausibly El” is usually more accurate than “portrait of El.”

### Discredited or weak theories

- A universal annual ritual in which Baal literally dies every summer and rises every autumn is overly schematic.
- “Temple prostitution” as a standard Canaanite fertility institution is not established.
- Pan-Babylonian derivations that identify every Semitic god with one Mesopotamian original ignore local evidence.
- Alexander Hislop’s *The Two Babylons* and descendants manufacture chains linking Nimrod, Kronos, El, Saturn, Moloch, and numerous later figures through unsound etymology.
- Claims that *El*, Arabic *Allah*, and a supposed pre-Islamic “moon god” are one continuously worshiped lunar deity are linguistically and historically false.
- Viral assertions that an uninscribed Ugaritic image depicts “Yahweh and Asherah” or El with certainty generally exceed the object’s evidence.
