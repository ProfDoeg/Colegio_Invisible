# The Dvergatal/Tolkien dwarves (Thorin, Dwalin, Fíli, Kíli, Dori, Nori, Ori, Óin, Glóin, Bifur, Bofur, Bombur): Pantheon Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary finding

This subject is not a divine being attested in Norse religion. It is a modern bundle comprising thirteen fictional dwarves in J. R. R. Tolkien’s *The Hobbit*, twelve of whose names—and the name Thorin—were adapted from dwarf-names in the Old Norse *Dvergatal*, the “tally of dwarfs” embedded in *Vǫluspá*. The Norse names do not form a thirteen-member company, possess the biographies Tolkien gave them, or receive collective worship in the medieval evidence. Conversely, Tolkien’s characters are mortal incarnate beings within his invented world, not gods.

Three evidentiary objects must therefore remain separate:

1. Individual names in medieval Old Norse manuscript traditions.
2. Tolkien’s twentieth-century fictional characters.
3. Subsequent illustrations, adaptations, fandom, merchandising, and occasional modern religious or esoteric reinterpretation.

The coincidence of name does not establish identity. A myth involving an Old Norse Dvalinn, for example, cannot automatically be assigned to Tolkien’s Dwalin, or even necessarily to the otherwise contextless Dvalinn in every medieval list. Medieval name reuse and homonymy prevent that inference.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### 1.1 Classification

**Old Norse evidence:** The names occur as names of *dvergar*, dwarfs, a class of nonhuman beings. In *Vǫluspá* 9–16, dwarfs emerge within a cosmogonic account and make anthropomorphic forms. Snorri Sturluson’s *Gylfaginning* 14 systematizes dwarfs as beings arising like maggots in the flesh and blood of the primordial giant Ymir before receiving intelligence and human form from the gods. This Snorrian synthesis, recorded in the thirteenth century, must not simply be projected backward as a uniform pre-Christian doctrine.

**Tolkien’s evidence:** The thirteen are male Dwarves, members of Thorin Oakenshield’s expedition to recover Erebor from Smaug. They are Children of Aulë by adoption in Tolkien’s developed mythology: Aulë makes the first Dwarves, but Ilúvatar gives them independent life. They are neither Valar nor Maiar and receive no worship in Tolkien’s fiction.

### 1.2 Name correspondences

| Tolkien form | Principal Old Norse form | Location and comments |
|---|---|---|
| Thorin | **Þorinn** | *Vǫluspá*’s dwarf-list; conventionally connected with *þora*, “dare,” hence “bold/daring one,” though dwarf-name etymologies are frequently less certain than popular glossaries imply. |
| Dwalin | **Dvalinn** | In the *Dvergatal* and several other Old Norse works. Usually related to *dvala/dvelja*, delay, torpor, or sleep: “delayer,” “dormant one,” or “one in a trance.” |
| Fíli | **Fíli** | *Dvergatal*. Etymology disputed; proposals connecting it with “file” or a filer are possible but not secure. |
| Kíli | **Kíli** | *Dvergatal*. Often associated with a wedge or wedge-shaped implement, but certainty is unwarranted. |
| Dori | **Dóri** | *Dvergatal*. Etymology uncertain. Tolkien normally omitted the Old Norse accent in *The Hobbit*. |
| Nori | **Nóri** | *Dvergatal*. Sometimes interpreted as “little fragment” or connected with narrowness, but disputed. |
| Ori | **Óri** | *Dvergatal*. Meaning uncertain; glosses involving frenzy or agitation are conjectural. |
| Óin | **Óinn** | *Dvergatal*. The Tolkien spelling removes final Old Norse *-nn* while retaining a long vowel. The name’s meaning is uncertain. |
| Glóin | **Glóinn** | *Dvergatal*. Transparently associated with *glóa*, “glow,” hence “glowing one.” |
| Bifur | **Bífurr/Bivurr** | Manuscript and editorial spellings vary. Proposed senses such as “shaker” remain uncertain. |
| Bofur | **Báfurr/Bafurr** | Tolkien substantially anglicized the vowel. Etymology uncertain. |
| Bombur | **Bǫmburr/Bömburr** | Frequently connected with *bumba*, “drum,” and secondarily a paunch or swollen form. Tolkien’s notably fat Bombur may exploit this semantic association, but no surviving statement by Tolkien proves that this characterization was generated by the etymology. |
| Thorin Oakenshield | **Þorinn + Eikinskjaldi** | Tolkien joined two separate *Dvergatal* names. *Eikinskjaldi* means approximately “oak-shield” or “one with an oaken shield.” In Tolkien’s later narrative, “Oakenshield” becomes an earned byname arising from Thorin’s use of an oak branch as a shield at Azanulbizar. |

Old Norse spelling varies between manuscripts and modern normalization. Forms such as *Bǫmburr* versus *Bömburr* can reflect editorial orthography rather than distinct ancient personalities.

### 1.3 Tolkien’s internal linguistic explanation

When *The Hobbit* was written, the Norse-derived names were primarily borrowed literary material. In the developed linguistic framework of *The Lord of the Rings*, Tolkien retroactively explained them as **translated outer names**. Dwarves guarded their private Khuzdul names and did not reveal them to outsiders, even placing translated public names on tombs. The northern human language of Dale is represented for readers by Old Norse, just as Westron is represented by modern English.

Consequently, “Thorin,” “Dwalin,” and the rest are not their recoverable Khuzdul birth-names. Tolkien never supplies the company’s secret inner names. Claims circulating online that reconstruct Khuzdul originals are modern inventions.

### 1.4 Titles and descriptive epithets

Only Thorin has a stable personal epithet:

- **Oakenshield**: attached to Thorin in the published *Hobbit* (1937) and historicized in *The Lord of the Rings*, Appendix A (1955).
- **King under the Mountain**: Thorin’s claimed and briefly recovered royal dignity in *The Hobbit*.
- **King of Durin’s Folk in exile**: a retrospective dynastic description supported by Appendix A.

“The Company of Thorin Oakenshield,” “Thorin and Company,” and “the Quest of Erebor” are collective literary designations, not cult titles.

### 1.5 No interpretatio or divine equation

No medieval source equates this thirteen-member set with gods under *interpretatio romana*, Christian demonology, or another religious system. No evidence identifies Þorinn with Thor merely because both names contain *Þor-*; the resemblance does not make Thorin a form of the god Þórr.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 Composition versus surviving material support

The dwarf-list survives as part of *Vǫluspá*, an anonymous Old Norse eddic poem usually dated, with substantial disagreement, to around the late tenth or early eleventh century. The poem’s composition is centuries earlier than its principal surviving manuscripts. Its “date” is consequently philological, not the date of a contemporary inscription.

The principal material witnesses are:

- **Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda**, Reykjavík, Árni Magnússon Institute, GKS 2365 4to, written in Iceland about **1270**. It is the chief witness to the eddic corpus.
- **Hauksbók**, principally Reykjavík, AM 544 4to, compiled in Iceland in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, containing an independent and textually different *Vǫluspá*.
- **Snorra Edda manuscripts**, preserving quotations and an adapted dwarf-list in *Gylfaginning*. Snorri’s work was composed around **1220**, although its principal manuscripts are later thirteenth- and fourteenth-century copies.

The earliest securely datable physical evidence for the relevant bundle is therefore thirteenth-century Icelandic parchment, not a Viking Age inscription, idol, temple object, or image.

### 2.2 What the earliest source says

In *Vǫluspá* 9–16, the gods deliberate over the making or ordering of dwarfs. Mótsognir and Durinn receive prominence. A catalogue follows, containing the names later used by Tolkien. The list concludes with a movement or lineage “from the halls of stone” toward Jöruvellir and with the “line of Lofarr.”

The poem does **not** say that Tolkien’s thirteen names constitute one expedition or family. It gives them no adventures resembling *The Hobbit*, no clothing colors, no dragon, no hobbit companion, and no leader named Thorin Oakenshield. *Þorinn* and *Eikinskjaldi* are separate entries.

All extant major versions contain dwarf-catalogue material, but wording, order, case forms, and surrounding syntax differ. Scholars have long debated whether parts of the catalogue are secondary expansion. Its awkward connection with the creation of the first humans in the following stanzas encouraged interpolation theories. Universal manuscript presence nevertheless means that it belonged to the poem’s recoverable medieval transmission; calling it a late insertion into a modern edition would be false.

### 2.3 Other medieval appearances of names

Most of the relevant names remain bare catalogue entries. **Dvalinn** is exceptional because that name occurs widely:

- *Hávamál* 143 associates Dvalinn with runes for dwarfs.
- *Alvíssmál* includes “Dvalinn’s playmate” as a dwarfish expression for the sun in some interpretations.
- *Fáfnismál* 13 names Dvalinn in connection with one lineage or group of Norns.
- *Grímnismál* gives Dáin and Dvalinn as stag names, illustrating the mobility of mythological names.
- The late medieval *Sörla þáttr* makes a dwarf Dvalinn one of four craftsmen who create a necklace obtained by Freyja after she sleeps with each maker. This text survives in the *Flateyjarbók* milieu and represents late Christian Icelandic narrative, not unmediated Viking Age cult testimony.
- Dvalinn also occurs in dwarf-lists and poetic circumlocutions.

These attestations demonstrate a name-tradition, not necessarily a continuous biography of one personage. None securely connects the narrative Dvalinn with Tolkien’s Dwalin.

### 2.4 Tolkien corpus

#### Early drafts, circa 1930–1933

John D. Rateliff’s edition of the manuscripts shows that the company was present from the earliest phase, but its leader was first called **Gandalf**, while the wizard was **Bladorthin**. Tolkien later transferred *Gandalf*—another *Dvergatal* name—to the wizard and renamed the dwarf-leader Thorin. This is direct evidence of literary selection and reassignment rather than recovery of a fixed traditional group.

The principal manuscripts are held in the J. R. R. Tolkien Collection at Marquette University. Rateliff’s *The History of The Hobbit* (2007; revised one-volume edition 2011) publishes and analyzes the draft phases, later revisions, maps, and abandoned 1960 recasting.

#### *The Hobbit* (21 September 1937)

This is the first public attestation of the thirteen as a company. Its evidence establishes:

- Thorin as leader and dispossessed heir of Erebor;
- Dwalin, Balin, Fíli, Kíli, Dori, Nori, Ori, Óin, Glóin, Bifur, Bofur, and Bombur as his twelve Dwarf companions;
- the expedition from Bag End to the Lonely Mountain;
- Smaug’s death, the dispute over the treasure, the Battle of Five Armies, and the deaths of Thorin, Fíli, and Kíli.

The 1951 second edition substantially rewrote “Riddles in the Dark” to align the Ring with *The Lord of the Rings*. The company’s central identities did not undergo a comparable replacement, but the revision transformed the larger metaphysical context around their quest. A 1960 attempt to rewrite the opening in the more sober style of *The Lord of the Rings* was abandoned.

#### *The Lord of the Rings* (1954–1955)

The main narrative includes Glóin at the Council of Elrond and his son Gimli as a member of the Fellowship. Appendix A, especially “Durin’s Folk,” supplies the principal dynastic history of Thorin, Dwalin, Fíli, Kíli, Óin, and Glóin. Appendix B supplies later chronology, including Balin’s Moria expedition and the deaths of Óin and Ori.

#### “The Quest of Erebor”

Written in the 1950s and published posthumously in *Unfinished Tales* (1980), this narrative retells the expedition’s formation largely through Gandalf’s retrospective account. It explains his manipulation of Thorin and Bilbo into a venture intended to remove Smaug as a strategic threat. It is a later authorial elaboration, not evidence for the book’s original 1930–1933 conception.

#### *The Silmarillion* (1977)

“Of Aulë and Yavanna” supplies Tolkien’s mature account of Dwarf origins. Aulë makes the Seven Fathers before the awakening of Elves and Men; Ilúvatar confronts him, accepts his repentance, gives the Dwarves true life, and requires that they sleep until after the Elves awaken. This text classifies the species but does not narrate the lives of Thorin’s company.

### 2.5 Archival state

Marquette holds the principal manuscripts and working drafts of *The Hobbit* and *The Lord of the Rings*. The Bodleian Libraries hold many other Tolkien papers and artworks. Access is regulated, and the Tolkien Estate identifies both institutions as the relevant scholarly archives. The manuscripts are modern paper documents with authorial revisions, not anonymous oral-traditional witnesses.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### 3.1 Norse domain

The *Dvergatal* names as a group have no separately documented domain. Old Norse dwarfs more broadly are associated in literary sources with:

- earth, rock, and enclosed subterranean spaces;
- metalwork and extraordinary crafted objects;
- wisdom, names, poetry, runes, and concealed knowledge;
- vulnerability to daylight in some narratives;
- ambiguous relations with gods, giants, and humans.

Those generic attributes cannot automatically be distributed to every bare name in *Vǫluspá*. The catalogue itself does not say that Þorinn forges weapons, that Bífurr lives underground, or that Bǫmburr has a sacred function.

### 3.2 Tolkienian attributes

The company’s collective domain is dynastic recovery: reclaiming the Lonely Mountain, its treasure, and Thorin’s kingship. Their equipment includes cloaks, hoods, swords, axes, mining tools, instruments, packs, ropes, and the map and key inherited from Thrór.

Chapter 1 of *The Hobbit* distinguishes them by hood colors and musical instruments, but Tolkien gives only a few individualized physical portraits:

- **Thorin:** proud, formal, politically authoritative, increasingly possessive of the treasure; wields Orcrist after the troll-hoard discovery.
- **Dwalin:** blue beard tucked into a golden belt, dark-green hood; among the strongest and most senior expedition members.
- **Fíli and Kíli:** the youngest, keen-eyed, energetic scouts; identifiable by blue hoods and yellow beards in the text.
- **Dori:** notably strong and repeatedly carries Bilbo.
- **Nori:** minimally individualized.
- **Ori:** minimally individualized in *The Hobbit*; later recognizable through the written record in Moria.
- **Óin:** associated with fire-making and later with Balin’s colony.
- **Glóin:** also associated with fire-making; later spokesman at Rivendell and father of Gimli.
- **Bifur:** minimally individualized in Tolkien’s text.
- **Bofur:** minimally individualized beyond family relationship and episodic action.
- **Bombur:** extraordinarily heavy; his fall into the enchanted stream in Mirkwood produces prolonged magical sleep. Later reports make him so fat that several young Dwarves are needed to lift him.

The heavy comic differentiation of every member familiar from Peter Jackson’s films is substantially a twenty-first-century adaptation strategy, not Tolkien’s characterization.

### 3.3 Earliest images

Tolkien’s own 1930s sketches, maps, dust jacket, and published illustrations form the earliest visual stratum. The first 1937 printing contained eleven monochrome drawings and maps plus a halftone image; four color plates were added to the second printing. His pictures concentrate on places—Bag End, Rivendell, the mountains, Mirkwood, the Elvenking’s Gate, Erebor, and Smaug—rather than producing canonical portrait sheets for all thirteen dwarves.

There is no identified medieval image securely labeled with any of these *Dvergatal* names. Generic dwarf imagery cannot be assigned to Þorinn or Dvalinn without an inscription or narrative context.

### 3.4 Sacred correspondences

No primary Norse or Tolkien text assigns this thirteen-member set sacred animals, plants, planets, weekdays, colors, ritual numbers, incense, offerings, or magical seals. Internet charts doing so are modern esoteric inventions unless they explicitly document a modern practitioner’s system.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### 4.1 Tolkienian genealogy

| Figure | Attested relationship |
|---|---|
| Thorin II | Son of Thráin II; grandson of Thrór; brother of Frerin and Dís; maternal uncle of Fíli and Kíli. Heir of Durin’s royal house. |
| Fíli and Kíli | Sons of Dís, Thorin’s sister; brothers; heirs close to Thorin in the royal line. Their father is never named. |
| Dwalin | Son of Fundin; brother of Balin. |
| Óin and Glóin | Sons of Gróin; brothers. |
| Glóin | Father of Gimli. Gimli’s mother is unnamed. |
| Bofur and Bombur | Brothers. |
| Bifur | Cousin of Bofur and Bombur. |
| Dori, Nori, Ori | No genealogy securely supplied in the principal texts. Their rhyming names do not prove brotherhood. |

Bifur, Bofur, and Bombur are associated with the people descended from the Dwarves of Moria but are not members of Durin’s royal line. The frequent assumption that all thirteen are close kin is unsupported.

### 4.2 Norse genealogy

The *Dvergatal* supplies no parents, spouses, children, or sibling relations for the relevant names. Its references to Dvalinn’s group and Lofarr’s line concern collective ordering but do not yield Tolkien’s family tree.

### 4.3 Divine-being interactions

Strictly applying the requested criterion, **there are no documented god–god interactions involving “the thirteen”**, because neither the Norse catalogue nor Tolkien classifies them as gods.

Relevant adjacent episodes must be labeled carefully:

- **Aulë and the first Dwarves — later Tolkienian canonical elaboration:** Aulë creates the remote ancestors of Dwarf-kind; Ilúvatar judges his act and gives them independent life. Source: *The Silmarillion*, “Of Aulë and Yavanna,” published 1977 from Tolkien’s evolving manuscripts. None of Thorin’s company appears in the scene.
- **Ilúvatar and the first Dwarves — later Tolkienian canonical elaboration:** Ilúvatar adopts them among his Children but delays their awakening. Same source and limitation.
- **Yavanna and Aulë — later Tolkienian canonical elaboration:** Their discussion of Aulë’s children and Yavanna’s plants leads toward the protection of trees by the Ents. No company member appears.
- **Dvalinn and Freyja — late medieval regional narrative:** In *Sörla þáttr*, a dwarf named Dvalinn participates in making Freyja’s necklace and sleeps with her as the price. This cannot securely be assigned to the catalogue’s Dvalinn and has no connection to Tolkien’s Dwalin.
- **Dvalinn and Óðinn — possible name-level relation only:** *Hávamál* associates Dvalinn with dwarf-runes and Óðinn with divine runes in a parallel catalogue. This is not a narrated meeting.

### 4.4 Other major Tolkienian interactions

These are literary character interactions, not cultic divine encounters:

- **Bilbo Baggins:** hired as burglar; rescued and assisted by the company; later estranged from Thorin over the Arkenstone; reconciled with the dying Thorin. *The Hobbit* (1937).
- **Gandalf:** organizes and repeatedly rescues the expedition; later explains his strategic motives in “The Quest of Erebor.”
- **Elrond:** receives the company at Rivendell and interprets the moon-letters on Thrór’s map.
- **The Great Goblin:** captures and interrogates them; killed by Gandalf.
- **Beorn:** shelters and equips them after testing their story.
- **Thranduil, the Elvenking:** imprisons the Dwarves after Thorin refuses to explain his business.
- **Smaug:** destroyed Erebor and later confronts Bilbo rather than the company collectively; his death makes their restoration possible.
- **Bard:** kills Smaug and demands part of the treasure for Lake-town and Dale; Thorin refuses until Bilbo’s Arkenstone intervention.
- **Dáin Ironfoot:** comes to Thorin’s aid, survives the Battle of Five Armies, and succeeds him as King under the Mountain.
- **Azog:** in Appendix A’s expanded history, kills Thrór and is killed by Dáin at Azanulbizar; his direct role in Thorin’s *Hobbit* quest is largely a film expansion.
- **Bolg:** commands the goblin host in *The Hobbit* and dies in the Battle of Five Armies.
- **Roäc:** raven who reports Smaug’s death and acts as Thorin’s messenger.
- **The Lord of the Eagles:** rescues the expedition and later joins the battle.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

## 5.1 The making and enumeration of dwarfs

**Earliest recoverable stratum:** *Vǫluspá*, probably composed around 1000 but surviving in thirteenth- and fourteenth-century manuscripts.

The gods convene after a crisis in the primordial order. The poem introduces Mótsognir as greatest of dwarfs and Durinn as second, then gives a long catalogue. The syntax around who makes whom is disputed: some readings make dwarfs form human-like shapes, while others connect the gods more directly with their creation. The poem then turns toward the creation of the first humans, Askr and Embla.

**Snorrian systematization:** *Gylfaginning* 14, circa 1220. Snorri places dwarf origins inside the Ymir cosmogony: they begin as maggot-like life in Ymir’s corpse, receive understanding and human form, and inhabit earth and stones. Snorri quotes or paraphrases the eddic list.

**Development:** The catalogue may contain expansions because its length and transitions interrupt the surrounding narrative, but it is present throughout the recoverable medieval tradition. No textual layer isolates Tolkien’s thirteen as a unit.

## 5.2 The fall of Erebor and Thorin’s exile

**Primary source:** *The Hobbit* (1937), supplemented by Appendix A (1955).

Thrór rules Erebor during an era of extraordinary wealth. Smaug attacks, expels the Dwarves, destroys Dale, and occupies the treasure. Thorin survives and becomes an exiled dynastic claimant. Appendix A adds the killing of Thrór by Azog in Moria, the War of the Dwarves and Orcs, the Battle of Azanulbizar, Thráin’s disappearance, and Gandalf’s receipt of the map and key from the captive Thráin in Dol Guldur.

This historical framework is a Tolkienian accretion with no counterpart in the *Dvergatal*.

## 5.3 Formation of Thorin’s company

In *The Hobbit*, the Dwarves arrive unexpectedly at Bag End in a carefully staged sequence. Gandalf has marked Bilbo’s door and represented him as an accomplished burglar. Thorin presents the expedition as a mission to recover ancestral treasure from Smaug. Bilbo accepts with confused reluctance.

“The Quest of Erebor” later makes Gandalf’s strategy more explicit: he fears Smaug could be used by Sauron against the North, considers Thorin’s original plans impractical, and inserts Bilbo as an unpredictable element. This is a later retelling from a different narrative perspective.

## 5.4 Journey to the Lonely Mountain

The company encounters:

- trolls, whose hoard yields Orcrist, Glamdring, and Sting;
- Elrond at Rivendell, who reads the moon-runes;
- Stone-giants and goblins in the Misty Mountains;
- Gollum and the Ring, encountered separately by Bilbo;
- Wargs, goblins, and the rescuing Eagles;
- Beorn;
- giant spiders in Mirkwood;
- the enchanted stream, which puts Bombur into a long sleep;
- imprisonment by the Wood-elves;
- escape in barrels to Lake-town.

The names’ Norse origin supplies no narrative source for these episodes. Tolkien combines fairy-story, heroic, folkloric, philological, and original materials.

## 5.5 Smaug, treasure, and the Arkenstone

Bilbo enters Erebor, steals a cup, converses with Smaug, and discovers the weak point in the dragon’s armor. A thrush communicates the information to Bard, who kills Smaug. Thorin then occupies the Mountain and claims the treasure.

When Bard and Thranduil request compensation and assistance for Lake-town, Thorin refuses. Bilbo gives them the Arkenstone, Thorin’s most valued dynastic object, to compel negotiation. Thorin condemns Bilbo and calls Dáin’s army.

This cycle converts the quest from dragon adventure into a study of possession, honor, inheritance, and political legitimacy. “Dragon-sickness” is narrative language for destructive possessiveness, not a documented Norse belief attached to Þorinn.

## 5.6 Battle of Five Armies and the deaths of the heirs

Goblins and Wargs attack, forcing Dwarves, Elves, and Men into alliance. Eagles and Beorn intervene. Thorin leads a sortie but is mortally wounded. Fíli and Kíli die defending him. Before dying, Thorin reconciles with Bilbo and repudiates the valuation of hoarded gold over food and fellowship.

Dáin becomes King under the Mountain. Bilbo returns west with a limited share of treasure. Of the thirteen, ten survive the quest.

## 5.7 Moria: the later fate of Óin and Ori

Appendix B and “The Bridge of Khazad-dûm” establish that Balin leads a colony to Moria in Third Age 2989. Óin and Ori accompany him. Óin is killed by the Watcher in the Water; Ori survives long enough to write the final pages of the Book of Mazarbul and dies during the colony’s destruction. The Fellowship finds their remains in 3019.

This grim sequel is absent from *The Hobbit* and belongs to the later *Lord of the Rings* integration.

## 5.8 Survivors after the quest

- **Dwalin:** survives into the Fourth Age, dying at the exceptional age of 340 according to Appendix A’s family tree.
- **Glóin:** prospers at Erebor, travels to Rivendell, and represents Dwarvish concerns at Elrond’s council.
- **Bombur:** remains in Erebor and becomes enormously fat.
- **Dori, Nori, Bifur, and Bofur:** survive the Battle of Five Armies, but Tolkien supplies no detailed final narratives for them.
- **Óin and Ori:** die in Moria.
- **Thorin, Fíli, and Kíli:** die in the Battle of Five Armies.

Nothing explicit establishes the ultimate deaths of Dori, Nori, Bifur, Bofur, Bombur, or Glóin.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### 6.1 Medieval Norse record

No temple, altar, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, prayer, pilgrimage, votive inscription, theophoric personal name series, or cult image is securely dedicated to this set—or to Þorinn, Fíli, Kíli, Bífurr, Báfurr, Bǫmburr, Nóri, Óri, Óinn, Glóinn, or Dóri individually.

Dvalinn’s wider literary presence is not evidence of a Dvalinn cult. No archaeological find presently establishes worship of him.

The material record therefore supports a **literary classification of dwarfs**, not a demonstrated institutional cult. Broad theories that dwarfs derive from ancestor spirits, the dead, disease beings, craft specialists, or nature spirits are scholarly reconstructions from names and narratives, not recovered liturgies.

### 6.2 Tolkien’s invented world

Dwarves honor Mahal, their name for Aulë, but Tolkien describes no temple system or priestly office among Thorin’s company. Funerary practices are more visible:

- Thorin is buried beneath Erebor with the Arkenstone and Orcrist.
- Balin is placed in a stone tomb in Moria.
- Dwarves prize genealogy, ancestral halls, crafted monuments, and guarded names.

These are features of fictional world-building, not records of a historical religion.

### 6.3 Modern practice

Some contemporary Heathens, animists, occultists, and Tolkien-inspired practitioners may address dwarfs or Tolkienian figures privately. Such practice is a modern revival or individual innovation unless supported by a documented organizational history. No evidence located establishes a continuous line of worship from medieval Iceland to a modern cult of Thorin’s company.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Phase I: Probable oral and poetic formation, circa tenth–eleventh century

An Old Norse cosmogonic poem incorporates dwarf names into a metrically organized catalogue. The date and internal growth of that catalogue remain debated. There is no evidence yet of the Tolkienian grouping.

### Phase II: Icelandic textual preservation and systematization, circa 1220–1400

Snorri adapts the material in the *Prose Edda*. The Codex Regius and Hauksbók preserve differing versions of *Vǫluspá*. Dwarf names also circulate through eddic poems, kennings, *þulur*, and prose narratives. Christian Icelandic scribes preserve and reorganize pre-Christian mythological material.

### Phase III: Antiquarian and philological transmission, seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

Eddic manuscripts become objects of Scandinavian antiquarian collection, transcription, editing, translation, and nationalist-era interpretation. Printed editions make the dwarf-list available to scholars outside Iceland. Increasingly standardized spellings can obscure manuscript variation.

### Phase IV: Tolkien’s creative appropriation, circa 1930–1937

Tolkien, an Oxford philologist trained in Old English and Old Norse, selects names from the *Dvergatal*. The initial leader Gandalf is renamed Thorin; the wizard receives Gandalf’s name. Tolkien combines Þorinn and Eikinskjaldi into Thorin Oakenshield. The published company first exists on 21 September 1937.

Tolkien later warned that the Eddic origin of the names was “no key” to his Dwarf legends. The borrowing is onomastic, not evidence that *The Hobbit* secretly retells a lost Norse myth.

### Phase V: Integration into the legendarium, 1937–1973

While composing *The Lord of the Rings*, Tolkien makes *The Hobbit* part of a much larger mythology. The 1951 revision alters the Ring narrative. *The Lord of the Rings* adds dynastic chronology, Glóin and Gimli’s later roles, the Moria colony, Khuzdul secrecy, and the translated-name convention. “The Quest of Erebor” recasts Gandalf’s motives in geopolitical terms.

### Phase VI: Posthumous editorial reconstruction, 1977–2011

Christopher Tolkien publishes *The Silmarillion* and *Unfinished Tales*, exposing the deeper Dwarf creation story and Gandalf’s retrospective account. Douglas A. Anderson’s *The Annotated Hobbit* documents sources and edition variants. Rateliff’s *History of The Hobbit* reveals the manuscripts, including Gandalf-the-dwarf and Bladorthin-the-wizard.

### Phase VII: Global multimedia reception, 1977–present

Animated, radio, game, illustrated, and live-action versions increasingly differentiate the company visually. Peter Jackson’s film trilogy—*An Unexpected Journey* (2012), *The Desolation of Smaug* (2013), and *The Battle of the Five Armies* (2014)—gave each dwarf an individualized costume, silhouette, weapon set, and performance identity. This became the dominant mass-market image but is not a neutral visualization of Tolkien’s descriptions.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### 8.1 Literature and illustration

The company became a template for the adventuring fantasy party: a leader, younger heirs, veteran warriors, craftsmen, comic figures, and a reluctant outsider. Later fantasy’s heavily differentiated dwarven names often imitate Tolkien, sometimes indirectly recycling the *Dvergatal*.

Artists including Tolkien, Pauline Baynes, Michael Hague, Alan Lee, John Howe, and many edition illustrators have produced incompatible visual traditions. None constitutes an ancient iconographic canon.

### 8.2 Screen adaptations

The Rankin/Bass animated *Hobbit* (1977) compresses the company and distinguishes only some members strongly. Jackson’s trilogy expands characterization, romance, combat, and political material:

- Thorin becomes a tragic action-hero with a prolonged personal feud with Azog.
- Kíli receives a romance with Tauriel, a character created for the films.
- Fíli and Kíli receive substantially expanded battlefield deaths.
- Bofur becomes a major comic and sympathetic interlocutor.
- Bombur’s body becomes the basis of elaborate action comedy.
- Each Dwarf receives distinctive hair, prosthetics, costume, and weapons.

The Tauriel–Kíli relationship, Azog’s pursuit of Thorin during the quest, and most individualized weapons are modern inventions of the film adaptation.

### 8.3 Games and derivative world-building

Role-playing games, miniatures, trading cards, video games, and online games assign statistics, classes, equipment, dialogue, and adventures not supplied by Tolkien. These are licensed or unlicensed extensions, not recovered canonical information. Games materially reinforced the concept that each company member should possess a unique combat role.

### 8.4 Commercial use

The characters appear in toys, collectible statues, LEGO sets, costumes, replica weapons, cards, posters, and branded tourism. Commercial visual identities generally depend more on cinematic design than on Tolkien’s sparse descriptions.

### 8.5 Esotericism and neopaganism

Unlike Odin, Thor, Freyja, or runes, Thorin’s company has no major documented role in Renaissance magic, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, or classical Theosophy. Modern invocations of them in magical or devotional settings are post-1937 Tolkien reception. Claims that medieval practitioners assigned these thirteen dwarfs planetary seals, directions, crystals, or festival days are unsupported.

### 8.6 Political appropriation

Tolkien’s Dwarves have been interpreted through exile, diaspora, industrial craft, kingship, nationalism, and ethnic identity. Tolkien acknowledged Jewish resonances in Dwarvish language and history in later correspondence, but reducing the company to a fixed ethnic allegory exceeds the texts.

Germanic and Tolkien imagery has sometimes been appropriated within far-right subcultures, but no evidence reviewed establishes a distinctive extremist cult centered on these thirteen. It would be irresponsible to infer one from generic use of runes, beards, or Norse names.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 9.1 Is the *Dvergatal* original to *Vǫluspá*?

The catalogue disrupts the transition between dwarf and human creation, and textual differences suggest growth during transmission. Some scholars regard much of it as interpolation. Others emphasize that every major medieval witness includes a catalogue and that list-poetry is an integral Old Norse mnemonic and encyclopedic form. The most defensible formulation is that the catalogue may be composite but belongs securely to the medieval transmitted poem.

### 9.2 What were Old Norse dwarfs?

No single theory commands consensus. Scholarship has variously emphasized:

- beings of earth and stone;
- master craftsmen;
- death or ancestor associations;
- disease or nightmare figures;
- liminal beings overlapping with elves;
- personifications encoded in poetic names.

Lotte Motz attempted to reconstruct dwarf functions through systematic name study. Later scholarship has warned that etymological inventories can manufacture coherence from obscure, corrupt, or reused names.

### 9.3 Are Dvalinn’s appearances one biography?

Not demonstrably. The name labels a dwarf, a stag, a figure connected with runes, and a participant in later prose narrative. Medieval poets freely reuse meaningful names. A “complete biography of Dvalinn” assembled from all occurrences may be a modern harmonization.

### 9.4 How directly did Tolkien borrow?

The name-list is direct and acknowledged. The narratives are overwhelmingly Tolkien’s construction. Tolkien’s statement that the Eddic names are “no key” to his legends rejects one-to-one decoding. His most important transformation was to place borrowed public names inside a fictional translation system: the characters’ “real” private names remain concealed.

### 9.5 Did Tolkien intentionally use every etymology?

Sometimes semantic play is plausible—especially Bombur’s size, Glóin’s fire association, and Thorin’s courage—but proof varies. Scholars distinguish:

- authorially stated derivation;
- transparent linguistic possibility;
- probable literary wordplay;
- coincidence noticed by readers.

Popular lists often present disputed philology as settled authorial intention.

### 9.6 Canon and textual strata

There is no single uncomplicated canon:

- The 1937 *Hobbit* is the earliest published narrative.
- The 1951 text revises it in light of *The Lord of the Rings*.
- The 1960 rewrite is an abandoned alternative.
- Appendix A supplies later authoritative history.
- “The Quest of Erebor” exists in multiple textual forms and was published posthumously.
- *The Silmarillion* is an editorially constructed 1977 text based on Tolkien’s manuscripts.
- Jackson’s films constitute a separate adaptation continuity.

Statements drawn from these strata should not be silently combined.

### 9.7 Important repositories and editions

- **Árni Magnússon Institute, Reykjavík:** Codex Regius and major Icelandic manuscripts.
- **Arnamagnæan Collection, Copenhagen/Reykjavík:** Hauksbók components and other Old Norse witnesses.
- **Marquette University:** *The Hobbit* and *The Lord of the Rings* manuscripts.
- **Bodleian Libraries, Oxford:** Tolkien papers, artwork, maps, and related holdings.
- **Critical tools:** normalized Old Norse editions, manuscript facsimiles, Ursula Dronke’s *The Poetic Edda*, Anthony Faulkes’s editions of Snorri, Anderson’s *Annotated Hobbit*, and Rateliff’s *History of The Hobbit*.

### 9.8 Discredited or unsupported claims

The following should not be presented as ancient fact:

- that Thorin’s company was a Norse divine brotherhood;
- that the thirteen names constituted a single medieval expedition;
- that Þorinn was a form or son of Thor;
- that “Oakenshield” belonged to Þorinn in *Vǫluspá*;
- that each Tolkien dwarf’s personality is encoded securely in his Norse name;
- that Tolkien translated a lost saga about Bilbo;
- that the company possessed medieval cult colors, planets, runes, seals, or feast days;
- that film-only relationships and appearances are Tolkien’s descriptions;
- that modern Khuzdul reconstructions reveal their secret canonical names.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Late tenth–early eleventh century, disputed | Probable composition or major formation of *Vǫluspá*; dating is philological rather than manuscript-based. |
| Circa 1220 | Snorri composes the *Edda*, adapting dwarf cosmogony and catalogue material in *Gylfaginning*. |
| Circa 1270 | Codex Regius, GKS 2365 4to, records the principal surviving *Vǫluspá* text containing the dwarf-list. |
| Late thirteenth–early fourteenth century | Hauksbók preserves another version of *Vǫluspá*. |
| Late fourteenth century | *Flateyjarbók* compilation preserves late narratives including *Sörla þáttr*, with a dwarf named Dvalinn. |
| Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries | Manuscript collection, printed editions, translations, and comparative Germanic scholarship make the Eddic catalogue internationally accessible. |
| Circa 1930–1931 | Tolkien begins *The Hobbit*; early material has Bladorthin as wizard and Gandalf as dwarf-leader. |
| 1932–January 1933 | Principal narrative phases completed; Thorin emerges as the leader’s published name. |
| October 1936 | Revised typescript submitted to George Allen & Unwin. |
| 21 September 1937 | *The Hobbit* published; first public appearance of the thirteen-member company. |
| 1938 | Tolkien publicly confirms the Eddic origin of the dwarf names in correspondence concerning *The Hobbit*. |
| 1947 | Tolkien prepares revisions connecting *The Hobbit* more closely to the emerging *Lord of the Rings*. |
| 1951 | Second edition publishes the revised “Riddles in the Dark.” |
| 1954–1955 | *The Lord of the Rings* publishes Glóin’s later appearance, the Moria record of Óin and Ori, and Appendix A’s dynastic history. |
| 1950s | Tolkien composes versions of “The Quest of Erebor.” |
| 1960 | A more extensive rewrite of *The Hobbit* is begun and abandoned after three chapters. |
| 1966 | Further textual revisions; Tolkien’s *Hobbit* sketch later used for an Unwin paperback cover. |
| 1977 | *The Silmarillion* publishes the mature Aulë–Ilúvatar account of Dwarf origins; Rankin/Bass releases its animated *Hobbit*. |
| 1980 | “The Quest of Erebor” published in *Unfinished Tales*. |
| 1988/2002 | Douglas A. Anderson’s *Annotated Hobbit*, later revised and expanded, consolidates textual and source commentary. |
| 2007 | Rateliff publishes the first edition of *The History of The Hobbit*. |
| 2011 | Revised one-volume *History of The Hobbit* published. |
| 2012 | Jackson’s *An Unexpected Journey* establishes the dominant cinematic portraits of the company. |
| 2013 | *The Desolation of Smaug* expands Kíli–Tauriel and other film-only narratives. |
| 2014 | *The Battle of the Five Armies* completes the film continuity’s account of Thorin, Fíli, and Kíli’s deaths. |
| Present | The figures persist through Tolkien publication, scholarship, adaptations, games, fandom, private spirituality, and merchandising; no continuous ancient cult has been demonstrated. |

### Evidentiary conclusion

The fullest reconstructable history is not that of an ancient god who later entered a novel. It is the history of a medieval list of dwarf-names, imperfectly preserved in Icelandic manuscripts; Tolkien’s conscious extraction and recombination of those names in the early 1930s; their integration into an increasingly systematic invented history; and their subsequent differentiation through scholarship, illustration, cinema, gaming, commerce, and modern personal reception. Cult, priesthood, temples, ancient iconography, and divine status are unattested.
