# Anubis: Pantheon Dossier

**Proposed filename:** `anubis_pantheon_dossier.md`

## Scope, method, and evidentiary labels

This dossier concerns the documented history of the Egyptian divine being conventionally called **Anubis**: the history of his name, images, ritual functions, myths, cults, transformations, and reception. It does not treat mythic episodes as ordinary biographical events. Dates apply to the surviving evidence or to the probable composition of texts, not to events within the mythic world.

Labels used below:

- **Early attested:** evidence from the Early Dynastic Period or Old Kingdom.
- **Later canonical:** a formulation broadly disseminated through Middle or New Kingdom funerary religion.
- **Regional variant:** a local theological or cultic arrangement that should not be universalized.
- **Priestly systematization:** especially temple-library mythography of the Late, Ptolemaic, or Roman periods.
- **Syncretistic identification:** a historically datable equation or fusion with another deity.
- **Late accretion:** evidence first securely found in substantially later sources.
- **Modern reconstruction:** a scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **Modern invention or retelling:** a recent teaching, ritual, genealogy, or narrative not demonstrable in ancient evidence.

Egyptian chronology varies between authorities, particularly before the New Kingdom. Dates are therefore approximate unless attached to a specifically dated object.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Anubis is an Egyptian male funerary god represented either as a black canid—usually recumbent and alert—or as an anthropomorphic male with a black canid head. His oldest securely identifiable functions concern the cemetery, royal and elite burial, bodily integrity, offerings, and access to the dead. From the Old Kingdom onward he is invoked as a guardian of burial grounds and the embalming installation; in the Pyramid and Coffin Texts he receives, protects, purifies, restores, and guides the deceased. With the expansion of Osirian religion, Anubis was not simply “replaced” by Osiris: his functions became more specialized and were incorporated into an Osirian mortuary system. In New Kingdom Books of the Dead he attends the mummy and the judgment of the dead. Under Greek and Roman rule he became a conspicuous member of the internationally diffused Isiac cult, a conductor between worlds, and, through identification with Hermes, **Hermanubis**.

Calling Anubis merely “god of death” is imprecise. Egyptian evidence more specifically makes him a god of the **necropolis, embalming, protection of the corpse, admission to the afterlife, and mediation across boundaries**. Osiris is normally sovereign of the dead in developed Osirian theology; Anubis prepares and conducts them.

### Egyptian name

The Egyptian consonantal form is conventionally transliterated:

- **jnpw**, increasingly written **ỉnpw** in modern Egyptological typography.
- Older publications commonly use **Inpw**, **Anpu**, **Anup**, or **Yinepu**.
- Egyptian writing omitted most vowels; “Anpu” is therefore a conventional Egyptological vocalization, not a directly preserved pharaonic pronunciation.
- UCL’s Digital Egypt illustrates the written name as the phonetic sequence *j-n-p* followed by a divine/canid determinative. The same resource stresses that “Anubis” is the Greek form, not the Egyptian spelling. [UCL Digital Egypt](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/writing/system1.html)

Hieroglyphic fonts are not stable across all Markdown renderers. In Manuel de Codage the name is commonly encoded approximately as `i-n:p-w` plus a divine or recumbent-canid determinative. A Unicode approximation sometimes displayed online as **𓇋𓈖𓊪𓅱** does not reproduce every historical spelling and should not be mistaken for a single canonical orthography.

Probable later vocalization is partially recoverable from:

- Greek **Ἄνουβις** (*Anoubis*);
- Coptic **ⲁⲛⲟⲩⲡ** or related forms conventionally transcribed *Anoup*;
- the Greek and Coptic evidence suggests a pronunciation nearer *Anūp* than the English “Anubis.”

### Greek, Latin, and later names

- Greek: **Ἄνουβις**, *Anoubis*.
- Latin: **Anubis**, borrowed from Greek.
- Greek syncretistic name: **Ἑρμανοῦβις**, *Hermanoubis*—Hermes + Anubis.
- Modern Arabic: **أنوبيس**, *Anūbīs*, a modern learned borrowing.
- French: *Anubis*.
- German: *Anubis*; Egyptological *Jnpw*.
- Italian and Spanish: *Anubi* and *Anubis*.
- Modern Kemetic communities frequently prefer **Anpu**, but this vocalization is reconstructive rather than a securely preserved Old Egyptian pronunciation.

### Etymology

The etymology of *jnpw* is unresolved. Comparisons with Egyptian words for a royal child, decay, or putrefaction have been proposed, as have derivations involving canine terms, but no proposal has achieved secure consensus. Assertions that the name literally means “jackal,” “royal child,” “he who decays,” or “prince” should therefore be labeled hypotheses, not translations.

The internet etymology **“Anu-bis,”** connecting Anubis with a supposed “Anu people,” the ibis, extraterrestrials, or a prehistoric mystery school, is a **modern invention** unsupported by Egyptian orthography or historical linguistics. Egyptian *jnpw* is not segmented as English “Anu + bis.”

### Principal epithets

Spellings and translations vary because Egyptian titles compress several possible nuances.

| Egyptian title | Conventional translation | Documentary significance |
|---|---|---|
| **tpy ḏw.f** | “He who is upon his mountain” | The god watches from the desert height overlooking the cultivated valley and cemetery. Attested in Old Kingdom mortuary formulae and persistent thereafter. |
| **ḫnty sḥ-nṯr** | “Foremost of the divine booth/pavilion” | Associates him with the embalming structure or divine mortuary tent. It is used in Book of the Dead Spell 151. [UCL, Spell 151](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/hpres151.html) |
| **imy-wt** | “He who is in the wrappings” or “He who is in the *wt* place” | Links Anubis with embalming and with the **imiut** or “nebride” fetish. The precise original meaning of *wt* and its relationship to the fetish remain debated. |
| **nb tꜣ ḏsr** | “Lord of the sacred land” | “Sacred land” commonly denotes the necropolis/desert burial zone. |
| **ḫnty tꜣ ḏsr** | “Foremost of the sacred land” | Necropolis authority; especially visible in later mortuary language. |
| **ḫnty ỉmntỉw** | “Foremost of the Westerners” | “Westerners” means the dead. This title was also the name/title of an older Abydene canid deity, Khentiamentiu, and must not always be assumed to mean Anubis. |
| **nb Rꜣ-stꜣw** | “Lord of Rosetjau” | Associates him with the necropolis and underworld zone called Rosetjau, especially in Memphite funerary geography. |
| **sꜣ n psḏt** | “Son of the Ennead” | A relational title in developed theological systems, not a stable biological genealogy. |
| **ỉmy-ỉb sḥ-nṯr** and related titles | “He who is within the divine booth” | Emphasize presence in the embalming space. |
| “Guardian of the scales” | Modern descriptive title | Accurately describes many judgment vignettes but is not a universal translation of one fixed ancient name. |

The common claim that all five “principal epithets” existed fully formed from the beginning of Egyptian history is too strong. Their components and functions are ancient, but their distribution must be assessed inscription by inscription. The formula “Anubis, upon his mountain, who is in the *wt*, lord of the sacred land” became especially durable in funerary offering texts.

### Classification through time

- **Early Dynastic and Old Kingdom:** divine canid, necropolis lord, guardian of royal and elite burial, recipient of offerings.
- **Middle Kingdom:** mortuary protector, embalmer, restorer, guide, and participant in expanding Osirian afterlife theology.
- **New Kingdom:** embalmer and mummy guardian; conductor into judgment; attendant or operator of the balance in funerary vignettes.
- **Ptolemaic temple theology:** local warrior, punisher of Seth, guardian of Osiris’s relics, and protagonist of detailed nome myths.
- **Hellenistic and Roman Mediterranean:** Isiac processional deity, psychopomp, mediator of upper and lower worlds; sometimes Hermanubis.
- **Christian polemic:** animal-headed pagan god or demonized emblem of Egyptian religion.
- **Modern occultism and Paganism:** guardian, initiator, psychopomp, funerary patron, or devotional deity—modern adaptations selectively based on ancient material.

### Interpretatio graeca and romana

Plutarch, writing in Greek around the late first or early second century CE, states that Anubis has approximately the same function among Egyptians as **Hermes** among Greeks because both operate in celestial and infernal spheres. This is historical Greco-Egyptian interpretation, not evidence that Egyptians had always regarded Anubis and Hermes as one god. [Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris*](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0239)

**Hermanubis** became an explicit composite or syncretistic figure in Roman-period religion and iconography. He may carry Hermes’ caduceus while retaining Anubis’s canid head. Apuleius describes Anubis in an Isiac procession holding a caduceus and palm, with a face alternately black and gold. [Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* XI](https://www.graverini.net/pubs/%5B001%5D%20GCA%202015b%20-%20Apuleius%20Madaurensis%20Metamorphoses%20XI.pdf)

Equation with **Hades/Pluto** is much less exact. Classical authors sometimes interpret Egyptian death gods through Greek underworld categories, but developed Egyptian sources normally distinguish Anubis’s preparatory and mediating roles from Osiris’s sovereignty.

The popular equation **Anubis = Wepwawet** is also misleading. Both are canid gods with overlapping processional, royal, and funerary functions, and late sources can associate or fuse them, but they remain separately named deities in much Egyptian evidence.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

Canid standards, recumbent desert canids, and the **imiut** fetish occur in Predynastic and Early Dynastic imagery. Identification of every early canid as Anubis is methodologically unsafe: Khentiamentiu, Wepwawet, and other local or not-yet-differentiated canid powers must be considered.

A frequently cited First Dynasty year-label from the reign of **Aha**, around the early third millennium BCE, has been interpreted as recording the “birth” or fashioning of an Anubis image. The reading is important but not equivalent to a narrative about Anubis and does not establish the later Osirian genealogy. It documents royal cult manufacture or ceremony. Early labels also display the imiut standard. Interpretations of these labels remain debated.

By the Old Kingdom, securely readable tomb offering formulae invoke Anubis with recognizable titles. These inscriptions make him a receiver or guarantor of mortuary offerings and burial privileges. What they do **not** supply is a continuous narrative biography, a fixed family tree, or the later story that he was the adulterous son of Osiris and Nephthys.

### Pyramid Texts

The Pyramid Texts were first inscribed in the pyramid of **Unas**, late Fifth Dynasty, around the twenty-fourth century BCE, and subsequently in royal pyramids of the Sixth Dynasty and those of several queens. They are the oldest extensive surviving Egyptian religious corpus. James P. Allen’s critical translation provides the accessible modern point of reference. [Allen, *The Ancient Egyptian Pyramid Texts*](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/AllenPyramid.pdf)

Anubis appears in several functional settings:

- the dead king is associated or identified with Anubis;
- Anubis receives the deceased;
- the god’s terrestrial or celestial localities mark stages in mortuary passage;
- he is associated with purification, bodily integrity, burial, and judgment;
- his daughter **Qebehut** refreshes or purifies the dead and gathers or protects bodily parts;
- the king may appear “as a jackal,” demonstrating that divine identification is ritual and transformational, not zoological description.

These spells are not a consecutive myth. They are ritual utterances whose allusions presuppose traditions not narrated in full.

### Private tomb formulae

Old Kingdom mastabas contain the **ḥtp-dỉ-nsw** or “offering which the king gives” formula addressed to Anubis. Such inscriptions are crucial evidence for his presence in non-royal elite mortuary religion. Titles invoking his mountain, embalming place, or sacred land connect divine authority to actual burial administration and landscape.

### Coffin Texts

The Coffin Texts, written principally on Middle Kingdom coffins but preserving older material, greatly expand the accessible mortuary corpus. Anubis occurs repeatedly as embalmer, guardian, purifier, judge, guide, and recipient of the deceased. Adriaan de Buck’s seven-volume edition remains foundational; the Oriental Institute/ISAC provides records for the volumes. [ISAC, *Egyptian Coffin Texts I*](https://isac.uchicago.edu/research/publications/oip/egyptian-coffin-texts-1-texts-spells-1-75)

The corpus shows:

- continued identification of the deceased with gods;
- Anubis making the corpse fragrant or receiving incense;
- protection against decomposition and enemies;
- increasingly explicit integration with Osiris;
- local topographies and gates involving Anubis;
- no single universally consistent genealogy.

The significance of Anubis across the corpus has been examined directly in modern research rather than merely inferred from dictionary summaries. [Macquarie University thesis, *The significance of Anubis as seen in Coffin Texts*](https://figshare.mq.edu.au/articles/thesis/The_significance_of_Anubis_as_seen_in_coffin_texts/19442297)

### Book of the Dead

The New Kingdom funerary corpus conventionally called the **Book of the Dead**, Egyptian *rꜥw nw prt m hrw* (“spells of going forth by day”), circulated from approximately 1550 BCE into the Roman period. Individual papyri select and arrange spells differently; there was no single immutable “Bible.”

Especially important appearances include:

- **Spell 17:** composite cosmological and mortuary identifications; annotations vary between manuscripts.
- **Spells 28–30B:** protection or restoration of the heart, often associated iconographically with judgment.
- **Spell 125:** admission to the Hall of the Two Truths and judgment. The earliest written witnesses are New Kingdom, not Old Kingdom. [UCL, Spell 125A](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/bd125a.html)
- **Spell 151:** a strongly visual, captioned composition in which Anubis stands over the mummy as “foremost of the divine booth,” places his hands upon the coffin, and wraps it by his skill. [UCL, Spell 151](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/hpres151.html)

The celebrated **Papyrus of Ani**, around 1250 BCE, shows Anubis steadying the balance while Thoth records the result and Osiris presides. Ani actually recites Spell 30B to his heart in this scene; popular captions frequently call the whole vignette “Spell 125,” compressing adjacent textual and visual units. [British Museum EA10470,3](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10470-3)

The **Greenfield Papyrus**, around 950–930 BCE, gives another elaborate Spell 125 vignette: Anubis and Thoth coordinate the weighing while Ammit and a lake of fire appear nearby. [British Museum EA10554,80](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10554-80)

### Ritual of Embalming and mortuary liturgies

Late copies of the **Ritual of Embalming** preserve speeches, anointings, wrapping acts, and divine identifications performed around the corpse. Anubis is the paradigmatic divine embalmer. The surviving manuscripts are late—principally Roman-period papyri—but portions of the ritual tradition are much older. It is methodologically incorrect either to date every instruction to the Roman period or to project the complete late manual unchanged into the Old Kingdom.

“Lamentations of Isis and Nephthys,” “Songs of Isis and Nephthys,” and Osirian festival liturgies also give Anubis protective and embalming functions. Female ritualists could embody the mourning goddesses. These are liturgical dramatizations, not documentary transcripts of a single historical funeral.

### Underworld books and royal tombs

New Kingdom royal tomb compositions—the **Amduat**, **Book of Gates**, **Book of Caverns**, and related works—contain canid guardians and forms connected with Anubis. Identification must depend on labels, because a canid-headed guardian is not automatically Anubis.

### The Tale of the Two Brothers

The **Papyrus D’Orbiney**, British Museum EA10183, is the sole complete manuscript of the *Tale of the Two Brothers*, copied in hieratic near the end of the Nineteenth Dynasty, commonly around 1200 BCE. It names two human or heroized brothers, **Anubis** and **Bata**. Anubis’s wife attempts to seduce Bata, falsely accuses him, and causes Anubis to pursue him. After divine intervention separates them, Anubis learns the truth and kills his wife. Bata undergoes deaths, transformations, and rebirth, becomes king, and appoints Anubis as successor. [British Museum, Papyrus D’Orbiney](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10183-6)

This text is relevant to divine traditions because Anubis and Bata are divine/local cult names and later nome mythography reworks their relationship. Yet the literary character “Anubis” should not automatically be treated as a transparent narration about the funerary god. The story deliberately moves between domestic folktale, royal succession tale, and divine mythology.

### Papyrus Jumilhac

The **Papyrus Jumilhac**, now Louvre E 17110, is a Ptolemaic or early Roman mythological-geographical manual for Middle Egyptian nomes. Its surviving physical manuscript is very late relative to Anubis’s earliest attestations, though it preserves older motifs. It presents Anubis as an active defender of Osiris’s body and a violent opponent of Seth: he binds, mutilates, brands, flays, or otherwise punishes Seth in several etiological episodes. The myths explain local cults, priestly prohibitions, animal forms, place-names, relics, and ritual objects. [Louvre, Papyrus Jumilhac](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark%3A/53355/cl010003518)

This is **priestly systematization and regional mythology**, not a universally canonical Egyptian account. Its detailed stories must not be projected unchanged into the Old Kingdom.

### Greco-Roman authors

- **Diodorus Siculus**, first century BCE, reports that Egyptians represented Anubis with a dog’s head because he guarded Osiris and Isis; he also records an explanation in which dogs aided Isis’s search and led her festival procession. This is Greek ethnography containing Egyptian information but framed through Hellenistic explanation. [Diodorus, *Library of History* I.87](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/diodorus_siculus/1d%2A.html)
- **Plutarch**, around 100 CE, gives the best-known continuous Greek narrative of Isis and Osiris. In his account Nephthys conceives Anubis by Osiris, exposes him, and Isis finds and raises him. Plutarch also allegorizes Anubis as the horizon and compares him to Hermes. These are late, philosophically interpreted versions, not evidence for a stable Old Kingdom genealogy. [Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris*](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0239)
- **Apuleius**, later second century CE, describes Anubis in the public procession of Isis: canine-headed, black and gold, carrying caduceus and palm. The passage documents Roman Isiac representation, though *Metamorphoses* is a literary work, not a temple service book. [Apuleius, Book XI](https://www.graverini.net/pubs/%5B001%5D%20GCA%202015b%20-%20Apuleius%20Madaurensis%20Metamorphoses%20XI.pdf)

### Greek and Demotic magical papyri

The Greek Magical Papyri and the Demotic Magical Papyrus of London and Leiden, principally Roman-period manuscripts, invoke Anubis in divination, dream or direct-vision rites, funerary adjurations, and syncretistic divine-name sequences.

British Museum EA10588 includes a Demotic and Greek invocation of Anubis, catalogued as a divinatory text. [British Museum EA10588](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10588-1)

In the London–Leiden papyrus, a boy-medium is instructed to ask Anubis which god will answer the inquiry; an “Anubis plant” is placed on the lamp. These texts belong to the ritual technology of Roman Egypt and combine Egyptian, Greek, Jewish, and other divine names. [Demotic Magical Papyrus](https://www.attalus.org/egypt/demotic_magical_papyrus.html)

The papyri do not prove a separate mass “Anubis mystery religion.” They document specialist ritual handbooks, probably connected in part with learned temple milieus.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and historical development

**Necropolis and burial ground.** The desert margin was both the physical cemetery and a religious threshold. “Upon his mountain” presents Anubis as the elevated watcher over tombs. His relationship to scavenging canids is a plausible ecological explanation, but no surviving Egyptian theological text says simply, “jackals haunted cemeteries, therefore we worship Anubis.” That formulation is modern reconstruction.

**Protection of the corpse.** Anubis guards against physical dismemberment, decomposition, hostile beings, and violation of the tomb. This protection is both ritual and magical.

**Embalming.** He presides over the *sḥ-nṯr*, the “divine booth,” and the *wt* place or wrappings. Spell 151 visualizes him bending over the mummy and working with his hands. Priests acting as Anubis could perform the divine role ritually.

**Guidance and admission.** He meets, conducts, or introduces the deceased. The modern term **psychopomp** is Greek-derived scholarly vocabulary, accurate as a comparison but not an Egyptian class-name.

**Judgment.** New Kingdom and later images show him attending or adjusting the scales. He does not normally pronounce final sovereign judgment: Thoth records, a divine tribunal witnesses, and Osiris presides. Modern descriptions making Anubis the sole “judge of souls” collapse distinct roles.

**Transformation and revival.** Embalming was not mere preservation; ritual transformed the deceased into an effective, divine dead being. Anubis’s acts therefore support rebirth.

**Local warrior and relic-guardian.** In Papyrus Jumilhac he fights Seth and protects the dismembered Osiris. This vigorous warrior role is a late-attested regional elaboration, though it may preserve earlier traditions.

### Animal identity

The species represented is debated. Egyptological literature has variously said:

- jackal;
- dog;
- wolf;
- a generalized wild canid or deliberately idealized divine species.

The extinct or rare North African wolf and several jackal-like canids complicated older zoological identifications. Egyptian art itself does not aim at modern taxonomic diagnosis. “Canid” is the safest neutral term; “jackal-headed” remains conventional.

### Black color

Anubis’s black color has at least two contextually appropriate associations:

1. the darkened or resin-treated body of the mummy;
2. the fertile black Nile soil and regenerative potential.

It should not be interpreted as the natural coat color of a specific animal, nor as a racial classification. Apuleius’s black-and-gold Anubis reflects Roman Isiac symbolism and ceremonial display.

### Standard forms

1. **Full canid:** black, recumbent, ears erect, forelegs extended, tail descending; often placed on a shrine or chest.
2. **Canid-headed anthropomorphic male:** standing, striding, kneeling, or bending over a mummy.
3. **Mummiform or composite forms:** less common, especially in late ritual and magical iconography.
4. **Hermanubis:** canid-headed anthropomorphic figure with caduceus, sometimes palm branch or Greek dress.
5. **Human-headed Anubis:** rare and context-dependent; a human-headed image without a label cannot securely be identified as Anubis.

### Recognizing Anubis

Reliable identification rests on a combination of:

- a written label naming *jnpw*;
- black canid head or full body;
- recumbency upon a shrine;
- location beside a bier or mummy;
- action at the balance;
- titles such as “foremost of the divine booth”;
- the imiut fetish;
- in Roman imagery, caduceus plus canine head.

A black canid alone may instead be Wepwawet, Khentiamentiu, Duamutef, or another guardian.

### Important surviving depictions

- Early Dynastic canid standards and imiut emblems: precursors or early cultic forms, but not all securely labeled Anubis.
- Old Kingdom tomb reliefs and offering inscriptions: among the first secure textual-iconographic contexts.
- The Anubis chapels in Hatshepsut’s temple at Deir el-Bahri, Eighteenth Dynasty: royal cult integration and richly preserved ritual scenes.
- New Kingdom royal tomb paintings: Anubis receives and protects kings in the afterlife.
- **Tutankhamun’s recumbent Anubis shrine**, KV62, fourteenth century BCE: a black, alert canid mounted on a shrine-like chest, probably one of the most influential ancient images in modern reception.
- **Papyrus of Hunefer**, around 1300 BCE: Anubis supports or attends the mummy and participates in judgment imagery.
- **Papyrus of Ani**, around 1250 BCE: Anubis operates or steadies the balance. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10470-3)
- **Greenfield Papyrus**, around 950–930 BCE: Anubis coordinates the weighing with Thoth. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10554-80)
- Saqqara recumbent statue, Dynasty 26–27, approximately 664–404 BCE: near-life-sized limestone canid excavated from a temple deposit. [Metropolitan Museum publication](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/An_Egyptian_Bestiary_The_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art_Bulletin_v_52_no_4_Spring_1995.pdf)
- Late Period–Ptolemaic faience amulets: small protective figures placed on or worn with the dead. [Metropolitan Museum 04.2.151](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/548208)
- Ptolemaic wooden Anubis figure with raised hands: the gesture signifies purification or transfiguration over a mummy. [Metropolitan Museum teaching catalogue](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_Art_of_Ancient_Egypt_A_Resource_for_Educators.pdf)
- Roman Isiac sculptures and reliefs of Hermanubis: canine head, Greek or Roman body, caduceus, and sometimes solar attributes.

### Objects and symbols

**Imiut fetish.** A headless animal skin, often feline or bovine in interpretation, suspended from a pole set in a vessel, with the tail hanging down. It appears very early and is strongly associated with Anubis and embalming. Its original meaning remains uncertain. Claims that it straightforwardly depicts a human sacrifice are unsupported.

**Bier or lion-bed.** Anubis bends over the mummy on a funerary bed, sometimes lion-shaped.

**Bandages, oils, incense, and resins.** These belong to embalming and ritual purification. They are not merely later decorative “correspondences.”

**Balance.** Especially New Kingdom and later. The balance is part of a larger judgment system involving Maat, Thoth, Osiris, the assessors, and sometimes Ammit.

**Flail or scepter.** Appears in some depictions but is not as universally defining as the canid form or embalming association.

**Caduceus and palm.** Hellenistic/Roman attributes resulting from association with Hermes and Isiac procession.

### Plants, colors, numbers, days, and celestial bodies

Ancient evidence securely supports black as Anubis’s characteristic representational color. It does **not** establish a universally canonical sacred number, weekday, planet, crystal, tarot card, chakra, incense recipe, or zodiac sign.

The “Anubis plant” occurs in late magical recipes, but its botanical identification is uncertain. Modern lists assigning Anubis obsidian, onyx, Scorpio, Saturday, Pluto, the Moon, eight, or thirteen are modern occult correspondences unless an explicit ancient source is supplied.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

Egyptian religion did not possess one binding pan-Egyptian genealogy for Anubis.

| Version | Source and date | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Anubis as independent primordial funerary power, without named parents | Early inscriptions and Old Kingdom mortuary evidence | **Early attested.** Absence of parents is not itself a genealogy, but it is the earliest documentary situation. |
| Daughter Qebehut belongs to Anubis | Pyramid Texts, late Fifth–Sixth Dynasties | **Early attested.** The mother is not securely fixed by the relevant early passages. |
| Anubis associated with or generated by the solar/Enneadic family | Various funerary and temple texts | **Priestly systematization; variant.** Exact parentage differs. |
| Son of Osiris and Nephthys, conceived secretly; raised by Isis | Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris*, around 100 CE | **Late Greco-Egyptian literary version.** Extremely influential today but not the earliest Egyptian genealogy. |
| Son or companion of Isis within the Isiac family | Hellenistic and Roman cultic environments | **Syncretistic development.** Relationship language can be symbolic or cultic rather than genealogically consistent. |
| Husband of Anput, father of Qebehut | Local and later reconstructions, especially Cynopolitan triadic presentation | **Regional/late systematization.** Often presented too confidently in modern summaries. |
| Son of Seth or Bastet | Scattered late theological variants or modern compilations | **Variant requiring source-specific verification.** Not a pan-Egyptian doctrine. |

**Anput** (*jnpwt*) is the feminine form of the name/title, a goddess represented anthropomorphically or with canine symbolism. She can function as Anubis’s female counterpart. Calling her his universally acknowledged “wife” imports the Egyptian tendency to construct local divine pairs and triads into a single family system.

### Position within the pantheon

In early mortuary evidence, Anubis has considerable autonomous authority over burial and the necropolis. As Osiris becomes the dominant ruler of the dead, especially from the late Old Kingdom and Middle Kingdom onward, Anubis’s roles are reorganized around Osiris’s body and tribunal. “Demotion” is too simple: Anubis remains indispensable because Osiris’s resurrection requires embalming, bodily defense, guidance, and ritual mediation.

### Documented god–god interactions

The following catalogue separates explicit meetings or actions from mere co-presence. No claim is made that two deities “interacted” solely because both occur in the same pantheon.

#### Osiris

- **Anubis embalms, wraps, guards, or restores Osiris.** Funerary liturgies, Coffin Texts, Book of the Dead Spell 151, and late temple manuals. **Later canonical**, with roots in Old Kingdom mortuary function.
- **Anubis protects Osiris’s corpse or relics from Seth.** Papyrus Jumilhac, Ptolemaic/early Roman manuscript. **Regional priestly systematization.**
- **Anubis attends Osiris’s tribunal and introduces the justified dead.** New Kingdom and later Books of the Dead. **Later canonical.**
- **Osiris fathers Anubis by Nephthys.** Plutarch, around 100 CE. **Late Greco-Egyptian accretion.**

#### Nephthys

- **Nephthys conceives Anubis by Osiris and exposes him.** Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris*. **Late accretion.**
- **Nephthys mourns Osiris while Anubis performs funerary protection.** New Kingdom and later mortuary scenes and liturgies. **Canonical co-participation**, though not always a narrated encounter.

#### Isis

- **Isis finds and raises Anubis after Nephthys exposes him.** Plutarch. **Late Greco-Egyptian version.**
- **Anubis guards Isis and assists in the search or protection of Osiris.** Diodorus reports explanations involving Anubis/dogs as guardians. **Hellenistic interpretation.**
- **Isis mourns or revives Osiris while Anubis embalms him.** Mortuary liturgies and later temple mythography. **Later canonical/ritual.**
- **Anubis appears with Isis in Isiac processions and sanctuaries.** Greek and Roman literary, epigraphic, and visual sources. **Syncretistic cultic interaction.**

#### Seth

- **Anubis fights, captures, binds, mutilates, brands, or flays Seth for attacks on Osiris’s body.** Papyrus Jumilhac. **Regional priestly systematization.**
- **Anubis wears Seth’s flayed skin or turns the punishment into the origin of a ritual skin/feline marking.** Papyrus Jumilhac. **Late regional etiological myth.**
- **Anubis forces Seth to carry Osiris.** Jumilhac traditions. **Late regional variant.**
- The familiar generalization “Seth killed Osiris, so Anubis defeated Seth” is only secure when tied to such late local manuals; it is not a continuous Old Kingdom narrative.

#### Horus

- **Anubis and Horus jointly protect or serve Osiris and the dead.** Pyramid, Coffin, and later funerary texts. **Early/later canonical**, depending on passage.
- **Anubis and Horus attend the judgment or conduct the deceased.** New Kingdom and later vignettes. **Later canonical.**
- **Anubis assists Horus’s party against Seth.** Papyrus Jumilhac. **Regional late systematization.**
- No universal myth makes Anubis Horus’s brother; that relationship follows only from particular late genealogical combinations.

#### Thoth

- **Anubis operates or steadies the balance while Thoth records its result.** Book of the Dead judgment vignettes, especially Ani and Greenfield papyri. **Later canonical iconographic interaction.**
- **Anubis and Thoth cooperate in restoration, ritual knowledge, or judgment.** Coffin Texts, Books of the Dead, and temple texts. **Canonical but source-variable.**
- In magical papyri their names may be combined in invocations. **Roman syncretistic ritual use.**

#### Maat

- **Anubis weighs or tests the deceased’s heart against Maat or her feather.** New Kingdom and later vignettes. **Later canonical.**
- Maat is sometimes a goddess and sometimes the represented principle of truth/order; describing the scene as personal combat or negotiation between Anubis and Maat would be false.

#### Ammit

- **Anubis attends the weighing while Ammit waits to consume the condemned heart.** Books of the Dead, including Ani and Greenfield examples. **Later canonical iconographic co-action.**
- No securely attested narrative has Anubis fighting or commanding Ammit.

#### Qebehut

- **Qebehut, daughter of Anubis, refreshes, purifies, or assists the dead.** Pyramid Texts. **Early attested.**
- Later modern accounts frequently turn this into a detailed father-daughter embalming partnership beyond what the early passages narrate.

#### Anput

- **Anput appears as feminine counterpart or consort in local theological arrangements.** Later and regional evidence. **Regional systematization.**
- No major surviving narrative cycle describes courtship, marriage, or conflict between them.

#### Wepwawet

- **Anubis and Wepwawet appear together or in parallel in funerary and local theology.** Egyptian ritual and temple sources. **Attested association.**
- Late sources can combine functions or use “Anubis-Wepwawet.” **Syncretistic/local fusion.**
- Their simple identity is not universal.

#### Khentiamentiu

- The title/name “Foremost of the Westerners” passes into the developing Abydene Osirian complex and can be borne by Anubis or Osiris. **Historical absorption and titular transfer**, not necessarily a narrative meeting.
- Direct personal interaction is not securely established merely by shared title.

#### Ra

- Anubis appears within solar-afterlife transformations and can assist passage through nocturnal regions. **Funerary theological association.**
- Late systems sometimes call him a child of Ra. **Variant systematization**, not the earliest genealogy.

#### Geb and the Ennead

- Anubis gives or executes judgment in divine tribunals and is associated with Geb’s judicial authority in funerary spells. **Text-specific canonical interaction.**
- “Son of the Ennead” is a theological placement, not a stable biological descent statement.

#### Bata

- In the *Tale of the Two Brothers*, Anubis is Bata’s elder brother: he pursues him after a false accusation, learns the truth, reconciles with him, safeguards his heart, restores him, and eventually succeeds him as king. **Ramesside literary narrative.**
- Papyrus Jumilhac reinterprets Bata within local divine geography and can identify Bata with a pacified or transformed Seth. **Late regional reinterpretation.**

#### Hermes

- Anubis is functionally compared and then fused with Hermes as psychopomp and boundary-crosser. Plutarch, Roman iconography, and inscriptions. **Hellenistic/Roman syncretism.**
- Hermanubis is a historically produced divine form, not evidence of an original prehistoric identity.

#### Serapis

- Anubis appears in the Isiac divine family with Isis, Serapis/Osiris, and Harpocrates in Roman cult. **Syncretistic cultic association.**
- Claims that Serapis was always Anubis’s father are late local or modern harmonizations.

#### Harpocrates

- Joint appearance in Isiac groups and sanctuaries. **Hellenistic/Roman cultic co-presence.**
- A specific mythic encounter is not securely required by the evidence.

#### Greek or Roman underworld gods

- Comparisons with Hermes, Hades, Pluto, and Cerberus occur in classical interpretation and modern scholarship. Hermes-Anubis fusion is directly attested; Anubis-Cerberus identity is largely comparative and should not be treated as an Egyptian doctrine.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The divine embalming of Osiris

**Early core.** Old Kingdom mortuary evidence already makes Anubis a guardian and specialist of burial. The earliest sources do not provide a continuous narrative in which Seth murders Osiris and Anubis then invents mummification.

**Middle and New Kingdom elaboration.** Coffin Texts and mortuary liturgies increasingly locate Anubis within the Osirian drama. Osiris’s damaged or inert body must be reassembled, purified, anointed, wrapped, and awakened. Isis and Nephthys mourn; Anubis treats the body; Horus restores or vindicates his father; Thoth supplies ritual knowledge; the deceased is assimilated to Osiris.

**Book of the Dead formulation.** Spell 151 makes the relationship visual and performative. Anubis places his hands on the “lord of life,” wraps him through his craft, and speaks transformative words. The figure on the bier is simultaneously the deceased and an “Osiris N.” [UCL, Spell 151](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/literature/religious/hpres151.html)

**Late elaboration.** Temple manuals such as Papyrus Jumilhac narrate attacks on specific bodily relics and Anubis’s defense of them. These myths explain local shrines, landscape features, ritual implements, animals, and priestly taboos.

**Function.** The cycle legitimates embalming as divine action. A human body treated according to ritual is not merely preserved but conformed to the restored body of Osiris.

### Judgment and the weighing of the heart

The best-known modern “Anubis myth” is actually a ritual-textual scene rather than a single narrative.

The deceased enters the Hall of the Two Truths, addresses Osiris and divine assessors, denies specified wrongs, and undergoes evaluation. The heart is weighed against Maat or her feather. Anubis attends and adjusts the apparatus; Thoth records or announces the result; Ammit waits; Horus may lead the vindicated deceased to Osiris.

Important cautions:

- The most famous Papyrus of Ani image joins Spell 30B’s address to the heart with judgment imagery.
- Anubis is not necessarily the judge who issues the verdict.
- The scene developed through New Kingdom and later manuscripts; it should not be projected as a fixed illustrated procedure into the First Dynasty.
- The “42 sins” are better called denials before named assessors than a universal Egyptian equivalent of the Ten Commandments.
- The heart is not automatically “the soul”; it is a bodily, moral, intellectual, and personal center.

The Ani vignette is documented by the British Museum in detail. [British Museum EA10470,3](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10470-3)

### Anubis against Seth

Papyrus Jumilhac gives the fullest surviving Anubis-centered combat cycle. Seth or his followers threaten or steal Osiris’s corpse and relics. Anubis tracks, captures, binds, punishes, castrates, brands, or flays the enemy in variant episodes. Transformations into animals account for local sacred and forbidden species. Seth’s skin can become an insignia worn by Anubis, supplying an etiological explanation for feline skins, spots, or the imiut.

This material is violent and must not be sanitized. It includes divine mutilation, flaying, execution, and forced burden-bearing. But its date and locality are equally important: it is preserved in a late mythological manual serving nome theology. It is not evidence that every Egyptian period taught one standard “Anubis flays Seth” story.

### Nephthys, Osiris, Isis, and the birth of Anubis

Plutarch relates that Nephthys, wife of Typhon/Seth, secretly has intercourse with Osiris and conceives Anubis. Fearful of Seth, she exposes the child. Isis searches for him, finds him with the help of dogs, and raises him. Anubis becomes Isis’s guardian and helper.

This is the source of the dominant modern genealogy “Anubis, son of Osiris and Nephthys, adopted by Isis.” Its preservation around 100 CE makes it valuable evidence for Roman-period Egyptian and Greek interpretation. It is nevertheless a **late version**, not the default genealogy of the Pyramid Texts.

Plutarch further allegorizes:

- Isis as the visible or fertile region;
- Nephthys as the marginal or invisible region;
- Anubis as their horizon or boundary;
- his canine nature as the capacity to see or operate by night and day.

The allegory is Plutarch’s philosophical presentation of traditions available to him. It should not be silently converted into “what all Egyptians believed.”

### The Tale of the Two Brothers

The Ramesside tale begins with Anubis as a married landholder and Bata as his younger brother. Anubis’s unnamed wife attempts to seduce Bata. Rejected, she injures herself and claims Bata attacked her. Anubis waits to kill Bata, but cattle warn the younger brother and the sun god creates a crocodile-filled barrier. Bata protests his innocence and mutilates himself as proof of his lack of sexual intent. Anubis returns home, discovers the deception, kills his wife, and reconciles with Bata.

Bata thereafter undergoes a series of deaths and metamorphoses. The gods fashion a wife for him, but she betrays him to the king. Bata becomes a bull, then trees; a splinter enters the woman and she bears him anew as a royal child. He becomes king and appoints Anubis as successor.

The tale’s domestic Anubis is not simply the funerary god acting in ordinary life. Yet the names, transformations, bodily restoration, heart motif, divine court, and later local cult geography show that the text participates in religious mythmaking. Its reinterpretation in Papyrus Jumilhac demonstrates that Egyptian priests themselves could connect or rearrange these figures.

### Anubis as guide in the Isiac cycle

Diodorus records explanations in which dogs guide and protect Isis during her search for Osiris, hence dogs lead her festival procession. Plutarch makes Anubis the guardian of Isis, and Roman Isiac processions place the masked canine god prominently among divine images. Apuleius’s Anubis mediates between heavenly and infernal gods while carrying the caduceus.

This is not one Egyptian-language “myth of Hermanubis.” It is a Hellenistic and Roman complex joining Egyptian mortuary roles, Greek psychopomp imagery, public procession, and philosophical allegory.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult centers

#### Hardai/Cynopolis

Hardai, Greek **Kynopolis** or “City of the Dog,” in Middle Egypt, was the principal city associated with Anubis and capital of the seventeenth Upper Egyptian nome. Its exact archaeological identification and surviving monumental history are less secure than popular accounts imply. Ramesside administrative sources, including the Harris and Wilbour papyri, attest an Anubis establishment and local landholding.

Later local theology grouped Anubis with Anput and Qebehut. This is a **Cynopolitan arrangement**, not necessarily a nationwide family.

#### Saqqara Anubieion

North Saqqara contained an Anubis temple precinct and extensive subterranean dog catacombs integrated into the Sacred Animal Necropolis. Excavation publications distinguish the settlement, temple precinct, and animal galleries. [Artefacts of Excavation, 1978–79 Saqqara](https://egyptartefacts.griffith.ox.ac.uk/excavations/1978-79-saqqara-0/)

The catacombs reached peak activity in the Late and Ptolemaic periods. Archaeological investigation suggests millions of canid remains. Many were puppies or very young animals; the scale implies off-site breeding and organized procurement. Some animals were carefully mummified; others were bundled collectively or incompletely. [Nicholson, Ikram, and Mills](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/74282/1/The%20Catacombs%20of%20Anubis%20At%20North%20Saqqara%20REVISED%2022-7-2014a.pdf)

The evidence points to a votive economy:

1. breeders or procurers supplied animals;
2. temple personnel maintained or killed some of them;
3. embalmers prepared bodies or bundles;
4. pilgrims paid for burial as a gift to the god;
5. priests supervised storage and catacomb deposition.

This involved systematic animal killing on a very large scale. It should neither be concealed nor mislabeled as “human sacrifice.” Scholars debate whether pilgrims understood that their payments might fund the burial of neonatal animals rather than long-lived sacred temple dogs.

The catacombs were later quarried or exploited for materials, including animal remains used as fertilizer. Modern excavation began to reconstruct their scale and institutional setting. [Cambridge, *Antiquity*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/abs/div-classtitlethe-catacombs-of-anubis-at-north-saqqaradiv/C3E80BB63086CA5DFC48767DD3C29402)

#### Abydos

Abydos was principally associated with Khentiamentiu and later Osiris, but Anubis participated in its funerary theology. Shared titles and canid imagery document absorption and overlap, not proof that all three were originally identical.

#### Deir el-Bahri

Hatshepsut’s Eighteenth Dynasty mortuary temple contains an Upper and Lower Chapel of Anubis. The god receives offerings and participates in royal mortuary transformation. These are subsidiary sanctuaries within a larger royal-Amun complex, not an independent national headquarters.

#### Memphis and other necropoleis

Anubis was invoked across Egypt wherever burial, embalming, and tomb protection were needed. His ubiquity in private mortuary formulae is stronger evidence of broad religious reach than the number of surviving stand-alone temples.

### Priesthood and offices

Relevant titles include:

- priest or prophet of Anubis;
- lector priest reciting funerary formulae;
- embalmer and overseer of embalmers;
- *ḥry-sštꜣ*, “master/keeper of secrets,” often associated with restricted mortuary knowledge;
- personnel of the *sḥ-nṯr*, divine or embalming booth;
- in animal cults, procurers, preparators, administrators, and catacomb workers.

Priests could embody Anubis ritually. Images of jackal-headed attendants may represent the god, a masked priest, or deliberate fusion of divine and human action. Actual surviving jackal masks are rare; therefore the blanket statement “all embalmers wore Anubis masks” exceeds the evidence.

### Embalming practice

Anubis’s cult was embedded in funerary work rather than confined to congregational worship. Practices included:

- washing and purification;
- evisceration and treatment of organs;
- desiccation;
- anointing with oils and resins;
- wrapping and application of amulets;
- recitation over body parts;
- mouth-opening and transfiguration rites;
- protection of the coffin and tomb;
- offerings of bread, beer, cattle, fowl, incense, linen, and unguents.

The surgical and craft labor of embalmers must be distinguished from the mythic attribution “Anubis embalmed Osiris.” Ritual caused human specialists to enact divine precedent.

### Offerings and ordinary devotion

The widespread offering formula addressed to Anubis demonstrates invocation by elite tomb owners and, later, broader social groups. Small faience amulets, coffins bearing recumbent canids, mummy nets, stelae, and theophoric names provide material evidence beyond literary texts.

Names such as **Padi-Anubis/Pa-di-Inpu**, “He whom Anubis has given,” document personal devotion and temple affiliation. A Ptolemaic sarcophagus owner could identify himself as a scribe or priest attached to Anubis’s cult.

### Festivals and processions

Detailed calendars varied locally. Relevant activities included:

- funerary processions to the necropolis;
- Osirian Khoiak rites;
- opening-of-the-mouth rites;
- temple processions carrying divine images;
- Isiac processions in the Hellenistic and Roman world.

Diodorus says dogs led an Isiac festival procession. Apuleius places Anubis among the divine figures in an Isiac *pompa*. Neither source supplies a universal pharaonic “Festival of Anubis” with one stable date.

### Divination and magic

Roman-period manuals invoke Anubis for:

- lamp and vessel divination;
- direct vision through a boy-medium;
- obtaining divine names or answers;
- necromantic or funerary access;
- protection;
- coercive ritual.

The British Museum’s bilingual papyrus explicitly catalogues an “Invocation of Anubis.” [British Museum EA10588](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA10588-1)

These practices show continuity and transformation of Egyptian priestly knowledge. They do not validate modern claims that any improvised “Anubis oracle” reproduces an ancient rite.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and violence

No securely documented cult of Anubis practiced human sacrifice as a normal offering to him. First Dynasty royal subsidiary burials have generated debate about retainer sacrifice, but associating those deaths specifically with Anubis because a canid or imiut appears in royal ritual is unwarranted.

There is no secure evidence for “temple prostitution” in Anubis’s cult. Greek and Roman stories about sexual deception in Isiac temples belong to polemic and scandal narrative unless independently corroborated. They cannot be generalized into institutional sacred prostitution.

Documented violence does include:

- the mass killing of dogs or puppies for votive mummification;
- mythic mutilation and flaying of Seth in Papyrus Jumilhac;
- Roman suppression of Egyptian cult associations at particular times;
- late antique legal prohibition, temple closure, appropriation, and occasional iconoclasm.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Predynastic and Early Dynastic background, before approximately 2686 BCE

Desert canids, canid standards, and animal-skin standards belong to the symbolic world from which historically recognizable Anubis emerged. Royal labels and seals suggest an already institutionalized divine or ceremonial canid. Exact differentiation from Wepwawet and Khentiamentiu remains uncertain.

### Old Kingdom, approximately 2686–2181 BCE

Anubis is securely established in tomb inscriptions and royal funerary texts. His primary documentary sphere includes:

- permission for burial;
- the necropolis and “sacred land”;
- protection of the body;
- mortuary offerings;
- embalming space and wrappings;
- divine identification of the king.

The Pyramid Texts give him a celestial and transformational dimension while retaining strong bodily and cemetery functions.

### First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom, approximately 2181–1650 BCE

Mortuary texts spread beyond royal pyramids onto elite coffins. Osiris’s reach expands, and Anubis becomes increasingly integrated into Osirian resurrection. The process is not a clean displacement. Anubis’s independent authority survives in formulae, local cults, and the specialized necessity of embalming.

### Second Intermediate Period and New Kingdom, approximately 1650–1070 BCE

Books of the Dead, royal underworld books, decorated tombs, and temple chapels multiply Anubis’s visual record. His anthropomorphic canid-headed form becomes especially familiar. He attends mummies and judgment, receives royal offerings, and protects tomb doors and burial equipment.

The Ramesside *Tale of the Two Brothers* supplies a major literary Anubis-Bata narrative, while administrative papyri document land and temple structures connected with Anubis.

### Third Intermediate and Late Periods, approximately 1070–332 BCE

Funerary papyri, amulets, coffins, and statues proliferate. Animal cults grow substantially. The Saqqara Anubieion and dog catacombs become part of a vast votive economy. Anubis remains nationally recognizable while acquiring increasingly elaborate local forms.

### Ptolemaic period, 332–30 BCE

Egyptian temple culture remained productive under Macedonian rulers. Priests compiled nome monographs and ritual manuals that linked Anubis to local geography, Osiris relics, Sethian enemies, animals, and taboos. Papyrus Jumilhac belongs to this scholarly environment.

Greek-speaking worshippers encountered Anubis within the expanding cult of Isis and Serapis. Translation into Greek religious categories created new meanings without erasing Egyptian ones.

### Roman period, 30 BCE–fourth century CE

Anubis traveled with the Isiac cult throughout the Mediterranean. Images and dedications occur in Roman Italy and other provinces. He appeared in processions, literature, sculpture, gems, and magical papyri. His caduceus-bearing form, Hermanubis, emphasized mediation.

Roman responses were not uniform. Egyptian cults could be supported, regulated, expelled, restored, mocked, or politically instrumentalized. A 2021 study of Anubis’s Roman cult stresses the differing literary, epigraphic, and iconographic contexts and the use of animal worship as a polemical sign of foreignness. [Mastrocinque, “Religious Agency and Time Regimes”](https://brill.com/view/journals/nu/68/1/article-p39_2.xml)

### Christianization and disappearance of temple cult, fourth–sixth centuries CE

Imperial laws in 391 CE prohibited sacrifices and temple worship, including in Egypt. Local change was uneven: some sanctuaries closed, some were reused, some survived informally, and some images were damaged. The transformation cannot be reduced to one empire-wide night of destruction. Archaeological evidence must be assessed site by site. [Oxford project on Late Antique religious change](https://www.ocla.ox.ac.uk/late-antique-egypt-and-holy-land-archaeology-history-and-religious-change)

Anubis’s public sacrificial cult disappeared with the Egyptian temple system. His name and image persisted in manuscripts, antiquarian memory, magical traditions, and Christian polemic.

A proposed relationship between Hermanubis and canine-headed **Saint Christopher** has attracted scholarship, but direct descent is not proven. Similar iconography, psychopomp-like functions, and eastern transmission make influence possible; they do not establish that Christopher “is Christianized Anubis.”

### Islamic and medieval Egypt

No evidence demonstrates continuous organized worship of Anubis through the Islamic period. Ancient monuments, animal cemeteries, magical objects, and stories remained in the landscape, but assigning any canine folkloric figure to a disguised Anubis requires explicit documentary support.

Arabic authors preserved knowledge of Egyptian antiquities but generally did not maintain Anubis as a living cult god. Claims of secret unbroken priesthoods are modern esoteric narratives.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

European antiquarians encountered Anubis through Greek and Latin literature, Roman sculptures, gems, and unreadable hieroglyphs. Interpretations were filtered through Plutarch, Hermeticism, biblical chronology, and allegory.

After Champollion’s decipherment in the 1820s, Egyptian-language evidence gradually displaced purely classical accounts. Museum acquisitions—including funerary papyri and animal mummies—made Anubis visually familiar. Budge’s editions and translations, although now philologically outdated, spread the image of Anubis through English-language occultism and popular culture.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Professional Egyptology refined:

- Egyptian grammar and the reading *jnpw*;
- distinctions among Anubis, Wepwawet, and Khentiamentiu;
- the development of mortuary corpora;
- the archaeology of the Anubieion;
- the late, regional character of Papyrus Jumilhac;
- the Mediterranean history of Hermanubis.

Modern Kemetic and Pagan worship represents revival or reconstruction, not demonstrated institutional continuity from an ancient temple. Independent devotees and organized groups invoke Anpu as psychopomp, protector, embalmer, guide in mourning, or guardian of ethical self-examination. Reliable population figures for Anubis-specific worship do not exist.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Egyptology and museum culture

The excavation of Tutankhamun’s tomb in 1922 made the recumbent black Anubis shrine globally recognizable. Museum displays, schoolbooks, jewelry, and reproductions subsequently made Anubis one of the few Egyptian gods instantly identified by a general audience.

This visibility altered emphasis: the black canine form and heart-weighing scene became more culturally dominant than his Old Kingdom offering formulae or local temple titles.

### Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in Britain in the late nineteenth century, combined Kabbalah, Christian esotericism, tarot, Rosicrucianism, alchemy, and Egyptological imagery. Its initiation rituals assigned Egyptian divine forms to ritual officers and treated Anubis as a threshold guardian and interpreter of judgment imagery.

These rites are **modern ceremonial magic**, not translations of ancient Egyptian temple ceremonies. Their “double Anubis,” tarot, elemental, and lodge correspondences depend on nineteenth-century occult synthesis and early Egyptological publications. Alex Owen’s study places the Golden Dawn in its specific British occult and social setting. [Owen, *The Place of Enchantment*](https://www.howardnema.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/9780226642048.UChicago-Press.Place-of-Enchantment_-British-Occultism-and-the-Culture-of-the-Modern-The.Alex-Owen.Dec2006.pdf)

### Thelema

Aleister Crowley’s **Liber Samekh**, composed in 1921, adapts the ancient “Headless” or *Akephalos* ritual of PGM V into a Thelemic operation for “Knowledge and Conversation of the Holy Guardian Angel.” Crowley’s Egyptianizing interpretations and occasional Anubis language belong to his modern magical system. They should not be attributed to the pharaonic cult merely because their remote source lies in a Roman Egyptian magical papyrus.

### Modern Pagan and Kemetic religion

Kemetic reconstruction and revival emerged through multiple independent streams in the later twentieth century. Devotees may use:

- reconstructed Egyptian offerings and hymns;
- modern devotional prayers;
- museum or Egyptological translations;
- household shrines;
- funerary or grief-related service;
- personal divine relationships.

The use of “Anpu” expresses a preference for an Egyptian rather than Greek name, but it does not restore a certainly known ancient pronunciation.

Modern teachings that Anubis is exclusively gentle, exclusively frightening, patron of veterinarians, patron of goth subculture, guide of “shadow work,” or ruler of a specified tarot card are contemporary devotional developments unless ancient documentation is supplied.

### New Age and psychological interpretations

Jungian and post-Jungian writers often read Anubis as:

- the shadow;
- initiator into the unconscious;
- guide through ego death;
- guardian of liminality;
- archetype of grief.

These are modern psychological interpretations. Jung did not discover a hidden Egyptian doctrine that Anubis “is the shadow archetype.” Such readings can be historically studied as twentieth-century reception without being passed off as translations of Egyptian texts.

Crystal, chakra, planetary, and angelic correspondences are likewise recent unless their modern source is named. Common lists connecting Anubis with obsidian, smoky quartz, Pluto, Scorpio, Saturday, or the root chakra are not ancient Egyptian canons.

### Literature, television, film, comics, and games

Influential appearances include:

- Tim Powers, **_The Anubis Gates_** (1983): Anubis supplies Egyptological atmosphere and supernatural threat rather than a reconstruction of his cult.
- *Stargate SG-1*: Anubis becomes a technologically powerful extraterrestrial antagonist, part of the franchise’s “ancient gods as aliens” premise.
- *The Mummy Returns* (2001): armies of canine “Anubis warriors” helped popularize Anubis as a martial monster. Ancient sources do not describe a standardized species of massed Anubis-headed soldiers.
- *Gods of Egypt* (2016): adapts the weighing of the heart into fantasy cosmology.
- Rick Riordan’s *Kane Chronicles*: reimagines Anubis for young-adult fiction and romance.
- Neil Gaiman’s *American Gods*: uses Egyptian death gods in a modern American funerary setting.
- Enki Bilal’s *Nikopol* works and their film adaptation: deploy Anubis within speculative political Egyptomania.
- *Assassin’s Creed Origins*: uses Anubis in dreamlike trials and game combat while reconstructing parts of Ptolemaic Egypt.
- *Smite*, *Age of Mythology*, *Yu-Gi-Oh!*, and numerous role-playing games: emphasize magical combat, curses, or monster design.

A recent academic survey observes that popular culture tends to privilege animal-headed gods, pyramids, mummies, curses, and ancient evil, often making Anubis a villain or cosmic power rather than a mortuary caretaker. [Aegyptiaca, “When the Egyptian Gods Ruled the (Future) World”](https://journals.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/index.php/aegyp/article/view/105658/104370)

### Commercial imagery

Anubis appears in tattoos, fashion, security-company logos, cryptocurrency branding, military insignia, toys, Halloween décor, and AI-generated “Egyptian” art. The visual formula usually combines:

- muscular human body;
- black jackal head;
- gold-and-blue costume;
- staff or sword;
- pyramids or scales.

The highly muscular armored warrior is principally a modern fantasy design. Ancient anthropomorphic images ordinarily use conventional Egyptian male proportions and ritual dress rather than plate armor.

### Nationalist and political use

Ancient Egyptian imagery is used in modern Egyptian cultural nationalism and tourism, generally as heritage rather than revived theology. Anubis can symbolize guardianship, indigenous antiquity, or mastery of death.

Afrocentric movements have sometimes used Anubis as an emblem of African antiquity, a historically defensible geographic framing when it recognizes Egypt as an African civilization. Claims derived from invented word division, extraterrestrial ancestry, or undocumented “Anu priesthoods” are not supported by Egyptian textual evidence.

No major, stable extremist Anubis cult is securely documented. Isolated online accounts and symbols should not be inflated into an organized movement without evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### From classical authors to decipherment

Before hieroglyphic decipherment, European knowledge depended heavily on Diodorus, Plutarch, Apuleius, Roman sculpture, and allegorical handbooks. Consequently, the Osiris–Nephthys parentage and Hermes comparison were long treated as primary definitions.

Champollion’s decipherment opened the much older Egyptian record. Old Kingdom offering texts and Pyramid Texts showed that Anubis existed millennia before Plutarch’s genealogy.

### Major editions and discoveries

- Publication of Pyramid Texts by Gaston Maspero and Kurt Sethe established the Old Kingdom textual corpus.
- Adriaan de Buck’s *Egyptian Coffin Texts* provided the standard copies of Middle Kingdom spells.
- Édouard Naville and E. A. Wallis Budge published major Book of the Dead materials. Budge remains accessible but is no longer the preferred authority for grammar, dating, or translation.
- James P. Allen’s translation and reorganization of the Pyramid Texts reflects modern philology. [Allen PDF](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/AllenPyramid.pdf)
- Jacques Vandier’s publication of Papyrus Jumilhac transformed understanding of local Anubis mythology.
- Excavations by Walter B. Emery, Harry Smith, Sue Davies, Paul Nicholson, Salima Ikram, and colleagues transformed knowledge of the Saqqara Anubieion and canine-mummy economy. [Cardiff project](https://www.cardiff.ac.uk/research/explore/find-a-project/view/1145229-the-catacombs-of-anubis)

### Principal debates

#### Is the animal a jackal or dog?

Older scholarship routinely used “jackal.” Zoological revision has favored “canid” or “African golden wolf” in some discussions. Egyptian categories need not correspond to modern species taxonomy. Iconography is stylized.

#### Did Anubis precede Osiris as ruler of the dead?

Anubis is securely attested earlier in surviving mortuary evidence. But the proposition that he once occupied every later Osirian function is reconstruction. The evidence supports early prominence and later specialization, not a recoverable constitutional history of a single “office of underworld king.”

#### Is Khentiamentiu Anubis?

Sometimes a title is applied to Anubis; elsewhere Khentiamentiu is a separately named Abydene deity later absorbed into Osiris. Identity varies by date and context.

#### Is Anput an ancient wife in a fixed triad?

Anput is real and her name is the feminine counterpart of Anubis. The neat family “Anubis + Anput = Qebehut” is principally a local or later systematization and should not override earlier ambiguity.

#### Did Anubis invent mummification?

Egyptian ritual treats him as the paradigmatic divine embalmer, especially of Osiris. “Invented mummification” is a modern narrative compression, not a sentence documented in the earliest sources.

#### Did embalmers wear jackal masks?

Ritual impersonation is plausible and visually supported, and some later images may represent masked priests. Evidence for routine mask-wearing by every embalmer is insufficient.

#### Does black mean death or rebirth?

Both mortuary darkness/resin and regenerative black soil are plausible within Egyptian symbolism. A universal one-word code is reductive.

#### Is the heart-weighing Old Kingdom doctrine?

Judgment and moral accounting have older antecedents, but the fully developed illustrated weighing familiar from Ani and Hunefer is New Kingdom and later.

#### Does Hermanubis descend directly into Saint Christopher?

Possible iconographic or functional influence has been argued. A continuous genealogical chain is unproven, and dog-headed Christopher traditions have their own manuscript and linguistic history.

### Lost and fragmentary evidence

The archaeological record is radically incomplete:

- most temple libraries vanished;
- organic papyri survive unevenly;
- Hardai’s principal Anubis temple is poorly preserved;
- tomb robbery removed cult equipment from context;
- early excavation often recorded animal remains inadequately;
- museum dispersal separated papyri and coffins;
- early canid imagery is frequently unlabeled.

Consequently, absence of a surviving early genealogy does not prove that no genealogy existed. It does mean that no specific unattested genealogy may be asserted as fact.

### Discredited or unsupported popular theories

- Anubis was an extraterrestrial or genetically engineered being.
- “Anubis” means “Anu-ibis.”
- The Sphinx was originally Anubis, presented as established fact. Proposals relating the Sphinx to a canid exist, but there is no scholarly consensus and its Fourth Dynasty leonine identity remains the standard interpretation.
- Anubis was a historical king later deified.
- His cult operated human-breeding institutions or temple prostitution.
- The Papyrus of Ani is a single Egyptian “Bible.”
- Anubis alone judges every soul and sends sinners to hell.
- Anubis and Wepwawet were always the same god.
- The Osiris–Nephthys parentage is the oldest Egyptian account.
- Modern tarot, crystal, zodiac, and chakra correspondences are pharaonic teachings.
- A secret Anubis priesthood survived continuously from antiquity to modern occult orders.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Event in the documented record |
|---|---|
| Predynastic period, before c. 3100 BCE | Canids and desert animals appear in mortuary and elite imagery. Direct identification as Anubis is often uncertain. |
| First Dynasty, early third millennium BCE | Royal labels and standards include canid/imiut imagery; an Aha label has been interpreted as recording the fashioning or “birth” of an Anubis image. |
| Early–mid Old Kingdom | Tomb offering formulae securely invoke Anubis with necropolis and embalming titles. |
| Reign of Unas, c. twenty-fourth century BCE | First surviving inscribed Pyramid Text corpus; Anubis participates in royal purification, protection, burial, and transformation. |
| Sixth Dynasty | Pyramid Texts in additional royal and queens’ pyramids expand the evidence; Qebehut is identified as Anubis’s daughter. |
| First Intermediate Period–Middle Kingdom | Coffin Texts disseminate and elaborate Anubis’s mortuary roles among non-royal elites. |
| Middle Kingdom | Anubis becomes increasingly integrated into developed Osirian resurrection theology without disappearing as an autonomous cult recipient. |
| Early New Kingdom, after c. 1550 BCE | Book of the Dead tradition develops; judgment and mummy-protection compositions become widespread. |
| Reign of Hatshepsut, fifteenth century BCE | Anubis chapels at Deir el-Bahri provide major temple iconography. |
| Reign of Tutankhamun, fourteenth century BCE | Recumbent black Anubis shrine deposited in KV62. |
| c. 1300 BCE | Papyrus of Hunefer supplies major mummy and judgment scenes. |
| c. 1250 BCE | Papyrus of Ani shows Anubis steadying the balance while Thoth records the result. |
| c. 1200 BCE | Papyrus D’Orbiney preserves the *Tale of the Two Brothers*, featuring Anubis and Bata. |
| Twelfth–eleventh centuries BCE | Ramesside administrative papyri attest temple estates and local institutions associated with Anubis/Hardai. |
| c. 950–930 BCE | Greenfield Papyrus depicts Anubis and Thoth coordinating judgment. |
| First millennium BCE | Anubis amulets, coffins, stelae, and animal cult activity become especially abundant. |
| Saite–Ptolemaic periods | Saqqara’s Anubieion and dog catacombs operate on a massive scale. |
| 332 BCE onward | Macedonian rule accelerates Greek-language interpretation and international Isiac cult development. |
| First century BCE | Diodorus records Anubis as canine guardian of Isis and Osiris and reports dogs leading an Isiac procession. |
| Late Ptolemaic/early Roman period | Papyrus Jumilhac compiles regional myths in which Anubis protects Osiris and violently defeats Seth. |
| c. 100 CE | Plutarch records the Nephthys–Osiris parentage, Isis’s adoption of Anubis, horizon allegory, and Hermes comparison. |
| Second century CE | Apuleius describes canine-headed Anubis with caduceus and palm in an Isiac procession. |
| Second–fourth centuries CE | Greek and Demotic magical papyri invoke Anubis in divination and syncretistic ritual. |
| 391 CE | Theodosian legislation prohibits temple sacrifice and worship in Egypt; implementation remains locally uneven. |
| Fourth–sixth centuries CE | Egyptian temple institutions decline or close under Christianization; public Anubis cult disappears. |
| Medieval period | Anubis persists through classical manuscripts, objects, polemic, and antiquarian memory, not demonstrable continuous temple worship. |
| 1744 | A Greek–Latin/English scholarly edition of Plutarch’s *On Isis and Osiris* illustrates early-modern dependence on classical evidence. |
| 1820s | Hieroglyphic decipherment begins making Egyptian-language Anubis evidence historically recoverable. |
| Late nineteenth century | Pyramid, coffin, and Book of the Dead materials are increasingly edited; Golden Dawn ritual transforms Anubis into a modern initiatory guardian. |
| 1895 | Budge publishes the Papyrus of Ani with text and translation, greatly influencing popular and occult reception. |
| 1922 | Discovery of Tutankhamun’s tomb brings the recumbent Anubis shrine to worldwide attention. |
| 1920s–1930s | Modern editions of magical papyri and Pyramid Texts expand access to Anubis’s textual record. |
| Mid-twentieth century | Publication of Papyrus Jumilhac and Coffin Text volumes sharpens distinctions between early mortuary roles and late local mythology. |
| 1970s–present | Renewed excavation and study of the Saqqara Anubieion and dog catacombs reconstruct the animal-cult economy. |
| Late twentieth century | Modern Kemetic, Pagan, occult, literary, cinematic, and gaming reinterpretations proliferate. |
| 1983 | Tim Powers publishes *The Anubis Gates*. |
| 1990s–2000s | Television and film increasingly recast Anubis as alien overlord, monster, or military power. |
| 2010s–2020s | Archaeometric research into canine mummies and reception studies place Anubis within animal-cult economics and global popular culture. |
| Present | Anubis remains an object of Egyptological study, museum display, reconstructed devotion, occult ritual, and commercial/media reinvention. No evidence establishes uninterrupted institutional worship from antiquity. |
