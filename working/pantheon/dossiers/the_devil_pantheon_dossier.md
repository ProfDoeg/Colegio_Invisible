# The Devil: Pantheon Dossier

**Requested filename:** `the_devil_pantheon_dossier.md`

## Scope and method

“The Devil” is not a divine being with one continuously attested identity. It is a historically layered Christian figure assembled from several earlier categories and characters: the Hebrew *śāṭān* (“adversary/accuser”); Second Temple Jewish leaders of hostile spirits such as Belial, Mastema, and Azazel; the Greek New Testament’s *Satanas* and *diabolos*; the apocalyptic dragon and ancient serpent; late-antique theories of angelic rebellion; medieval Lucifer, tempter, jailer, and lord of demons; early-modern witches’ alleged master; vernacular trickster; and, from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries onward, a literary or religious emblem of revolt and individual autonomy.

This dossier therefore treats “the Devil” as a **tradition-defined composite**. An identification belongs to the date of the source making it. Thus Genesis’s serpent is not called Satan in Genesis; Isaiah’s fallen morning star is addressed as a human king in its immediate literary setting; Job’s accuser remains a member of the divine court; and Azazel, Belial, Mastema, Samael, Beelzebul, and Lucifer cannot simply be declared timeless aliases. Their equations with the Devil developed unevenly.

Dates below ordinarily refer to scholarly estimates of composition, not to the age of the narrated events. Biblical books often survive in manuscripts centuries later than composition. “Canonical” indicates reception in Christian biblical canons, whose precise contents differ among churches.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

In most historic Christian theologies, the Devil is a created, incorporeal personal intelligence: originally a good angel, subsequently corrupted by free choice, leader or exemplar of rebellious angels, tempter and deceiver of humanity, accuser, promoter of sin and falsehood, adversary of Christ and the Church, and a creature ultimately subject to God’s sovereignty and judgment. He is not normally a god, coeternal evil principle, or independent ruler equal to God. New Testament and later Christian sources nevertheless give him considerable provisional authority as “ruler of this world,” “god of this age,” and leader of hostile spiritual powers.

That synthesis is not present in the earliest Hebrew evidence. It emerged by combining texts and traditions, especially Genesis 3; Job 1–2; Zechariah 3; Isaiah 14; Ezekiel 28; Wisdom 2:24; Jewish apocalyptic demonologies; the Gospel temptation and exorcism narratives; John 8; Luke 10; 2 Corinthians 4 and 11; Ephesians 6; 1 Peter 5; 1 John; Jude; 2 Peter; and Revelation 12 and 20.

| **Mephistopheles** | German/Neo-Latin forms | Demon of the Faust tradition, first prominent in sixteenth-century Faust books. | **Old Nick, Old Harry, Old Scratch, Black Man, Clootie, Auld Hornie** | English and Scots folklore | Euphemistic or vernacular names, especially early modern and modern. “Black Man” in witch-trial testimony does not always indicate skin color; it could mean a dark-clothed or sinister apparition. |
| **Iblis/al-Shayṭān** | Arabic إبليس / الشيطان | Islamic figures frequently compared with the Christian Devil. Christianity and Islam share late-antique narrative environments, but Iblis is not simply a Christian name for Satan: Qur’anic texts have their own classifications and interpretive disputes, including whether Iblis is a jinn or angelic being. This dossier does not collapse the Islamic figure into the Christian one. |
| **Baphomet** | Medieval Latin/Old French forms | Originally a likely corruption of “Mahomet” in medieval anti-Muslim discourse and Templar accusations. Éliphas Lévi’s goat-headed androgyne of 1854–56 and the later goat-pentagram “Sigil of Baphomet” are modern occult constructions. The Church of Satan documents the sigil’s pre-LaVeyan graphic history and its own adoption in [its historical account](https://churchofsatan.com/history-sigil-of-baphomet/). ### Classification changes

1. **Hebrew court official:** Job and Zechariah portray “the accuser” within YHWH’s court.
2. **Independent inciter or hostile supernatural actor:** possibly 1 Chronicles 21.
3. **Second Temple enemy leader:** Belial, Mastema, and related figures lead spirits or the lot of darkness, although traditions remain plural.
4. **New Testament eschatological adversary:** tempter, demon-ruler, deceiver, persecutor, and defeated cosmic enemy.
5. **Patristic fallen angel:** created good but self-corrupted; Christianity rejects ontological dualism.
6. **Medieval prince of demons/Hell:** increasingly individualized visually and narratively.
7. **Folkloric trickster or contract-maker:** dangerous but frequently defeatable by saints, clergy, peasants, and clever women.
8. **Early-modern witches’ alleged master:** central to elite demonological constructions of the witches’ sabbath and pact.
9. **Romantic rebel:** from Miltonic reception through Blake, Byron, Shelley, and later countercultures.
10. **Modern symbol or deity:** atheistic Satanism treats Satan as symbol; some theistic Satanists and Luciferians treat Satan or Lucifer as an actual spiritual intelligence.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest recoverable evidence

No pre-Christian inscription or image securely identifies the later Christian Devil. The earliest evidence consists of Hebrew literary uses of *śāṭān*.

- **Numbers 22:22, 32:** the angel of YHWH stands “as an adversary” (*ləśāṭān*) against Balaam. This is not the Devil; it proves that the noun could describe a divinely authorized obstructer.
- **1 Samuel 29:4; 2 Samuel 19:22; 1 Kings 5:4; 11:14, 23, 25:** human political or military adversaries are called *śāṭān*.
- **Zechariah 3:1–2:** usually assigned to the early Persian period, around 520–518 BCE. The high priest Joshua stands before YHWH’s angel while *ha-śśāṭān* accuses him. YHWH rebukes the accuser. The text establishes a heavenly prosecutorial figure but not a rebel ruler of evil. The surviving textual record includes the Masoretic tradition and ancient Greek translations; the complete medieval Hebrew codices are much later than composition.
- **Job 1–2:** dating is disputed, commonly somewhere from the sixth to fourth centuries BCE for the poetic/book form. The accuser attends the divine council, questions Job’s disinterested piety, and receives limited permission to destroy his property, children, and health. He cannot act beyond God’s authorization. [Sefaria’s Hebrew text](https://www.sefaria.org/Job.1?lang=bi&vhe=hebrew%7CMiqra_according_to_the_Masorah&with=Lexicon) visibly retains *ha-śśāṭān* rather than requiring a proper name.
- **1 Chronicles 21:1:** probably Persian or early Hellenistic period. Satan/an adversary incites David’s census. Its alteration of 2 Samuel 24:1 is important evidence for transferring a morally troublesome action away from God, but the grammar does not alone settle whether the term is already a personal name.

### Hebrew Bible passages later incorporated into the Devil’s biography

- **Genesis 3:** an intelligent serpent persuades the woman to eat from the forbidden tree. It is called a creature made by YHWH, not Satan, Devil, fallen angel, or Lucifer. Wisdom 2:24, Revelation 12:9, and patristic exegesis enabled its later Satanic identification.
- **Isaiah 14:4–21:** a taunt against the king of Babylon includes the fall of *hêlēl ben-šaḥar*. The king seeks to ascend above divine stars but is brought to Sheol. Application to Satan is an interpretive afterlife.
- **Ezekiel 28:1–19:** laments the ruler/king of Tyre using Edenic, cherubic, and jeweled imagery. Later Christians read the “anointed cherub” corrupted by pride as Satan before his fall. In its literary setting the target is Tyrian royal arrogance.
- **Isaiah 27:1; Psalm 74:13–14; Job 40–41:** Leviathan and dragon combat imagery later fed Devil iconography.
- **Zechariah 3:** its rebuke formula reappears in Jude 9.

### Second Temple Jewish corpus

This period supplies much of the conceptual bridge between the biblical accuser and the New Testament enemy.

- **1 Enoch, Book of the Watchers (1 Enoch 1–36):** Aramaic core conventionally dated to the third century BCE; fragments survive among the Dead Sea Scrolls, while the complete work survives principally in Ethiopic. Watchers descend, take women, generate giants, and teach forbidden arts. Shemihazah leads the oath; Azazel teaches weaponry and ornamentation and receives distinctive punishment. The story is related to Genesis 6:1–4 but is not simply a story of Satan’s primordial fall.
- **Jubilees:** Hebrew work, commonly mid-second century BCE, fragmentarily preserved at Qumran and fully in Ethiopic. The Prince of Mastema asks to retain one tenth of the evil spirits after the Flood, participates in testing Abraham, and seeks to kill Moses. Mastema approximates several later Satanic functions while remaining within divine permission.
- **Wisdom of Solomon 2:24:** Greek Jewish work, generally first century BCE or early first century CE: “through the devil’s envy death entered the world.” Whether *diabolos* here means Satan or the slanderous serpent is debated, but Christians widely read it as identifying Satan behind Genesis 3.
- **Life of Adam and Eve/Apocalypse of Moses:** a family of Greek, Latin, Armenian, Georgian, and Slavonic recensions, probably built on a Jewish work from roughly the first century CE, with extensive Christian transmission. Some recensions explain Satan’s fall as refusal to honor Adam; others emphasize envy. These traditions supplied a motive absent from Genesis.
- **Dead Sea Scrolls:** approximately third century BCE to first century CE. Sectarian texts place the community amid conflict between the spirits/lots of truth and perversity. Belial leads darkness in the Community Rule, War Scroll, Damascus Document, and 11QMelchizedek. The [Encyclopedia of the Dead Sea Scrolls](https://www.rexresearch1.com/DeadSeaScrolls/EncyclopediaDeadSeaScrollsSchiffman.pdf) surveys Belial, Mastema, Melchiresha, and related leaders; a focused study notes that Belial is the Scrolls’ most frequent evil-leader designation and that proposed restorations of “Satan” in damaged magical texts can be uncertain ([Guerra dissertation](https://etheses.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/7877/4/Guerra17PhD.pdf)).
- **Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs:** composite Jewish and Christian textual history, difficult to date cleanly; Beliar appears as an eschatological enemy.
- **Testament of Job:** probably Hellenistic or Roman period, retelling Job with Satan as more overt enemy and shape-shifter.
- **Testament of Solomon:** Greek demonological work with layers perhaps from the first through fourth centuries CE and later. Beelzeboul is prince of demons; Solomon subdues spirits by a divine seal. It is influential in magical and demonological reception but not canonical.

### New Testament corpus

The New Testament writings were composed approximately 50–120 CE, with individual dates disputed. Earliest substantial codices are chiefly fourth century, although earlier papyri preserve portions.

- **Pauline letters:**  
  - 1 Thessalonians 2:18: Satan obstructs Paul.  
  - 1 Corinthians 5:5 and 7:5: Satan can afflict or tempt.  
  - 2 Corinthians 2:11: Satan devises schemes; 4:4 has “god of this age”; 11:3 recalls the serpent, while 11:14 says Satan disguises himself as an angel of light; 12:7 gives Paul a “messenger of Satan.”  
  - Romans 16:20 promises that God will soon crush Satan beneath believers’ feet.
- **Mark, Matthew, Luke:** Satan/the Devil tempts Jesus; is ruler associated with demons; removes seed from hearers; binds the afflicted; enters Judas; demands to sift Peter; and is said to fall like lightning. Mark 3/Matthew 12/Luke 11 equate the charge that Jesus works by Beelzebul with the absurdity of Satan casting out Satan.
- **Matthew 25:41:** eternal fire is “prepared for the devil and his angels.”
- **John:** the Devil is murderer and father of falsehood (8:44); Satan enters Judas (13:27); the ruler of this world is cast out and judged.
- **Acts:** Satan fills Ananias’s heart (5:3); Elymas is a “son of the Devil” (13:10); Christian mission turns people from Satan’s power to God (26:18).
- **Ephesians and Colossians:** cosmic powers and the “prince of the power of the air” are integrated into spiritual warfare; Colossians emphasizes Christ’s triumph over rulers and authorities.
- **Pastoral Epistles:** Devil as ensnarer and slanderer; opponents may be held captive to his will.
- **Hebrews 2:14:** the Devil has “the power of death,” which Christ destroys through death.
- **James 4:7 and 1 Peter 5:8–9:** believers must resist the Devil; 1 Peter compares him to a roaring lion.
- **1 John:** Devil sins “from the beginning”; Cain is “of the evil one”; Christ appears to destroy the Devil’s works.
- **Jude 9:** Michael disputes with the Devil over Moses’s body but refuses direct condemnation, saying, “The Lord rebuke you.” The lost or noncanonical source behind the scene is usually associated with the *Assumption/Testament of Moses*.
- **2 Peter 2:4:** sinning angels are cast into Tartarus; the text does not name Satan as their leader.
- **Revelation:** provides the most influential canonical synthesis. Revelation 12 explicitly identifies the great dragon, ancient serpent, Devil, and Satan, and calls him world-deceiver and accuser. Michael expels him and his angels from heaven; the dragon attacks the woman and her offspring and empowers the beasts. Revelation 20 binds him in the abyss, releases him briefly, and casts him into the lake of fire. The complete chapter is available in the [NRSVue text of Revelation 12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?interface=amp&search=Revelation+12&version=NRSVUE).

### Early Christian and patristic corpus

- **Ignatius, Polycarp, Justin Martyr, and the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* (second century):** Devil as persecutor, deceiver, and ruler behind idolatry.
- **Justin Martyr (mid-second century):** pagan gods are reclassified as demons; this helped Christianity absorb non-Christian cult figures into a Devil-governed demonology.
- **Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* and *Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching* (c. 180–190):** Satan is an apostate angel behind the serpent, jealous of humanity and active in Cain. Irenaeus explicitly distinguishes serpent and hidden angel while uniting them narratively ([*Demonstration* 16–17](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/demonstrationapostolic.html)).
- **Tertullian (late second–early third century):** the Devil imitates or counterfeits divine rites and operates through pagan religion.
- **Origen, *On First Principles* (c. 220–230):** systematizes rational spiritual creatures’ fall through free will while acknowledging that church teaching had not clearly defined every detail concerning the Devil and his angels ([text](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/anf04.vi.v.i.html)).
- **Athanasius, *Life of Antony* (c. 356–362):** demons tempt, terrify, assault, and shape-shift before the ascetic; the Devil is ultimately weak against Christ and disciplined holiness.
- **Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechetical Lectures* (c. 350):** calls the Devil an archangel who became Devil through slander and Satan through adversarial action ([Lecture II](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf207.ii.vi.html)).
- **Augustine, *City of God* and other works (early fifth century):** the Devil was created good and became evil through will, especially pride; evil is privation rather than an independent substance. Augustine reads Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 as figures of Satan while arguing against Manichaean dualism ([*City of God* XI.15](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf102.iv.XI.15.html)).
- **Pseudo-Dionysius, *Celestial Hierarchy* (late fifth–early sixth century):** influential angelic orders later used to locate the fallen angels’ former ranks.
- **Gregory the Great, *Moralia in Job* (sixth century):** expansive moral and ecclesiological interpretation of Job’s adversary and Leviathan.

### Medieval and early-modern corpus

- **Old English *Genesis B* (probably ninth century, surviving in the Junius Manuscript, c. 1000):** elaborates Satan’s pride, rebellion, fall, and revenge through Adam and Eve.
- **Mystery plays (fourteenth–sixteenth centuries):** present the Devil bodily, noisily, and often comically; theatrical Devils help standardize horns, tails, grotesque masks, and spectacular Hell-mouths.
- **Dante, *Inferno* XXXIV (c. 1308–1321):** Lucifer is a gigantic, immobilized, three-faced being frozen at Hell’s center, chewing Judas, Brutus, and Cassius. Dante’s Devil is not a fiery king freely torturing souls but the most confined prisoner in the cosmos.
- **Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* (thirteenth century):** systematizes angelic intellect, will, fall, hierarchy, temptation, and limited demonic powers.
- **Regino of Prüm’s *Canon Episcopi* (c. 906), later canon-law collections:** describes women who believe they ride at night with Diana, treating the experience as diabolic delusion. Later demonologists increasingly construed witches’ assemblies as physically real. The manuscript and redactional problem is summarized in this [textual overview](https://www.divinityatlas.com/esoteric-texts/canon-episcopi).
- **Papal bull *Summis desiderantes affectibus* (1484):** authorized inquisitors Heinrich Kramer and Jacob Sprenger in specified German territories and endorsed claims of harmful sorcery. It did not create European witch persecution by itself.
- **Heinrich Kramer, *Malleus maleficarum* (first printed 1486/87):** makes Devil, witch, and divine permission the three necessary elements of witchcraft; elaborates pacts, sexual demons, maleficium, and prosecution. A digitized 1487 witness is held by the [University Library Basel](https://www.e-rara.ch/bau_1/content/titleinfo/28651115); the familiar English version is Montague Summers’s problematic 1928 translation, available through [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Malleus_Maleficarum).
- **Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum* (1563):** accepted demons but argued that many accused witches were melancholic and deceived rather than powerful criminals.
- **Jean Bodin, *De la démonomanie des sorciers* (1580); King James VI/I, *Daemonologie* (1597); Pierre de Lancre (1612):** defended aggressive prosecution and elaborated the diabolic pact and sabbath.
- **The *Historia von D. Johann Fausten* (1587):** Faust contracts with Mephistopheles; later versions transform the pact narrative.
- **John Milton, *Paradise Lost* (1667; revised 1674):** gives Satan extraordinary rhetorical and psychological prominence. The poem’s proud rebel, fallen host, Pandemonium, voyage through Chaos, and temptation of Eve profoundly shaped later popular “biblical” memory, but these details are literary invention and synthesis.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic actions

The Devil’s securely attested Christian domains are not natural phenomena in the manner of a weather or fertility god. They are relational and moral:

- accusation: Zechariah 3; Revelation 12;
- testing and temptation: Job 1–2; Matthew 4; Luke 4; 1 Thessalonians 3;
- deception and falsehood: John 8:44; 2 Corinthians 11:14; Revelation 12:9;
- obstruction of mission: 1 Thessalonians 2:18;
- demon leadership: Mark 3 and parallels;
- bondage, illness, or affliction: Luke 13:16; Acts 10:38; 2 Corinthians 12:7, with different texts assigning different causal relationships;
- incitement or possession: Luke 22:3; John 13:27; Acts 5:3;
- persecution and empire: Revelation’s dragon empowers the beasts;
- death: Hebrews 2:14 attributes “the power of death” to the Devil, while other Christian texts reserve ultimate power over life and death to God;
- eschatological deception and defeat: Revelation 20.

### Powers and limitations

Christian sources generally grant the Devil superhuman intelligence, mobility, deception, temptation, and influence over subordinate spirits and worldly systems. They deny him divine omnipotence, omniscience, creative power, or independence.

Job makes divine permission explicit. Gospel exorcisms subordinate demons to Jesus. James and 1 Peter say he can be resisted. Revelation gives him only a short time and ends with irrevocable judgment. Patristic and scholastic theologians often maintained that demons could manipulate bodies, imagination, air, and appearances but could not create substance, compel the will absolutely, or know future free choices as God does.

### Biblical appearance

No biblical passage supplies the familiar single humanoid appearance with horns, goat legs, red skin, bat wings, and pitchfork.

- Revelation’s **dragon** has seven heads and ten horns.
- Genesis has a **serpent**, only later identified with Satan.
- 1 Peter uses a **roaring lion** simile.
- 2 Corinthians says Satan can disguise himself as an **angel of light**.
- Gospel temptation narratives do not describe the tempter’s anatomy.
- “Red” derives particularly from Revelation’s red dragon and later associations with fire and Hell.
- The pitchfork derives from instruments of punishment, agricultural tools, and infernal theatrical conventions rather than a biblical weapon.

### Earliest and major depictions

1. **Aquileia floor mosaic, fourth century:** a grotesque or apotropaic face has sometimes been proposed as an early Christian Devil, but the identification is contested.
2. **Sant’Apollinare Nuovo, Ravenna, c. 520:** in the sheep-and-goats judgment mosaic, Christ is flanked by a red angel beside sheep and a blue-violet angel beside goats. Many art historians identify the latter as the earliest unambiguous anthropomorphic Devil, although he lacks later monstrosity. The Italian medieval-art encyclopedia [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/diavolo_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Medievale%29/) dates the mosaic and traces subsequent developments.
3. **Carolingian and Ottonian manuscripts, ninth–eleventh centuries:** dark, diminutive, shaggy, winged, or contorted demons multiply.
4. **Romanesque art, eleventh–twelfth centuries:** animal-human hybridity becomes standard—horns, claws, tails, hair, snouts, multiple faces, and batlike wings.
5. **Autun Cathedral, c. 1120–1135:** Gislebertus’s Last Judgment demons weigh, seize, and torment souls.
6. **Florence Baptistery mosaic, c. 1260–1270:** a gigantic horned Satan devours the damned while serpents issue from his body.
7. **Giotto, Arena Chapel, c. 1303–1305:** blue, horned, enormous Satan devours and excretes souls.
8. **Dante illustrations:** often portray the three-faced, bat-winged, ice-bound Lucifer of *Inferno*.
9. **Saint Michael images:** the Devil appears as dragon or humanoid under Michael’s spear or sword. A late-medieval pilgrim badge in the [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/image/1613154592) shows a horned, claw-footed Devil beneath Michael.
10. **Virgin trampling serpent/dragon:** Marian imagery applies Genesis 3:15 and Revelation 12 typologically. A Parisian ivory of c. 1310–1330 shows the Virgin standing over a monster ([British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1978-0502-3)).
11. **Renaissance and Baroque:** Satan may appear as a beautiful fallen angel, classical nude, serpent, dragon, satyr, or monstrous tempter.
12. **William Blake, Gustave Doré, and Romantic art:** Miltonic grandeur produces an athletic, tragic, or heroic Satan.
13. **Éliphas Lévi’s Baphomet, 1854–56:** goat-headed, winged, androgynous figure labeled the “Sabbatic Goat.” Lévi did not merely reproduce an ancient Satan-image.
14. **Twentieth-century popular culture:** red skin, horns, goatee, cape, cloven hoofs, and pitchfork become a standardized comic shorthand.

### Symbols and alleged correspondences

There is no universally canonical Satanic color, animal, plant, number, weekday, planet, or metal.

- **Serpent and dragon:** canonical through Revelation 12 and 20.
- **Goat:** indirectly associated through Matthew 25’s goats of judgment; reinforced by satyr/Pan imagery and later sabbath iconography.
- **Lion:** predatory metaphor in 1 Peter.
- **Bat wings:** medieval visual contrast with feather-winged good angels.
- **Pentagram:** not originally or universally Satanic. Inverted pentagrams gained explicit occult-Satanic meanings primarily in modern Western esotericism.
- **Inverted cross:** historically the Cross of Saint Peter; its automatic interpretation as Satanic is modern popular usage.
- **666:** Revelation 13 calls it the beast’s number, not Satan’s personal number. Later culture transfers it to Satan because the beast serves the dragon.
- **Black:** associated with darkness, death, sin, and concealment, but not a canonical exclusive color.
- **Red:** fire, blood, and Revelation’s dragon.
- **Friday, Saturday, Saturn, goats, yew, sulfur:** associations belong to particular occult, folkloric, or artistic systems, not an ancient universal cult calendar.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin rather than biological genealogy

Christian orthodoxy gives the Devil no parents, spouse, biological siblings, or necessary offspring. God creates all angelic natures good; the Devil becomes evil through will. “Father of lies” in John 8:44 denotes origination or imitation, not biological paternity. “Children of the Devil” denotes moral affiliation.

Competing origin systems include:

- **Canonical inference:** a rebellious angel, derived synthetically from Luke 10:18, Revelation 12, Jude, 2 Peter, and rereadings of Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28.
- **Adam-centered apocrypha:** Satan refuses to bow to newly created Adam and falls through pride or envy.
- **Watcher traditions:** angelic transgression consists of descent, sex with women, and forbidden teaching. Satan is not always their leader.
- **Origenist speculation:** rational spirits fall to different degrees through misuse of freedom.
- **Lactantius:** presents a distinctive account involving two spirits/sons, one remaining faithful and one becoming envious; this did not become dominant.
- **Bogomil and Cathar-related polemical reports:** some medieval dualist cosmologies give Satan or Satanael a quasi-demiurgic role. Surviving evidence is often mediated through hostile inquisitorial or heresiological sources, and “Cathar theology” was not a single system.
- **Milton:** Satan is one of heaven’s greatest angels, later leader of a military revolt. This is a poetic system, not a biblical genealogy.

### Hierarchy

Patristic writers disagreed about Satan’s original rank. He could be called angel, archangel, cherub, or a member of a high order inferred from Ezekiel 28. Medieval scholastics debated whether he came from the highest angelic order. Scripture never states “Lucifer was the chief archangel” or supplies a canonical pre-fall rank.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 The heavenly prosecutor and Job

**Early attested form:** Job 1–2.

The sons of God assemble before YHWH. The accuser arrives from roaming the earth. When God praises Job, the accuser argues that Job’s devotion is purchased by protection and prosperity. God permits attacks on Job’s possessions and family but initially forbids harm to Job himself. After Job remains pious, the accuser receives permission to afflict his body but must spare his life.

The source does not make the accuser God’s cosmic enemy. He participates in court, challenges a claim, needs authorization, and disappears after chapter 2. Later Jewish and Christian retellings intensify his malice, identify him with the Devil, or give him disguises and speeches.

**Function:** the prose frame initiates an inquiry into disinterested piety, innocent suffering, divine justice, and the inadequacy of simplistic retribution. The Devil-centered reading is secondary to the book’s sustained human-divine debate.

### 5.2 Eden and the serpent

**Early attested form:** Genesis 3, probably incorporated into the Pentateuch during the first millennium BCE.

The serpent, “more crafty” than other animals made by YHWH, questions the prohibition and tells the woman she will not immediately die but gain divine knowledge. The humans eat, acquire awareness of nakedness, and are expelled. God curses the serpent to crawl and predicts enmity between its seed and the woman’s.

Genesis does not say:

- the serpent is Satan;
- the event is an angelic rebellion;
- the fruit is an apple;
- Satan possesses the serpent;
- the Devil falls at this moment;
- sexual intercourse constitutes the transgression.

**Development:**

- Wisdom 2:24 connects death’s entrance with “the devil’s envy.”
- Life of Adam and Eve traditions explain Satan’s envy through Adam’s exaltation.
- Revelation 12:9 calls the apocalyptic dragon “the ancient serpent,” enabling explicit canonical identification.
- Irenaeus says the serpent carried the hidden Slanderer.
- Augustine integrates the event with original sin.
- Medieval art combines serpent, dragon, female-headed serpent, and Devil.
- The fruit becomes an apple especially in Western art and wordplay involving Latin *malum* (“apple”/“evil”), not through Genesis.

### 5.3 The fall of the rebellious angel

There is no single biblical narrative describing Satan’s creation, rank, motive, revolt, battle, and fall in sequence.

The later myth was reconstructed from:

- Isaiah 14’s fallen morning star;
- Ezekiel 28’s Edenic cherub/king;
- Luke 10:18’s falling Satan;
- John 8:44 and 1 John 3:8;
- Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4’s sinning angels;
- Revelation 12’s heavenly war;
- Watcher and Adam-apocrypha traditions.

**Competing versions:**

1. **Prideful ascent:** Isaiah 14 read allegorically—Lucifer seeks God’s throne and is cast down.
2. **Corrupted cherub:** Ezekiel 28 read as Satan’s pre-fall perfection and corruption.
3. **Envy of Adam:** Satan refuses to honor Adam or envies divine gifts to humanity.
4. **Sexual transgression:** Watchers abandon heaven for human women; their leader is not necessarily Satan.
5. **Primordial or eschatological war:** Revelation 12 can be read as primordial history, an effect of Christ’s victory, or end-time apocalypse. The text’s narrative setting does not force one chronology.
6. **Miltonic civil war:** Satan organizes angelic armies, battles with artillery, falls into Hell, forms Pandemonium, and seeks revenge by corrupting humanity. This is the most influential continuous version but is seventeenth-century poetry.

### 5.4 Temptation of Jesus

Mark gives a compressed scene: the Spirit drives Jesus into the wilderness, Satan tempts him for forty days, wild animals surround him, and angels serve him.

Matthew and Luke narrate three temptations:

- turn stones to bread;
- obtain or display supernatural protection at the Temple;
- receive the world’s kingdoms in exchange for worship.

Their order differs: Matthew ends on the mountain with kingdoms; Luke ends at Jerusalem. Satan quotes Psalm 91; Jesus replies from Deuteronomy. Luke says Satan departs “until an opportune time.”

The cycle defines Jesus as obedient where Israel and Adam were tested, subordinates Satan through scripture, and associates political domination with demonic temptation. Later art supplies bodily appearances absent from the text.

### 5.5 The ransom, trap, and defeat

New Testament texts describe Christ’s work through several metaphors: victory over rulers, liberation from Satan, destruction of the Devil’s works, and defeat of death. Some Fathers developed a “ransom to the Devil” or deception motif: Satan possesses a limited claim over sinful humanity, takes Christ’s mortal flesh as bait, and is defeated by the concealed divinity and resurrection.

Augustine’s mousetrap image—Christ’s cross as trap and death as bait—influenced medieval art, including interpretations of Joseph’s mousetraps in the Mérode Altarpiece. The art-historical argument is documented in the Metropolitan Museum’s study of the [Mérode cityscape and mousetrap symbolism](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/On_the_Cityscape_of_the_Merode_Altarpiece_The_Metropolitan_Museum_Journal_v_11_1976.pdf).

No ecumenical Christian consensus requires a literal commercial payment to Satan. Satisfaction, recapitulation, Christus Victor, sacrifice, and other atonement models coexist.

### 5.6 Michael and the dragon

Revelation 12 places a woman, child, and dragon in cosmic conflict. The dragon seeks to devour the child. Michael and his angels fight it; the dragon and its angels lose their place in heaven. It is explicitly identified as ancient serpent, Devil, and Satan. Cast to earth, it pursues the woman and makes war on her remaining offspring.

Later readings identify the woman variously as Israel, the Church, Mary, or a composite symbol. Medieval liturgy and art turn Michael’s victory into the standard visual emblem of divine triumph over the Devil. Revelation does not say Michael kills Satan; Revelation 20 assigns the final casting into fire to divine judgment after imprisonment and release.

### 5.7 Binding, release, and final judgment

Revelation 20 says an angel binds the dragon for a thousand years and shuts it in the abyss so it cannot deceive the nations. After release, Satan gathers Gog and Magog; divine fire consumes them, and Satan is cast into the lake of fire.

Christian interpretations differ:

- literal future millennium;
- symbolic present church age;
- recapitulation of earlier apocalyptic events;
- historical schemes assigning the binding or release to specific eras.

Augustine’s amillennial interpretation treats the binding as restriction of Satan’s ability to prevent the nations’ conversion, not total inactivity ([*City of God* XX.7](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf102.iv.XX.7.html)).

### 5.8 The diabolic pact and witches’ sabbath

Medieval sources gradually combined harmful magic, apostasy, demon worship, nocturnal gatherings, sexual intercourse with demons, flight, infanticide, cannibalism, and conspiracy into a cumulative stereotype. Earlier penitential texts often treated night flight as illusion; fifteenth-century demonologists increasingly asserted bodily transport and organized sectarian worship of the Devil.

The *Malleus* declares witchcraft possible through the witch’s intention, demonic aid, and God’s permission. Trial testimony sometimes includes Devil names, bodily marks, animal forms, sex, gifts, and renunciation of baptism. Such records are evidence that interrogators and witnesses narrated these claims—not direct evidence that an underground Devil-worshipping organization existed. Torture, leading questions, copied demonological scripts, local folklore, and defendants’ strategic speech shaped the archive.

Executions were real; the alleged international Satanic cult is not historically established. Accused persons suffered imprisonment, torture, execution, property loss, and social destruction under secular and ecclesiastical jurisdictions whose practices varied markedly by region.

### 5.9 Faust and the contract

The 1587 German Faustbook gives Faust a term of power through a pact mediated by Mephistopheles and ends in violent damnation. Marlowe dramatizes theological despair and self-deception. Goethe transforms the wager: Mephistopheles receives permission to test Faust, echoing Job, but Faust is ultimately saved.

This cycle made the signed contract in blood, fixed term, exchange of soul for knowledge or pleasure, and legalistic Devil central to European culture. These are literary and folkloric developments, not standard biblical demonology.

### 5.10 The Devil outwitted

European vernacular tales repeatedly make the Devil a bridge-builder, church-builder, mill-builder, treasure-owner, wagerer, or claimant to the first soul crossing a structure. Humans substitute a dog, goat, or rooster; saints imprison him; peasants exploit contract wording; women repel him with riddles or bodily grotesquerie.

These tales domesticate cosmic evil into a dangerous but limited local being. Place-names such as Devil’s Bridge, Devil’s Dyke, and Devil’s Punch Bowl often acquired etiological stories after the landscape feature already existed.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No ancient Christian temple cult to the Devil

There is no securely documented ancient or medieval public temple system devoted to worshipping the Christian Devil comparable to a Greco-Roman cult. Christianity’s institutional practices concerning him are overwhelmingly **apotropaic and adversarial**: renunciation, exorcism, prayer for protection, baptismal rites, penitence, preaching, drama, and images of defeat.

Claims by Christian polemicists that pagans, heretics, Jews, Muslims, or witches worshipped Satan must be evaluated as accusations. Christian apologetic reclassification of another community’s god as a demon records Christian interpretation, not necessarily the worshipper’s self-understanding.

### Baptismal renunciation

Early baptismal liturgies required candidates to renounce Satan, his works, pomp, angels, or service, sometimes while facing west, then turn east to confess Christ. Forms vary across Syriac, Greek, Latin, Coptic, and later vernacular rites. The ritual makes Satan the rejected former master, not the recipient of cult.

Exorcistic elements remain in several modern churches. A Vatican doctrinal study affirms continuing Catholic baptismal exorcisms and belief in Satan as a personal creature while rejecting reduction to a mere abstraction ([“Christian Faith and Demonology”](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_19750626_fede-cristiana-demonologia_en.html)).

### Exorcism

New Testament precedent lies in Jesus and disciples expelling unclean spirits. Late-antique exorcists formed a clerical order in parts of the Church. Medieval and early-modern rites used adjurations, Gospel readings, crosses, relics, holy water, names of God, and saints’ intercession.

Exorcism sources describe demons in the plural; they do not prove every afflicted person was believed to contain Satan personally. Medical, psychological, social, and ritual explanations have overlapped or competed. Modern Catholic practice distinguishes solemn exorcism, authorized by a bishop, from ordinary prayers; Orthodox and Pentecostal traditions have their own liturgical and charismatic practices.

### Prayer and protective objects

Documented practices include:

- the Lord’s Prayer petition traditionally translated “deliver us from evil/the Evil One”;
- sign of the cross;
- Saint Michael prayers;
- Gospel incipits and scriptural amulets;
- holy water, blessed salt, medals, relics, bells, and church thresholds;
- apotropaic inscriptions and images;
- charms combining canonical, liturgical, and magical language.

The classification “religion” versus “magic” commonly reflects clerical or scholarly categories rather than clean differences in practitioners’ actions.

### Drama, festivals, and masquerade

Devils appear in medieval mystery plays, saints’ plays, carnival, Alpine Krampus traditions, Iberian and Latin American dances, and Mexican *pastorelas*. Their role may be didactic, comic, frightening, or all three. A twentieth-century Mexican Satan mask used in *La Pastorela* is documented by the [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Am1990-08-259).

These performances are not necessarily Devil worship. They ordinarily dramatize temptation and defeat while allowing controlled inversion, satire, and spectacle.

### Witchcraft accusations, violence, and politics

From the fifteenth through seventeenth centuries, Devil-pact theory helped turn diverse allegations of maleficium into the crime of apostasy and conspiratorial anti-Christian religion. The stereotype was heavily gendered but victims included women and men in regionally varying proportions.

Authorities used demonology to enforce confessional discipline, prosecute marginal persons, explain catastrophe, settle local conflicts, and display judicial or pastoral vigilance. Confessional competition after the Reformation could intensify demonological claims, although prosecution patterns depended strongly on local law and political structure.

Alleged infant sacrifice, cannibalism, orgies, and temple-like sabbaths are abundantly documented **as accusations and forced or mediated testimony**. A continuous organized cult performing them is not established by the trial archive.

### Modern Satanist institutions

The first well-documented durable public organizations positively identifying themselves as Satanist arose in the modern era.

- **Church of Satan:** Anton Szandor LaVey announced it in San Francisco on 30 April 1966; *The Satanic Bible* followed in 1969. The Church describes Satan as a positive symbol of pride, liberty, individualism, and carnality while rejecting supernatural belief ([official press kit](https://churchofsatan.com/online-press-kit/)). Its ritual is psychodramatic rather than sacrifice to a literal Devil.
- **Temple of Set:** Michael Aquino and others left the Church of Satan in 1975. Set, not simply the Christian Devil, became the central independently existing intelligence in its initiatory system.
- **The Satanic Temple:** founded in the United States in 2013. It is nontheistic and uses Satan as a literary-symbolic rebel in campaigns involving secularism and religious liberty. Its own [account](https://thesatanictemple.com/pages/about-us) distinguishes it from the Church of Satan.
- **Theistic Satanism and Luciferianism:** decentralized currents whose adherents may treat Satan, Lucifer, or related beings as real. Claims of ancient continuous descent are generally not documentable; most identifiable organizations, texts, and online networks are modern.

There is no credible basis for treating criminal abuse by an individual who uses Satanic imagery as evidence of a unified worldwide Satanist cult. Documented crimes should be attributed to the proven perpetrators and organizations.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Israelite and Judahite adversaries, c. tenth–sixth centuries BCE

*Śāṭān* is an ordinary adversarial term. Supernatural uses need not indicate a cosmic evil being. Israelite religion also knew chaotic sea monsters, destructive angels, wilderness beings, and foreign gods polemically subordinated to YHWH, but these do not yet form one Devil.

### II. Persian-period heavenly accuser, c. 520–300 BCE

Zechariah and Job attest a heavenly prosecutorial figure. He belongs to the divine administration and can be rebuked or limited. Chronicles possibly moves toward a more independently culpable inciter.

Persian/Zoroastrian influence has often been proposed to explain increasing dualism. Contact is historically plausible, but direct derivation of Satan from Angra Mainyu is not demonstrable. Jewish texts retain God’s ultimate sovereignty and develop through internal exegesis as well as intercultural contact.

### III. Second Temple plurality, c. 300 BCE–100 CE

Apocalyptic literature intensifies cosmic conflict. Distinct evil leaders—Azazel, Shemihazah, Mastema, Belial, Satan—perform overlapping functions. Explanations of evil include primordial angelic teaching, giant spirits, human inclination, national angelic conflict, and two opposed lots or spirits.

This is not a simple linear evolution from one name to another. Different communities selected different leaders and etiologies.

### IV. New Testament synthesis, c. 50–120 CE

Satan becomes a personal eschatological enemy tied to temptation, demon rule, persecution, political domination, deception, and death. Yet he remains subordinate and already judged through Christ. Revelation explicitly unites Satan, Devil, serpent, and dragon.

### V. Patristic systematization, c. 150–600

Christian writers:

- identify Satan behind Eden;
- read Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 as his fall;
- explain pagan cults as demonic deception;
- reject Manichaean and other strong ontological dualisms;
- establish that the Devil’s nature was created good;
- interpret evil as corruption of will;
- narrate ascetic combat and exorcistic victory.

The figure acquires a reconstructed prehistory more continuous than the biblical texts themselves provide.

### VI. Medieval expansion, c. 600–1450

Monastic literature, preaching, penitentials, drama, hagiography, scholasticism, art, and folklore make the Devil ubiquitous. He becomes at once:

- cosmic enemy;
- courtroom claimant to souls;
- grotesque monster;
- torturer in Hell;
- tempter of monks;
- seducer or sexual demon;
- animal shape-shifter;
- builder and bargain-maker;
- comic loser defeated by saints.

Hell is often imagined as his kingdom, yet formal theology usually treats him as Hell’s prisoner or punishing functionary under divine judgment, not an autonomous sovereign.

### VII. Diabolic conspiracy and confessional Europe, c. 1450–1750

The cumulative witch stereotype makes the Devil central to criminal prosecution. Printing spreads demonological manuals and sensational accounts. Catholic and Protestant regions both prosecute, though intensity varies. Skeptics such as Weyer challenge interpretations without necessarily denying demons.

The Devil also becomes a polemical label for confessional enemies. Protestants identify the papacy with Antichrist or diabolic power; Catholics answer with similar demonization; colonizers describe Indigenous religions as Devil worship, helping rationalize missionization, destruction of sacred objects, and suppression of ritual specialists.

### VIII. Enlightenment, Romanticism, and folklore, c. 1700–1900

Elite confidence in literal demonic causation declines unevenly. The Devil survives in sermons, popular tales, revivalism, occultism, literature, and art.

Milton’s Satan is reread sympathetically. Blake’s claim that Milton was “of the Devil’s party without knowing it,” Romantic rebellion, Byron’s Cain, Shelleyan anti-tyranny, and Goethe’s ironic Mephistopheles turn the adversary into an intellectual, political, or psychological figure.

Éliphas Lévi’s occult synthesis joins Tarot, Kabbalah, magic, and the Baphomet image. Later occultists reuse Christian demon names as ritual intelligences, symbolic forces, or psychological complexes.

### IX. Psychology, mass media, and organized Satanism, 1900–1980

Freudian, Jungian, and existential readings treat Satan as projection, shadow, archetype, or symbol of alienation. Literature and cinema produce influential images: *The Devil and Daniel Webster*, *Rosemary’s Baby*, *The Exorcist*, *The Omen*, and others.

LaVey’s Church of Satan converts the hostile Christian name into a public, self-applied religious identity. Its Satan is formally symbolic and atheistic, though ritual imagery borrows grimoires, horror, carnival, and anti-Christian inversion.

### X. Satanic panic and culture war, c. 1980–2000

Therapeutic suggestion, sensational media, fundamentalist anti-occult campaigns, policing theories, and daycare allegations generated claims of vast hidden Satanic networks committing ritual abuse and murder. Abuse did occur in society and required investigation; the proposed transnational cult structure and many spectacular claims were unsubstantiated.

Kenneth Lanning’s 1992 FBI-associated guide stressed evidentiary problems and the danger of allowing “satanic” interpretations to impede investigation. The report is preserved by the US Department of Justice’s [Office of Justice Programs](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/136592NCJRS.pdf).

Heavy metal and role-playing games became targets of moral panic. Some musicians or offenders employed Satanic imagery, but image, religious identification, and proof of criminal conspiracy are separate evidentiary questions.

### XI. Twenty-first century

The Devil remains:

- a personal being in Catholic, Orthodox, evangelical, Pentecostal, and other Christian traditions;
- a metaphor for systemic or radical evil in secular speech;
- a horror and comedy character;
- a symbol in political accusations and conspiracy movements;
- a nontheistic religious emblem;
- an actual spiritual being for minority theistic Satanists and Luciferians;
- a commodity in music, fashion, games, advertising, and tourism.

Digital culture accelerates the circulation of false antiquities: “ancient sigils” taken from early-modern grimoires, Lévi’s nineteenth-century Baphomet labeled a medieval god, Miltonic episodes presented as biblical quotations, and modern correspondences marketed as timeless Satanic doctrine.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Renaissance and grimoire traditions

Renaissance ritual magic usually claimed divine authorization to command spirits rather than worship Satan. Texts attributed to Solomon organized demons by kings, dukes, planets, directions, seals, and offices. The *Testament of Solomon*, *Sworn Book of Honorius*, *Key of Solomon*, *Lemegeton*, and related manuscripts have complex dates and are not products of one “Satanic church.”

Demonological catalogues such as Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577 appendix) and the later *Ars Goetia* list named demons, but Satan does not consistently occupy the same rank. Modern occult charts that pretend these hierarchies form one ancient system are harmonizations.

### Romantic Satan

Milton did not write a Satanist scripture, but Romantic readers extracted a rebel against tyranny. Blake, Byron, Shelley, and later Anatole France’s *La Révolte des anges* (1914) helped create “literary Satanism”: admiration for Satan as an emblem need not imply belief or ritual worship.

### Lévi, theosophy, and ceremonial magic

Éliphas Lévi’s *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* (1854–56) created the famous Baphomet illustration as a symbolic reconciliation of opposites. Later reproductions detached it from Lévi’s explanation and treated it as a portrait of Satan.

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn generally sought ascent and balance rather than Satan worship. Aleister Crowley’s use of “the Beast 666” and antinomian rhetoric encouraged popular Satanic identification, which he did not accept in the ordinary Christian sense. Modern claims that Crowley founded ancient Satanism are inaccurate.

### Psychology

- **Freudian interpretations:** Devil figures can externalize forbidden desire, guilt, paternal conflict, and cultural repression.
- **Jungian interpretations:** Satan may represent the rejected shadow or the theological problem created by one-sided images of divine goodness. These are twentieth-century analytical interpretations, not recovered ancient doctrine.
- **Existential and political readings:** Satan symbolizes revolt, nihilism, totalitarian evil, or the mechanisms by which communities manufacture enemies.

### Organized modern Satanisms

LaVeyan Satanism is a documented modern religion with a 1966 institutional foundation and a 1969 defining text. Its claim to be the first openly organized, durable religion positively calling itself Satanist is substantially stronger than claims of medieval continuity, although isolated earlier occultists or small groups used Satan-positive language.

The Satanic Temple emerged in 2013 and deploys Satan as a symbol of resistance to arbitrary authority, not as a supernatural master. Its public monuments and equal-access campaigns deliberately expose how states manage religious pluralism.

Theistic forms are harder to enumerate because they are decentralized, frequently private, and internet-mediated. No reliable global membership count exists.

### Literature, visual art, film, comics, and games

Works materially shaping the modern figure include:

- Dante’s *Inferno*;
- Milton’s *Paradise Lost*;
- Goethe’s *Faust*;
- Blake’s Miltonic art;
- Gustave Doré’s biblical and Dante illustrations;
- Mark Twain’s *The Mysterious Stranger* traditions;
- Mikhail Bulgakov’s *The Master and Margarita*;
- C. S. Lewis’s *The Screwtape Letters*;
- *Rosemary’s Baby*;
- *The Exorcist*;
- *The Omen*;
- *The Devil’s Advocate*;
- comics including *Lucifer*;
- television series *Lucifer*;
- games such as *Diablo*, *Doom*, and numerous role-playing franchises.

These works freely combine Satan, Lucifer, Mephistopheles, Antichrist, demons, Hades, and folkloric devils. Their influence on public iconography can exceed that of scripture.

### Political uses and extremist appropriation

Calling opponents “Satanic” has accompanied religious persecution, colonial suppression, antisemitism, anti-Catholicism, anti-Protestantism, anti-Masonic conspiracy theory, anti-communism, and contemporary partisan rhetoric. Medieval and modern accusations may recycle older blood-libel and witches’ sabbath structures.

Some extremist individuals and groups have used Satanic or occult symbols, just as others use Christian, pagan, nationalist, or esoteric symbols. The symbol alone does not establish organizational lineage or a common theology.

### Commercialization

Horned mascots, “deal with the Devil” advertising, Halloween costumes, hot-sauce labels, music branding, tarot decks, jewelry, and tourism reduce the figure to instantly legible signs. Commercial Baphomet merchandise frequently advertises an image invented in the nineteenth century as “ancient Satan.”

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major approaches

1. **Confessional demonology:** treats the dispersed sources as testimony about one real personal being.
2. **History of religions:** reconstructs development from adversarial function to cosmic enemy while comparing Iranian, Mesopotamian, Canaanite, and Greek materials.
3. **Literary and redaction criticism:** resists harmonization and studies what each text does in its own narrative.
4. **Social history:** examines how Devil belief organized authority, discipline, colonial encounter, witch persecution, and group boundaries.
5. **Iconography:** traces the absence of a biblical body and the gradual construction of a composite monster.
6. **Folklore studies:** studies the gap between clerical prince of evil and the foolish, contractual, localized Devil.
7. **New-religion studies:** distinguishes accusations of Satanism from self-identified modern Satanism.

### Central debates

#### Was Satan originally a proper name?

Job and Zechariah’s definite article strongly suggests a title. First Chronicles lacks the article, but whether that signals a personal name is debated. New Testament usage is more individualized, yet grammatical absence of an article does not automatically prove modern proper-name status. Cirafesi’s [comparative study](https://academic.oup.com/jts/article/70/1/21/5404472?guestAccessKey=c37805a7-8042-4cf5-9105-b26db54f1198) cautions against overly simple transitions.

#### Persian influence

The historical setting makes some Iranian influence possible, especially concerning cosmic opposition and eschatology. Strong claims that Jews simply borrowed Angra Mainyu and renamed him Satan exceed the surviving evidence. Jewish adversary traditions, scriptural interpretation, court imagery, and apocalyptic innovation also account for development.

#### Are Genesis’s serpent and Satan the same?

Not within Genesis’s explicit wording. They are identified by later Jewish-Christian interpretation, decisively for Christian canon through Revelation’s “ancient serpent.” A historical answer must therefore be twofold: distinct in the earlier document; identified in later reception.

#### Is Lucifer the Devil’s original name?

Not in Isaiah’s immediate sense. *Lucifer* is a Latin astronomical/common noun used to translate a royal metaphor. Patristic exegesis transformed the king’s fall into a type of Satan’s fall. “Lucifer” then became a proper name through reception.

#### Are Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 primordial myths?

Both use mythic and Edenic imagery against human rulers. Scholars debate their West Semitic backgrounds and the extent to which older divine myths lie behind the poems. Reconstructed Canaanite parallels do not make their named human targets disappear, nor do they independently attest the Christian Devil.

#### Watchers versus Satan

Older scholarship sometimes folded all fallen angels into one Satan-led revolt. The Book of the Watchers names multiple leaders and makes illicit descent, sex, and teaching central. Adamic, Watcher, and Lucifer myths interacted later but began as distinguishable explanations.

#### Did medieval witches worship Satan?

Some accused persons may have held heterodox beliefs or performed harmful or counter-religious rites; individual cases differ. The vast conspiratorial sect described by demonologists is not established. Trial confessions are highly mediated evidence, frequently produced through coercion and elite expectations.

#### Pagan horned gods as the source of Satan

Medieval artists could borrow features from satyrs, Pan, Bes, theatrical monsters, bestiaries, and animal symbolism. The claim that the Christian Devil is simply “the pagan Horned God,” or that medieval witchcraft preserved an organized prehistoric fertility religion, is discredited. Margaret Murray’s witch-cult theory strongly influenced modern Wicca and popular culture but not current witch-trial historiography.

#### Satan and Baphomet

The Lévi image is nineteenth-century. The goat-pentagram emblem was developed through modern French occult imagery and was formally adopted and modified by the Church of Satan. Calling it a surviving Templar or ancient Mesopotamian Satanic seal is unsupported.

### Material evidence and archives

The evidence is dispersed across:

- biblical manuscripts: Dead Sea Scrolls, Septuagint witnesses, major codices, Masoretic codices, Vulgate manuscripts;
- Qumran scrolls in the Israel Museum and Jordanian collections;
- Ethiopic manuscripts preserving 1 Enoch and Jubilees;
- Greek, Latin, Syriac, Armenian, Georgian, Slavonic, Coptic, and Arabic Christian manuscripts;
- cathedral sculpture, mosaics, manuscripts, ivories, badges, prints, paintings, and theatrical objects;
- municipal, ecclesiastical, and state witch-trial archives;
- printed demonologies and grimoires;
- oral-history and folklore collections;
- institutional archives and publications of modern Satanist organizations.

Loss is substantial: apocryphal works survive only in translations or quotations; Jude’s Moses source is lost or incompletely identifiable; medieval paintings and plays disappeared; trial records were destroyed, excerpted, or never created; oral traditions were recorded late.

### Major modern scholarship

Foundational or influential studies include:

- Jeffrey Burton Russell’s multi-volume history: *The Devil* (1977), *Satan* (1981), *Lucifer* (1984), and *Mephistopheles* (1986);
- Henry Ansgar Kelly, *Satan: A Biography* (2006), challenging the standard linear “evil rebel” account;
- Elaine Pagels, *The Origin of Satan* (1995), emphasizing social boundary-making in early Christianity;
- Neil Forsyth, *The Old Enemy* (1987), emphasizing combat myth;
- Luther Link, *The Devil: A Mask Without a Face* (1995), on iconography;
- Maximilian Rudwin, *The Devil in Legend and Literature*;
- Norman Cohn, *Europe’s Inner Demons*;
- Stuart Clark, *Thinking with Demons*;
- Brian Levack, *The Witch-Hunt in Early Modern Europe*;
- Carlo Ginzburg, *Ecstasies*, whose long-duration comparative arguments remain influential and contested;
- Per Faxneld and Jesper Aagaard Petersen, editors of *The Devil’s Party*;
- Asbjørn Dyrendal, James R. Lewis, and Jesper Aagaard Petersen, *The Invention of Satanism*.

Jeffrey Burton Russell’s *Lucifer* is catalogued with its coverage of medieval Devil belief, art, folklore, and witchcraft by [WorldCat](https://search.worldcat.org/title/557921104). Modern Satanism scholarship explicitly treats *The Satanic Bible* as a constructed modern scripture rather than the endpoint of an unbroken ancient cult ([Oxford chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/2880/chapter-abstract/143494900)).

## 11. Sources

### Primary-source and documentary base

The core primary dossier consists of the Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, New Testament, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Dead Sea Scrolls, Life of Adam and Eve recensions, patristic writings, medieval canon law and hagiography, demonological treatises, trial records, liturgies, visual objects, literary works, and modern organizations’ own publications.

Particular caution applies to:

- editions silently harmonizing variant manuscripts;
- translations using “Satan” where the source has a common noun;
- editions of pseudepigrapha that fail to identify the language or recension;
- nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century occult translations presented as critical editions;
- witch-trial confessions detached from interrogation procedure;
- museum labels that identify uncertain figures more confidently than specialist literature;
- modern organizations’ self-histories, which are primary evidence for self-presentation but require independent corroboration for broader historical claims.

### Evidentiary conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction yields no single moment when “the Devil” first appears complete. Instead:

1. an adversarial office is attested in Persian-period Hebrew texts;
2. Second Temple Jewish texts diversify supernatural evil into several competing leaders;
3. New Testament writings concentrate these functions in Satan/the Devil and Revelation explicitly fuses serpent, dragon, accuser, and world-deceiver;
4. Fathers reconstruct a fall narrative from otherwise separate scriptures and apocrypha;
5. medieval theology, art, drama, hagiography, and folklore supply the familiar body and behavior;
6. early-modern demonology makes the Devil the juridical center of alleged witch conspiracy, with lethal consequences for accused people;
7. Milton, Romanticism, occultism, psychology, horror, and mass media repeatedly reinvent him;
8. positive self-identified Satanism is securely institutionalized only in modernity, with major branches disagreeing over whether Satan exists literally.

The variants are not corruptions of a recoverable single original. They constitute the documented history of the figure.
