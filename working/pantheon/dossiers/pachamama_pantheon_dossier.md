# Pachamama: Pantheon Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

Pachamama is not recoverable through a pre-Columbian written canon. The Andes produced no surviving alphabetic or deciphered narrative corpus comparable to Mesopotamian tablets or Greek hymns, and no pre-Hispanic inscription securely names her. The earliest evidence is therefore colonial: Spanish-language chronicles, administrative and missionary reports, Quechua prayers transcribed in Latin characters, Aymara and Quechua dictionaries, Indigenous-authored manuscripts, and later ethnography. These sources preserve Indigenous testimony but were produced after conquest, often under evangelizing or extirpatory pressure. Statements about “Inca belief” consequently mean “reported in a colonial source as Inca belief,” not direct pre-conquest documentation.

The following labels are used:

- **Early colonial attestation:** sixteenth-century testimony, sometimes explicitly describing pre-conquest practice.
- **Later colonial systematization:** seventeenth-century compilation or elaboration.
- **Regional ethnographic variant:** documented local belief or practice, not necessarily pan-Andean.
- **Syncretistic development:** historically produced conjunction with Catholic or other beings and institutions.
- **Modern reconstruction/reinvention:** scholarly, nationalist, environmentalist, New Age, artistic, or commercial formulations not demonstrably attested in the early record.

“Pachamama” can designate a named powerful being, the animate and productive earth, a spatial-temporal totality, or the earth addressed relationally as mother. Those senses overlap, but they must not be assumed to be identical everywhere.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Pachamama is the animate, productive, potentially dangerous earth or world addressed as a maternal being in Quechua-, Aymara-, and neighboring Andean traditions. Colonial writers variously call her *tierra*, *madre tierra*, *diosa*, *huaca/wak’a*, or a “second cause”; contemporary speakers may call her *Madre Tierra*, *la Pacha*, a tutelary being, a living totality, or simply the earth to whom food and drink are owed. She gives crops, pasture, animals, bodily and communal reproduction, minerals, and protection, but may also withhold fertility, “eat” people, cause illness or accidents, or punish neglected reciprocal obligations. Treating her only as a benign “nature goddess” removes the danger and appetite prominent in ritual and ethnography.

### Names

### Etymological dispute

Both *pacha* and *mama* occur in Quechua and Aymara. Ludovico Bertonio’s 1612 Aymara vocabulary records *Pachamama*, while Diego González Holguín’s 1608 Cuzco Quechua vocabulary does not list the compound, although it extensively records *pacha*. This asymmetry led some scholars to propose an Aymara origin. It is not decisive: Polo de Ondegardo had already recorded *Pachamama* in a Quechua-Andean setting in 1567, and the imperial and early-colonial southern Andes were intensely multilingual. A uniquely Quechua or uniquely Aymara origin remains unproved. González Holguín’s bibliography and text history are catalogued by [Glottolog](https://glottolog.org/resource/reference/id/104046); the colonial semantic range of *pacha* is analyzed in the [Museo Chileno study](https://boletinmuseoprecolombino.cl/html/art-04-inostroza-30-2/).

### Classification through time

- **Sixteenth-century Spanish categories:** “goddess,” “idol,” *huaca*, natural element, or earth as a divine “second cause.”
- **Indigenous and mestizo colonial writing:** a maternal and protecting earth, recipient of prayer and sacrifice, but not always isolated as an anthropomorphic individual.
- **Extirpation literature:** an illicit object of “idolatry,” sometimes rhetorically attributed to demonic deception.
- **Modern ethnography:** a person-like earth, owner or guardian, mother, encompassing world, dangerous consumer, and participant in reciprocal relations.
- **Modern environmental and legal discourse:** “Mother Earth” or “Nature” as an ecological and juridical subject.
- **Popular global discourse:** frequently flattened into a universal, uniformly benevolent “Inca earth goddess.” That formula is modern simplification.

### Syncretistic identifications

Pachamama has been associated regionally with the Virgin Mary and particular Marian images, including the Virgins of Copacabana, Urkupiña, and the Socavón. These are neither universal nor simple replacements. Worshippers may distinguish the Virgin, the earth, mountain powers, God, saints, and mine beings while addressing them during the same festival. Maternal imagery and the placement of Marian shrines in powerful landscapes facilitated historical convergence. A modern ethnographic overview notes the maternal relationship drawn between Pachamama and Mary in the central Andes. [Celestino, “Transformaciones religiosas en los Andes peruanos”](https://www.ugr.es/~pwlac/G13_06Olinda_Celestino.html)

The common equation with Greek Gaia, Roman Terra Mater, “Mother Nature,” or a universal “Great Goddess” is comparative or New Age interpretation, not an Andean historical identification.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest layers

#### 1530s: unnamed female images and cult objects

Pedro Sancho de la Hoz and Miguel de Estete describe gold and silver female figures in or near Cuzco. Neither securely identifies one as Pachamama. Such objects may have represented royal women, ancestral females, or goddesses. They are evidence for female sacred imagery, not a first attestation of Pachamama.

#### 1553–1555: Earth as mother, without the compound name

Pedro Cieza de León’s *Señorío de los Incas* (written c. 1553) records a protective invocation in which the Sun, Moon, Earth, mountains, trees, stones, and royal ancestors protect the Inca. Agustín de Zárate’s *Historia del descubrimiento y conquista del Perú* (1555) reports the claim that the Sun was father and Earth mother. Zárate places this declaration in the Atahualpa–Valverde encounter; the speech is mediated by Spanish historiography and cannot be treated as a verbatim transcript. This is the earliest explicit colonial textual stratum identifying the Andean earth as mother. [Di Salvia, pp. 93–95](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/REAA/en/article/download/42302/40262/60354)

Cristóbal de Molina “the Almagrist” wrote in 1552 that people “worshipped the Earth because they said it had a mother” and had made its *bulto* and shrines. The wording—earth as possessing a mother rather than simply being mother—should not be harmonized away.

#### 1567: earliest clear named Pachamama

Juan Polo de Ondegardo’s *Instrucción contra las ceremonias y ritos que usan los indios conforme al tiempo de su infidelidad* states:

> “Es cosa común entre Indios adorar a la tierra fértil, que es la tierra que llaman Pachamama, o Camac pacha…”

He reports offerings of chicha, coca, burnt fat, guinea pigs, and camelids during plowing, sowing, and harvest, with fasting, drinking, and dancing. This is the earliest securely identifiable use of the compound name found in the consulted corpus. Polo was a colonial jurist and investigator; the text is hostile administrative ethnography, not an Indigenous scripture. Its physical witness is a colonial manuscript tradition transmitted in later editions. [Comparative transcription in Di Salvia](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/REAA/en/article/download/42302/40262/60354)

### What the early corpus does not contain

There is no securely pre-Columbian hymn manuscript, temple inscription, birth story, divine marriage epic, or long Pachamama narrative. Molina’s prayers are the closest surviving equivalent to hymnic material. The *Huarochirí Manuscript* preserves a rich Quechua mythological corpus around Pariacaca, Chaupi Ñamca, Cuniraya Viracocha, and local huacas, but Pachamama is not a principal named protagonist. Her absence warns against treating every Andean earth or fertility episode as “a Pachamama myth.”

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The best-attested early domain is productive earth:

- fertility of cultivated fields;
- germination, crops, and harvest;
- protection of *chacras*;
- receipt of liquid and food offerings;
- protection of ruler and community in state ceremony;
- the uncultivated land in Murúa’s 1616 distinction;
- more broadly the terrestrial world and its generative depth.

Later regional ethnographies extend or foreground:

- reproduction of herds and wild animals;
- ownership or guardianship of land and animals;
- health, luck, travel, domestic welfare, work, and commerce;
- metals and mining, often in negotiated relation with El Tío, Supay, or the mountain;
- earthquakes, landslides, crop failure, animal loss, illness, and accidental death;
- ingestion: the earth “opens,” is hungry, or consumes careless people.

A contemporary Calchaquí study describes Pachamama as a tutelary entity securing biological and social reproduction, while documenting that failure to maintain ritual agreements is believed to produce crop, animal, and climatic harm. [Páez et al., 2023](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/bitstream/handle/10915/153105/Documento_completo.pdf-PDFA.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

### Powers and limitations

Pachamama is powerful but relational rather than absolutely sovereign. She is fed, remembered, entreated, and sometimes feared. Her beneficial action depends on respect, timing, and reciprocity. In many local accounts she does not create the universe ex nihilo; she sustains and reproduces life within it. She may work jointly with rain, Sun, Moon, mountains, springs, ancestors, saints, and agricultural “mothers.”

No early source gives her a standardized weapon, throne, sacred number, weekday, planet, or universal color. Lists assigning such correspondences are modern esoteric constructions unless independently sourced.

### Early material representations

1. **Earth or landscape itself.** The primary “image” is frequently the land addressed and fed directly.

2. **Upright field stone.** Murúa, Arriaga, Oliva, and Cobo describe long stones in fields. Arriaga calls one a *chichic/huanca* or *chacrayoc*, “owner/lord of the field”; Cobo says it served as an altar or statue of the earth goddess. These descriptions do not prove that every upright Andean stone represented Pachamama. [Di Salvia, pp. 100–101](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/REAA/en/article/download/42302/40262/60354)

3. **Santacruz Pachacuti’s Qorikancha diagram, c. 1613.** Pachamama appears as a circular-terrestrial form with mountains and flowing water. The drawing is an Indigenous colonial cosmographic exposition, transmitted in the autograph manuscript. Santacruz says it reflects a Qorikancha wall arrangement, but the surviving drawing is seventeenth-century and shaped by colonial graphic and Christian explanatory conventions. It is not a surviving Inca mural or a photograph-like copy. Its claimed pre-conquest prototype cannot be verified. [Beauclair’s analysis](https://revistas.pucp.edu.pe/index.php/lexis/article/download/15051/15564/59647)

### Images falsely or insecurely identified

- The so-called Pachamama Monolith at Tiwanaku is more conventionally the Bennett Monolith; calling it Pachamama is a modern identification, not an inscriptional reading.
- Moche, Nazca, Wari, Tiwanaku, and Inca female or agricultural figures are not automatically Pachamama.
- The polychrome “Pachacamac Idol” represents the distinct coastal oracle Pachacamac; name similarity does not make it an image of Pachamama. Scientific study dates the carved object to approximately the eighth–ninth century CE and associates its imagery with Wari traditions. [Pozzi-Escot et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6961831/)
- Modern statues commonly show a broad-bodied Indigenous woman, a pregnant nude, an elderly woman with food, a mountain-bodied woman, or a globe-bearing “Mother Earth.” No single form is an inherited pan-Andean iconographic canon.

### Animals, plants, and substances

Securely documented ritual associations include llamas or other camelids, guinea pigs, coca, maize, potatoes, quinoa, chicha, fat, wool, colored feathers, shells, aromatic wood, gold and silver miniatures, and later wine, beer, sweets, cigarettes, incense, confetti, and llama or pig fetuses. These are offerings or media of relation, not necessarily exclusive “symbols” of Pachamama.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical evidence

Pachamama has no stable early genealogy. No colonial source securely identifies universal divine parents, siblings, spouse, and children.

Molina’s prayers call the Inca her “son,” but this is sacral-political kinship, not necessarily a biological myth. Santacruz Pachacuti’s Pachamamaachi is mother of Apomanco Cápac and partner of Apotampo; whether this ancestral woman is an aspect of cosmic Pachamama or merely bears a related title is disputed. Modern charts naming Pachamama as wife of Pachacamac, mother of Inti and Mama Killa, daughter of Viracocha, or mother of the four elements generally conflate distinct traditions and lack a securely cited early source.

### Pantheon position

The colonial evidence places Earth among Sun, Moon, weather, mountains, waters, huacas, and ancestors. Cuzco state ceremonies could invoke her alongside major imperial beings, but her cult was also domestic, agricultural, and local. “Supreme goddess of the Inca pantheon” is too categorical. “Major and widely addressed power whose state and household dimensions overlapped” fits the evidence better.

### Every documented named divine interaction

Because the sources contain little narrative, most interactions are ritual conjunctions rather than mythic meetings.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Absence of a canonical Pachamama epic

The documentary record does not support a long, standardized cycle of Pachamama myths. Her centrality is ritual and relational more than narrative. Modern retellings often fill this gap with a smooth biography assembled from unrelated Andean beings.

### Earth as mother of living things and crops

**Early core:** Zárate’s 1555 account calls Earth mother; López de Gómara and other sixteenth-century compilers call her mother of things and recipient of agricultural devotion. Garcilaso, in 1609, says the earth was called Mother because she gave fruits.

**Development:** Later ethnography turns this into many local propositions: people are her children; crops and animals are raised within her; she gestates life; burial returns people to her interior. These propositions need not derive from one narrative.

### Pachamama protects and holds the Inca

Molina’s Citua prayer asks Pachamama to embrace, support, and keep her son the Inca peaceful and fortunate:

> *Pachamama qasillacta qespillacta, Qhapaq Inka wawaykiqta marq’ariy, hat’alliy.*

The surviving prayer is colonial transcription of reported Inca liturgy, not a pre-conquest manuscript. Its function is royal protection and territorial purification. Molina also records a prayer joining Pachamama and the disputed Kuirumama title. [Text and translation](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/REAA/en/article/download/42302/40262/60354)

### Pachamamaachi and the roots of the Incas

Santacruz Pachacuti recounts that Apomanco Cápac, after the deaths of his parents Apotampo and Pachamamaachi, organizes his people and departs on conquest carrying Tunapa’s staff. Two trees symbolize the parents as root and trunk of the Incas. The story legitimates descent and political order through an ancestral landscape genealogy.

This is the nearest colonial text to a Pachamama-related origin narrative, but the mother’s name is *Pachamamaachi*. The equation with Pachamama is interpretive, and the narrative should not be rewritten as “Pachamama gave birth to the first Inca.” [Santacruz material summarized and transcribed in Di Salvia](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/REAA/en/article/download/42302/40262/60354)

### The hungry or consuming earth

In twentieth- and twenty-first-century southern-Andean narratives, Pachamama is hungry—especially in August—or may “eat” people who traverse dangerous places, neglect offerings, sleep on exposed ground, or suffer accidents. A northwestern Argentine formula asks “Pachamama, holy earth, do not eat me.” These are regional folklore and ritual speech, not securely attested Inca scripture.

The imagery explains why offerings are placed into holes, buried, or burned: the earth is treated as a recipient with a mouth and appetite. Contemporary Bolivian practitioners say she awakens hungry and thirsty after the dry season and must be fed with *mesitas*, drink, grains, sweets, coca, and sometimes animal fetuses. [Associated Press field report, 2025](https://apnews.com/article/3ff0b82f0324e9fef5cd24a4b6a6552d)

### The earth opened and the August cycle

Regional accounts describe August as a dangerous transitional month when the earth is open, resting, hungry, or preparing for cultivation. Practices include avoiding unnecessary digging, drinking herbal preparations, burning offerings, opening a ritual pit, feeding it, and closing it after communal consumption.

There is a suggestive historical connection with Molina’s August Citua, but direct continuity of every contemporary August detail cannot be demonstrated. Citua was an imperial purification festival; modern August Pachamama rites are locally variable domestic, pastoral, commercial, civic, and touristic events.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult centers and temples

No surviving structure can be securely identified as a temple devoted exclusively to Pachamama through an inscription or unambiguous excavated image.

- **Cuzco/Qorikancha and central plaza:** Molina and later Cobo support Pachamama’s participation in state ritual alongside Sun, Moon, Viracocha, and thunder. Santacruz’s diagram associates *mama pacha/camac pacha* with the Qorikancha’s cosmological arrangement. This establishes ritual inclusion, not an exclusive temple.
- **Fields and household landscapes:** Upright stones, cultivated plots, springs, pits, and the soil itself were more characteristic ritual loci.
- **Mountains and mines:** Modern rites may address Pachamama alongside apus, mountain owners, El Tío, saints, or the Virgin. These are networks of powerful places rather than centralized Pachamama temples.

Claims that Tiwanaku, Pachacamac, or a particular pre-Inca “Mother Goddess temple” was a Pachamama sanctuary require evidence not presently available.

### Ritual specialists

Colonial sources refer to Indigenous priests, diviners, “sorcerers” in missionary terminology, and women offering directly. Arriaga says women especially spoke to Mamapacha at sowing, either themselves or through ritual specialists.

Modern specialists include *yatiris*, *paqos/pacos*, *altomisayoq*, *kallawayas*, curers, diviners, and locally named ceremonial experts. Titles, initiation, and competence vary. Lightning strikes, dreams, inherited knowledge, unusual birth, apprenticeship, or selection by mountain powers may authorize specialists in particular communities; none forms a single pan-Andean Pachamama priesthood.

### Offerings and sacrifice

#### Early colonial record

Polo and Murúa list:

- chicha;
- coca;
- burnt fat;
- guinea pigs;
- lambs or camelids;
- fasting from meat, salt, chili, and cooked foods;
- drinking and dancing during plowing, sowing, and harvest.

Arriaga adds chicha and ground maize poured at sowing. Cobo’s farmers pray at upright field stones for protection and fertility. [Colonial comparison](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/REAA/en/article/download/42302/40262/60354)

#### Modern practice

Common forms include:

- **ch’alla/challa:** sprinkling alcohol, chicha, beer, wine, or other liquid on earth, tools, vehicles, shops, houses, or fields;
- **pago/despacho/mesa:** a composed offering of coca, food, fat, sweets, miniatures, wool, incense, colored paper, shells, alcohol, and regionally animal fetuses;
- **corpachada:** “hosting” or feeding the earth, especially in northwestern Argentina;
- **wilanch(a):** animal sacrifice and blood offering, especially in Aymara contexts;
- burial in a ritual pit;
- burning an offering and reading its smoke, flame, or ashes;
- sharing the same food and drink among humans and the earth.

Recent ethnography in Argentina emphasizes that substances do not merely symbolize reciprocity: food, coca, alcohol, and cigarettes materially establish relations among humans, animals, land, and other-than-human powers. [HAU study](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/740967)

### Human sacrifice

Inca *capacocha/qhapaq hucha* included the sacrifice of children and young people, documented by colonial sources and high-altitude archaeology. The rite served imperial incorporation, ruler-related ceremonies, fertility, crisis response, and mountain or place consecration. Molina reports offerings “to heaven” and “to earth” during the capacocha cycle, but the evidence does not justify saying that every capacocha victim was sacrificed specifically to Pachamama. Mountain beings, local huacas, Sun, creator powers, rulers, and imperial political relations were also involved. The distinction matters. [Bioarchaeological review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4543117/)

Modern rumors that builders or miners routinely purchase human beings or human fetuses for Pachamama foundation sacrifices are not supported by evidence comparable to that for llama sacrifice. Criminal deaths, disappearances, folklore, sensational reporting, and ritual narratives must not be conflated.

### Calendar

- **August:** principal modern Pachamama season across much of Bolivia, Peru, Chile, and northwestern Argentina, especially 1 August and the following weeks.
- **Planting and harvest:** timing varies by ecology and locality.
- **Carnival:** ch’alla of fields, houses, vehicles, mines, and businesses often overlaps Pachamama relations.
- **Citua:** Molina assigns an imperial purification cycle to August; correlation with the modern August cult is plausible but not a proof of unchanged continuity.
- **Capacocha:** Molina associates an April ceremony with offerings to heaven and earth.

### Economics and politics

Offerings concern production: crops, herds, mines, commerce, vehicles, construction, and wage labor. They also redistribute food and drink and affirm household, neighborhood, labor, and political alliances. Public Pachamama ceremonies have become components of heritage festivals, tourism, municipal spectacle, Indigenous mobilization, and state ceremony. Private domestic rites may differ sharply from performances designed for visitors. The Calchaquí study explicitly distinguishes private memory-based ritual from public forms conditioned by tourism and markets. [Páez et al., 2023](https://sedici.unlp.edu.ar/bitstream/handle/10915/153105/Documento_completo.pdf-PDFA.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before 1532: inference without named documentation

Agricultural offerings, landscape veneration, huaca cults, camelid sacrifice, ritual burial, and reverence for mountains and waters are archaeologically ancient. None by itself proves worship of a being named Pachamama. Claims dating “the Pachamama cult” to 6000 BCE confuse remote antiquity of Andean land-related ritual with evidence for this particular name and figure.

### 1530s–1560s: colonial recognition

Early conquistador accounts notice female sacred images and natural worship but do not name Pachamama. By 1555 Earth is explicitly called mother; by 1567 Polo records *Pachamama/Camac pacha* and agricultural offerings. The evidence suggests an already widespread practice encountered by Spaniards, but it cannot determine when the compound name originated.

### 1570s–1650s: recording and repression

Molina records Quechua prayers and imperial ceremonies; Murúa systematizes categories; Indigenous and mestizo authors place Earth within cosmology and political ancestry; Arriaga and other extirpators document continuing rites in order to eliminate them.

The First Lima Council of 1551 and later ecclesiastical campaigns prohibited public Indigenous rites. Extirpators confiscated or destroyed cult objects, punished specialists, interrogated communities, and attempted to relocate worship into Christian institutions. Guamán Poma documents both the earlier prohibition on insulting Pachamama and colonial punishment of those maintaining inherited rites. The archive is therefore simultaneously evidence and instrument of suppression.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries: decentralization and Catholic entanglement

The disappearance of Inca state institutions did not eliminate field, household, pastoral, and mining practices. These persisted in locally transformed forms, using Christian prayers, crosses, saints’ days, Marian feasts, and sacramentals alongside coca, libation, animal sacrifice, and landscape address.

There is no documentary basis for a perfectly concealed, unchanged pre-Hispanic “Pachamama church.” Survival occurred through adaptation, domestic practice, seasonal labor, ritual specialization, and the capacity to interpret Catholic beings through Andean relations to place.

### Late nineteenth–twentieth centuries: folklore, anthropology, and nationalization

Folklorists and ethnographers increasingly represented Pachamama as the defining deity of the southern Andes. Major lines of study emphasized:

- earth fertility and maternal imagery;
- reciprocity and feeding;
- fear, appetite, and punishment;
- complementary powers of mountain, rain, and earth;
- relations between domestic and state ritual;
- Catholic syncretism;
- mining and underground beings;
- the semantic breadth of *pacha*.

Ana María Mariscotti de Görlitz’s *Pachamama Santa Tierra* and related work became foundational for comparative study of the central-southern Andes. Later scholars criticized any tendency to turn locally variable relations into a single pan-Andean theology.

### Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries: urban expansion and Indigenous politics

Pachamama rites became increasingly visible in cities, migrant communities, labor unions, universities, Indigenous organizations, tourism, and national ceremony. Vehicles, shops, offices, construction projects, and political inaugurations may be ch’alla’d.

This is not simply “revival.” Rural practices continued, while new public, urban, legal, ecological, and media forms developed.

### Constitutional and international transformation

Ecuador’s 2008 Constitution, article 71, declares:

> “La naturaleza o Pacha Mama, donde se reproduce y realiza la vida…”

and recognizes rights to the maintenance and regeneration of natural cycles. [Constitutional text](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Constituci%C3%B3n_de_Ecuador_de_2008/T%C3%8DTULO_II)

Bolivia’s 2009 Constitution invokes both Pachamama and God in its preamble: “con la fortaleza de nuestra Pachamama y gracias a Dios.” [Spanish constitutional text](https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Bolivia_2009?lang=es)

Bolivia’s Law 071 of 21 December 2010 recognizes rights of Mother Earth and corresponding duties of state and society. [Official legal text via FAOLEX](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/bol144985.pdf)

At Bolivia’s initiative, the UN General Assembly designated 22 April International Mother Earth Day in 2009. The resolution refers to Earth and ecosystems rather than establishing worship of Pachamama. [United Nations background](https://www.un.org/en/observances/earth-day/background)

These measures transform Pachamama from a locally fed and feared being into a symbol and legal language of planetary ecology. Scholars debate whether this translation expands Indigenous political ontology or domesticates it within state environmental law. [Miriam Tola, 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1057/s41305-018-0100-4)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Indigenismo, nationalism, and decolonial politics

Twentieth-century Andean indigenismo helped make Pachamama a transregional emblem of Indigenous authenticity. More recent movements connect her with *sumak kawsay/suma qamaña*, territorial sovereignty, anti-extractivism, food sovereignty, and climate justice.

Governments have also used Pachamama language while maintaining mining, hydrocarbons, roads, and industrial agriculture. This tension has prompted criticism that ceremonial and legal recognition can mask extractive policy. Public ritual may function simultaneously as sincere devotion, Indigenous representation, state legitimation, and tourism.

### New Age and global spirituality

From the late twentieth century, retreat centers, “shamanic” tourism, yoga communities, neo-Inca movements, psychedelic tourism, and online spirituality globalized *despacho* ceremonies and Pachamama invocations. Common additions include:

- Gaia equivalence;
- four-element systems;
- chakras and energy grids;
- Jungian “Great Mother” archetypes;
- crystal correspondences;
- planetary ascension;
- reconstructed “Inca priestess” lineages;
- gender-essentialist claims that women inherently embody Pachamama.

These may be meaningful modern traditions but are not demonstrated by the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century corpus. Commercial ceremonies sold as exact survivals of a single ancient Inca religion require lineage-specific documentation.

Unlike Isis, Hermes, or Hecate, Pachamama has no important documented role in Renaissance Hermetism, the nineteenth-century Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoire traditions. Claims of an ancient Hermetic Pachamama current are modern invention.

### Literature, music, and visual art

Pachamama has become a broad emblem in Andean folk music, nueva canción, ecological poetry, murals, sculpture, protest graphics, tourism logos, and commercial branding. Modern visual culture often anthropomorphizes her as a pregnant Indigenous woman or mountain-bodied mother—forms far more standardized than colonial iconography.

Juan Antin’s animated film *Pachamama* premiered in 2018 and received global Netflix distribution in 2019. Its village, sacred-object, conquest, and ecological themes introduced an international children’s audience to a generalized Andean sacred-world narrative. It is historical fantasy, not a primary source for pre-Hispanic belief. [Netflix announcement](https://about.netflix.com/en/news/juan-antins-cesar-award-nominated-animated-feature-film-pachamama-to-debut-on-netflix-in-june-2019)

### The 2019 Amazon Synod controversy

During the October 2019 Synod for the Amazon, carved figures of a nude pregnant Indigenous woman appeared in Vatican-related ceremonies and displays. Critics labeled them pagan Pachamama idols; defenders described motherhood, life, Amazonian peoples, or inculturated symbolism. On 21 October activists removed figures from Santa Maria in Traspontina and threw them into the Tiber.

Pope Francis later referred to them as “statues of the pachamama,” said they had been present “without idolatrous intentions,” announced their recovery, and apologized to those offended by their theft. [Vatican News transcript](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/pope/news/2019-10/pope-francis-comments-on-statues-stolen-from-church.html)

The event does not establish a traditional Amazon-wide Pachamama cult: Pachamama is historically Andean, while Amazonia contains many distinct peoples and religious traditions. Nor does the pope’s shorthand resolve the sculptures’ original local identity. The controversy is best understood as a modern Catholic culture-war episode in which “Pachamama” became a global political label.

### Commercial use

The name now brands restaurants, clothing, cosmetics, tourism, environmental organizations, music festivals, beverages, and wellness products. Such use testifies to global recognition, not ritual continuity. Commercial imagery usually favors benevolent ecological motherhood and suppresses sacrifice, appetite, danger, and local obligations.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The colonial-source problem

The earliest written witnesses are post-conquest and interested:

- soldiers noticed wealth and “idols”;
- royal officials catalogued practices for governance;
- missionaries classified them as idolatry;
- extirpators needed actionable lists of specialists and objects;
- Indigenous Christian writers argued within colonial theological and political frameworks;
- later chroniclers copied predecessors.

Parallel wording between Polo and Murúa illustrates textual dependence. Multiple colonial mentions are not always independent corroboration.

### Was Pachamama an Inca state goddess?

One position emphasizes Molina’s prayers, joint sacrifices, and Cuzco ceremonial appearances as evidence of incorporation into imperial religion. Another stresses the agricultural, local, domestic, and southern-Andean character of the cult and warns that later chroniclers systematized heterogeneous earth relations into a pantheon.

The safest conclusion is two-level: Earth received official Cuzco ritual attention, while decentralized local worship was crucial to survival and probably never formed a uniform cult.

### Quechua or Aymara origin?

Bertonio’s 1612 Aymara lexical attestation and González Holguín’s failure to list the compound encouraged an Aymara-origin hypothesis. Polo’s 1567 use and widespread multilingual circulation prevent a decisive conclusion. The name may have moved through both languages before documentation.

### Being or category?

Some scholarship treats Pachamama as a personified goddess; other work emphasizes that *pacha* is an encompassing time-place-world and that “Mother Earth” can mislead by implying a bounded Western-style divine individual. The sources support both personalized address and diffuse terrestrial scope. The difference may be contextual rather than mutually exclusive.

### Gender

Colonial Spanish grammar and maternal terminology strongly feminize Pachamama. Yet Andean gender is relational, complementary, situational, and not reducible to a Western male/female pantheon grid. Ethnographic materials sometimes describe earth powers with ambiguous or shifting gender, while modern political discourse often turns Pachamama into an essentialized universal woman. Neither ambiguity nor femininity should be erased.

### Iconographic overreach

Because no pre-Hispanic image is inscribed “Pachamama,” attribution must proceed cautiously. Frequent popular errors include:

- naming the Bennett Monolith Pachamama;
- identifying any pregnant figure as Pachamama;
- conflating Pachamama and Pachacamac;
- treating Santacruz’s 1613 colonial diagram as an untouched Inca original;
- interpreting all female agricultural figures as one goddess.

### Human sacrifice and sensationalism

Archaeology securely documents Inca capacocha sacrifice. It does not justify attributing every victim to Pachamama. Conversely, apologetic denial of all Inca human sacrifice is untenable. Modern foundation-sacrifice stories require case-by-case evidentiary evaluation.

### “Ancient environmentalism”

Colonial and ethnographic evidence demonstrates reciprocal relations with animate land. It does not prove that pre-Hispanic societies followed modern conservation policy or avoided intensive agriculture, imperial extraction, mining, warfare, and ecological change. Pachamama as the legal personification of a globally threatened biosphere is a significant modern translation.

### State of the evidence

Important witnesses and repositories include:

- Guamán Poma’s autograph, GKS 2232 4º, Royal Danish Library;
- Santacruz Pachacuti’s manuscript tradition in the Biblioteca Nacional de España;
- Molina’s *Relación* in the composite manuscript *Papeles varios sobre los indios Incas, Huarochiris y otras antigüedades del Perú*;
- early printed copies of Arriaga’s 1621 *Extirpación*;
- archaeological collections containing field stones, miniatures, offering deposits, and Inca ritual materials—few of which can be attributed exclusively to Pachamama.

A major methodological change came from reading colonial texts against archaeology and living ritual without assuming either perfect continuity or total rupture.

### Evidentiary conclusions

1. The earliest unmistakable named attestation currently identified is Polo de Ondegardo’s 1567 *Instrucción*.
2. Earth-as-mother language is documented slightly earlier, in the 1550s.
3. Pre-conquest worship is strongly supported indirectly by early testimony, but the name has no surviving pre-Hispanic inscription.
4. Pachamama’s earliest securely documented activity is cultic—receiving offerings, sustaining crops, and protecting ruler and community—rather than participation in an epic mythology.
5. No stable early genealogy exists.
6. No surviving pre-Hispanic image can be identified as Pachamama with inscriptional certainty.
7. Modern benign “Mother Nature” imagery preserves part of the tradition but often omits her hunger, danger, and reciprocal demands.
8. Constitutional “rights of Mother Earth,” global eco-spirituality, and the Amazon Synod controversy are historically important modern transformations, not transparent survivals of one ancient doctrine.
