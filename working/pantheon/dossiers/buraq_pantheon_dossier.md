# Buraq: Pantheon Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

**Subject:** Arabic **al-Burāq** (ٱلْبُرَاق), the supernatural riding animal associated above all with Muḥammad’s Night Journey (*al-isrāʾ*) and, in later integrated narratives, his Ascension (*al-miʿrāj*).

Burāq is frequently called a “divine being” or “mythical horse” in modern comparative writing, but those labels do not reproduce the classifications of the Islamic sources. The Arabic texts ordinarily call it a **dābba** (دابّة, riding creature or animal), sometimes a mount from Paradise. It is neither a deity nor an object of normative Islamic worship. Its importance derives from service to God, the prophets, and especially Muḥammad. Later literature and art transformed the minimally described white riding animal of the early Arabic reports into a winged, often human- or female-headed celestial hybrid.

Dates below distinguish:

- **Qurʾānic layer:** seventh-century scripture, but without Burāq’s name.
- **Early transmitted layer:** reports attributed to first- and second-generation Muslims but surviving principally in eighth- and ninth-century compilations.
- **Canonical Sunni layer:** especially the ninth-century *Ṣaḥīḥs* of al-Bukhārī and Muslim and the *Jāmiʿ* of al-Tirmidhī.
- **Later elaboration:** medieval commentary, expanded *miʿrāj-nāma* narratives, poetry, cosmography, and animal lore.
- **Regional development:** Persianate, Ottoman, South Asian, Maghribi, Southeast Asian, and other local forms.
- **Modern reinvention:** mass print, nationalism, transport branding, literature, and contemporary art.

“Earliest” means earliest surviving or reconstructable documentary witness, not necessarily the first occasion on which an account circulated orally. Hadith reports place words in the seventh century but are materially witnessed in later books and manuscripts. That distinction is essential.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### 1.1 Basic identification

Burāq is the celestial or paradisiacal mount that, in Islamic narrative tradition, carries Muḥammad from the sanctuary at Mecca to the “farthest place of prostration” (*al-masjid al-aqṣā*), conventionally identified with Jerusalem. Some narrations then have Muḥammad ascend through the heavens while still mounted on Burāq; others leave Burāq tethered in Jerusalem and give the heavenly ascent a different means or no expressly named vehicle. The familiar seamless story—Mecca, Jerusalem, seven heavens, divine audience, return—is therefore a synthesis of partially independent *isrāʾ* and *miʿrāj* materials.

Burāq has no ordinary birth, death, genealogy, independent cult, moral jurisdiction, priesthood, or temple economy in the sources. It functions as:

- a God-provided riding animal;
- a proof or instrument of Muḥammad’s miraculous journey;
- a link between Muḥammad and earlier prophets;
- in later imagery, a visual surrogate for the journey and sometimes for Muḥammad himself;
- in some late eschatological narratives, a mount connected with resurrection or salvation.

### 1.2 Names and scripts

| Language or tradition | Form | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Classical/Modern Arabic | **ٱلْبُرَاق**, *al-Burāq* | Definite form normally used in Arabic narrative. |
| Arabic without article | **بُرَاق**, *Burāq* | Used as a proper name in translation and naming. |
| Persian | **بُراق**, *Borāq/Burāq* | Common in Persian poetry, prose, and manuscript captions. |
| Ottoman Turkish | **Burâk/Burak** | Also occurs in *miʿrāciyye* literature and illustration. |
| Modern Turkish | **Burak** | A widespread masculine personal name; its modern use should not be projected backward as evidence for a personal cult. |
| Urdu | **بُراق**, *Burāq* | Frequent in *miʿrāj-nāma*, devotional print, and popular preaching. |
| Chaghatay | usually **براق** | Prominent in the 1436 Herat *Miʿrāj-nāma*, BnF Supplément turc 190. |
| Malay/Indonesian/Javanese | **Buraq, Burak, Buroq, Bouraq** | The spelling *Buroq* is especially visible in north-coast Javanese performance traditions. |
| European languages | Buraq, Burak, Borak, Boraq, Bouraq | Transliteration rather than separate ancient names. |

No securely established pre-Islamic inscription, theophoric name, papyrus, or Arabic poem naming this creature has been identified.

### 1.3 Etymology

Medieval Arabic lexicographers and commentators connected *Burāq* with the root **b-r-q**:

- **barq** (بَرْق): lightning;
- **baraqa**: to flash, gleam, glisten, or shine;
- **barqāʾ**: an animal with contrasting or variegated coloring.

Ibn Durayd (d. 933) reportedly favored derivation from “lightning,” understood through the creature’s speed. Al-Nawawī (d. 1277), commenting on Muslim’s *Ṣaḥīḥ*, records multiple explanations: speed like lightning, brilliance or gleaming, whiteness, or variegated coloring. Al-Damīrī (d. 1405) transmitted comparable lexical discussion. None can be demonstrated as the single historical origin of the name; these are medieval learned etymologies, not contemporary seventh-century explanations. The common translation “Lightning” is therefore plausible but interpretive.

### 1.4 Epithets and titles

Burāq possesses no stable litany of ancient cult titles. Documented descriptive expressions include:

- **al-dābba al-bayḍāʾ** — “the white riding creature/animal,” canonical hadith description.
- **fawqa al-ḥimār wa-dūna al-baghl** — “larger than a donkey and smaller than a mule,” canonical Sunni formula.
- **dābba min dawābb al-janna** — “a creature among the creatures of Paradise,” especially later and Shiʿi reports.
- **markab/maṭiyya al-nabī** — “the Prophet’s mount,” later devotional and scholarly usage.
- **al-Burāq al-nabawī al-sharīf** — approximately “the Prophet’s noble Burāq,” found in modern popular prints.
- **rākib al-Burāq** — “rider of Burāq,” an epithet of Muḥammad in later devotional prayer and praise poetry, rather than an epithet of the animal.

### 1.5 Classification

| Source stratum | Classification |
|---|---|
| Qurʾān | None: Burāq is unnamed and undescribed. |
| Early sīra and hadith | A **dābba**, a riding animal; white, exceptionally swift, intermediate in size between donkey and mule. |
| Some medieval reports | A paradisiacal animal used by prophets, especially Abraham and Muḥammad. |
| Persianate and Ottoman visual culture | Celestial hybrid, often equine-bodied, winged, crowned, and human- or female-headed. |
| South Asian and Southeast Asian popular culture | Winged female-headed horse-like hybrid; sometimes furnished with a peacock tail, jewels, or composite-animal body. |
| Modern fantasy taxonomy | “Mythical beast,” “winged horse,” or “Islamic Pegasus”; these are comparative modern categories, not precise early Islamic classifications. |

Burāq is not classified in authoritative early sources as an angel, jinn, demon, god, goddess, or prophet.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 The Qurʾānic substrate

Qurʾān 17:1 states that God took “His servant” by night from *al-masjid al-ḥarām* to *al-masjid al-aqṣā* to show him divine signs. The verse names neither Muḥammad nor Jerusalem nor Burāq, although classical Muslim exegesis overwhelmingly identifies the servant as Muḥammad and the destination with Jerusalem. The verse is the earliest securely transmitted textual foundation for the Night Journey, but **it is not an attestation of Burāq by name or form**.

Qurʾān 53:1–18 describes a visionary encounter involving the “Lote Tree of the Boundary.” Later interpreters related it to the Ascension, but the passage likewise contains no Burāq. Scholars disagree over whether the Qurʾānic passages originally concerned the same event that later narratives call *isrāʾ wa-miʿrāj*. The disagreement cannot be resolved by inserting the later animal into the Qurʾān.

### 2.2 Earliest recoverable named evidence

The earliest substantial literary witness is the *Sīra* of **Muḥammad ibn Isḥāq** (d. 767), composed in Arabic in the eighth century. Ibn Isḥāq’s original book does not survive as an autograph or complete independent manuscript. Its material is reconstructed chiefly through Ibn Hishām’s recension (d. 833/834), al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), and quotations by other authors.

Ibn Isḥāq collected multiple, sometimes incompatible accounts. In the Night Journey complex Burāq appears as the mount brought by Gabriel. The text also preserves disagreement over whether Muḥammad’s experience was bodily or visionary: reports associated with ʿĀʾisha and Muʿāwiya were used to support a spiritual or visionary understanding, while the dominant later Sunni reading treated it as bodily. Those disagreements concern the journey more than Burāq’s anatomy, but they materially affect whether the creature is construed as a physical mount, a visionary entity, or both.

An early tradition associated with al-Zuhrī (d. 742) and Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyab (d. ca. 713) says that Burāq was the animal on which Abraham visited Ishmael. Because the report survives in later compilations, it is evidence for early attribution, not a surviving first/eighth-century document in the reporters’ hands.

### 2.3 Major Sunni hadith witnesses

#### Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī

Muḥammad ibn Ismāʿīl al-Bukhārī died in 870. His Arabic *Ṣaḥīḥ* survives through later manuscript transmission. In the Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa/Anas complex, Burāq is a white animal, smaller than a mule and larger than a donkey. Muḥammad sets out with Gabriel; the narration rapidly proceeds into the heavenly ascent and meetings with prophets. It does not describe a human face, female sex, peacock tail, or wings.

The creature’s stride is described in a related version as reaching the farthest point of its sight. This formula, not wings, is the canonical textual explanation of extraordinary speed.

#### Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim

Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj died in 875. *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 162a, through Anas from Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa, gives the fullest widely accepted basic description:

- Burāq is white and elongated;
- larger than a donkey, smaller than a mule;
- it places its hoof at the limit of its vision;
- Muḥammad rides to Jerusalem;
- he tethers it to the ring used by the prophets;
- he enters the sanctuary and prays;
- the heavenly ascent follows.

This report is the most important canonical textual anchor for the creature.

#### Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī

Al-Tirmidhī died in 892. His collection preserves a dramatic interaction: Burāq is brought bridled and saddled but shies or proves refractory. Gabriel rebukes it, asking whether it behaves so toward Muḥammad when no one more honored by God has ridden it; Burāq then sweats. This gives the creature emotion, bodily reaction, and limited agency. It remains an animal, not a speaking deity.

The wording “no one more honored … has ridden you” implies prior riders. Later authors use this to support the tradition that earlier prophets rode Burāq.

### 2.4 Other early and medieval corpora

- **Musnad Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal** (compiler d. 855): preserves Night Journey variants, including reports associated with Ḥudhayfa. Some versions imply Gabriel and Muḥammad together on the mount; wording varies.
- **Tafsīr and Tārīkh of al-Ṭabarī** (d. 923): gather alternative ascension reports, including weakly transmitted expansive narratives. Al-Ṭabarī is indispensable evidence for variation, but inclusion does not mean endorsement.
- **Tafsīr al-Thaʿlabī** (d. 1035) and later *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*: expand descriptions and prophetic prehistory.
- **Al-Qushayrī** (d. 1072), **al-Sulamī** (d. 1021), and later Sufi commentators: spiritualize the Ascension as a model of proximity to God. Burāq can become an allegory of resolve, love, or the purified lower soul, but no single allegorical equation is universal.
- **Al-Baghawī** (d. 1122), **al-Zamakhsharī** (d. 1144), **Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī** (d. 1210), **al-Qurṭubī** (d. 1273), **Ibn Kathīr** (d. 1373): synthesize Qurʾān, hadith, theology, and linguistic explanation.
- **Abū al-Ḥasan al-Bakrī**, under whose name an expanded *miʿrāj* narrative circulated: the attributed author is historically uncertain. The work elaborates temptations, marvels, punishments, and dialogue. Frederick Colby’s textual work shows that “Ibn ʿAbbās” and Bakrī recensions underwent substantial permutation; attribution to Ibn ʿAbbās cannot be treated as proof of first-century composition.
- **Al-Damīrī, Ḥayāt al-ḥayawān al-kubrā** (late fourteenth century): integrates Burāq into learned animal lore and reviews etymology and reports.
- **Shiʿi compilations:** Imami traditions likewise accept Burāq as Muḥammad’s miraculous mount. Later collections, including al-Majlisī’s *Biḥār al-anwār* (seventeenth century), transmit descriptions of it as a beautiful paradisiacal creature capable, by divine permission, of crossing this world and the next in a single course. Such reports must be dated to their individual earlier sources where possible; the date of al-Majlisī’s compilation is not the date of every report it contains.
- **Miʿrāj-nāmas:** Arabic, Persian, Turkic, and Urdu narrative books amplify the journey into tours of heaven and hell. Burāq becomes increasingly pictorial and physiognomically elaborate.

### 2.5 Manuscript gap

No seventh-century manuscript describing Burāq is known. The principal named accounts survive in codices copied generations or centuries after their compilers. Isnād analysis may recover earlier circulation, but it does not turn later parchment or paper into a contemporary artifact. The safest formulation is:

1. Qurʾān 17:1 supplies a seventh-century Night Journey statement without a mount.
2. Burāq is securely present in the eighth-century sīra tradition as transmitted later.
3. Detailed, widely canonical descriptions are securely documented in ninth-century Sunni compilations.
4. Human-headed and winged physiognomy is securely visible only in much later art.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### 3.1 Domain and function

Burāq does not “govern” lightning, travel, heaven, horses, or any human activity. Its functions are episodic:

- miraculous transport;
- passage between sacred centers;
- connection between terrestrial Jerusalem and the heavenly ascent;
- validation of prophetic continuity;
- demonstration of Muḥammad’s rank;
- in art, immediate recognition of the *miʿrāj* scene.

It is therefore closer to a **sacred mount and narrative instrument** than a domain-bearing god.

### 3.2 Secure early attributes

From the canonical hadith layer:

- white;
- physically long or tall;
- larger than a donkey and smaller than a mule;
- hoof or stride reaches the horizon of sight;
- saddled and bridled in al-Tirmidhī;
- capable of fear, reluctance, or agitation;
- sweats after Gabriel’s rebuke;
- can be tethered;
- associated with earlier prophets.

No sound early report establishes:

- wings;
- a human or woman’s face;
- a peacock tail;
- horns;
- a crown;
- speech;
- a fixed sex;
- independent intercession;
- residence in a named heavenly stable.

### 3.3 Sex and gender

Arabic grammatical gender does not reliably establish biological sex. *Dābba* is grammatically feminine; pronoun variation and translations have led to “he,” “she,” and “it.” Some later artistic and literary traditions explicitly feminize the face or creature. The canonical Sunni description supplies no unambiguous biological sex.

### 3.4 Development of iconography

The familiar image—a white winged horse with a beautiful woman’s face—is **not early hadith description**. It is a medieval Persianate visual construction drawing on a wider repertoire of human-headed winged creatures. Comparisons with the sphinx, harpy, siren, Pegasus, Huma, or Central Asian shamanic mounts may identify formal parallels, but no surviving document proves a single direct source.

Recognizable visual traits came to include:

- equine or mule-like body;
- white hide, sometimes spotted or jeweled;
- large feathered wings;
- human, commonly feminine, face;
- crown, tiara, earrings, or jeweled headdress;
- peacock-like tail;
- saddle and harness;
- Muḥammad mounted, with Gabriel leading or accompanying;
- angels bearing censers, trays, banners, or flames;
- celestial clouds and gold ground.

### 3.5 Major surviving images

1. **Ilkhanid Persian manuscripts, early fourteenth century.** Among the earliest securely surviving illustrated *miʿrāj* cycles are Ilkhanid works associated with Rashīd al-Dīn’s historical production and the illustrated *Jāmiʿ al-tawārīkh* milieu. Attribution of individual dispersed folios can be difficult; these already show a developed pictorial tradition.

2. **Topkapı Palace Library H. 2154, fourteenth century.** A major illustrated ascension manuscript cited in the art-historical literature. It documents that the narrative had become a courtly book-art subject.

3. **BnF Supplément turc 190, Herat, 840 AH/1436 CE.** This Chaghatay Turkic *Miʿrāj-nāma*, written in Uighur script under Timurid patronage, is the most celebrated complete illustrated cycle. Burāq bears Muḥammad through an extensively visualized cosmos. The manuscript is evidence for fifteenth-century Timurid narrative and imagery, not a facsimile of seventh-century belief.

4. **Shāh Ṭahmāsp’s *Khamsa* of Niẓāmī, British Library Or. 2265, ca. 1539–43.** Folio 195r shows Muḥammad ascending on Burāq, guided by Jibrāʾīl and surrounded by angels. The work belongs to Safavid court art.

5. **Būstān of Saʿdī folio, ca. 1525–35, Metropolitan Museum 1974.294.2.** Probably written in Herat and illustrated in Bukhara. Muḥammad is shown unveiled on Burāq amid angels. It demonstrates that unveiled prophetic portrayal remained possible in elite Muslim patronage.

6. **Deccan “Fabulous Creature Buraq,” ca. 1660–80, Metropolitan Museum 1992.17.** Burāq appears without Muḥammad, with a woman’s face and a body composed of many animals, fish, and birds. This is a regional Deccani composite-animal transformation, not a canonical description.

7. **Indian, Iranian, Ottoman, and Arab popular paintings, eighteenth–twentieth centuries.** The female-headed winged equine becomes an independent devotional image. Peacock tails and richly jeweled tack become especially prominent.

8. **Maghribi chromolithographs and reverse-glass painting, ca. 1900 onward.** Recent scholarship documents a transregional revival through print networks spanning the Levant, North Africa, South Asia, and West Africa.

### 3.6 Veiling and aniconism

Islamic attitudes toward figural sacred art have never been uniform. Manuscript images were generally private or courtly objects, not mosque icons. From the early Safavid period the flame halo and facial veil increasingly marked Muḥammad’s sanctity; later artists sometimes replaced his body with a flame, cloud, rose, name, or calligraphic form. Damage to faces in surviving manuscripts documents later censorship or devotional alteration, but each object must be examined separately.

### 3.7 Supposed correspondences

No authoritative source assigns Burāq:

- a sacred weekday;
- planet or zodiacal sign;
- number;
- plant;
- metal;
- incense;
- sacrificial victim;
- magical seal.

Modern occult correspondences advertised online as “ancient Islamic lore” are unsubstantiated unless tied to a specific dated text.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### 4.1 Genealogy

No early or canonical genealogy exists. Burāq has:

- no named parents;
- no birth narrative;
- no consort;
- no offspring;
- no siblings;
- no species history securely described.

“Animal of Paradise” is a classification or provenance, not a genealogy.

### 4.2 Hierarchical position

Burāq is subordinate to God’s command and Gabriel’s direction. It carries prophets but does not outrank them. In the al-Tirmidhī episode Gabriel disciplines it, and Muḥammad’s divinely granted honor is explicitly superior to the mount’s reluctance.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 The Night Journey to Jerusalem

**Early core:** God takes the servant by night from the sacred sanctuary to the farther sanctuary (Q 17:1). Burāq is absent.

**Eighth-century sīra form:** Gabriel comes to Muḥammad with Burāq. The creature is compared in size with a donkey and mule; its extraordinary locomotion carries Muḥammad northward. Expanded accounts add stops at places later connected with Medina, Sinai, Bethlehem, or other prophetic sites. These stations vary and are not all in the earliest recoverable form.

At Jerusalem, Muḥammad tethers Burāq. Muslim 162a specifies the ring used by the prophets. Muḥammad enters the sacred place and prays two units. Other versions have him lead earlier prophets in prayer. The episode signifies prophetic continuity and, in later exegesis, Muḥammad’s precedence among prophets.

**Variant:** Gabriel rather than Muḥammad pierces a stone or secures the animal. Medieval Jerusalem traditions variously locate the tether at a gate, in the southern/qibla area, beside a stone, or at the western wall. These cannot be collapsed into one topographical statement.

### 5.2 Burāq’s reluctance

In al-Tirmidhī’s ninth-century compilation, the saddled and bridled Burāq shies from Muḥammad. Gabriel says, in effect, that no one more honorable to God has ridden it. The animal breaks into a sweat.

This short episode does several things:

- acknowledges earlier riders;
- establishes Muḥammad’s superior honor;
- anthropomorphizes the mount without making it human;
- converts reluctance into submission under angelic correction.

Later retellings expand the scene into dialogue, vows, or requests for intercession. Those expansions are not in the concise canonical report.

### 5.3 The Ascension through the heavens

The Night Journey and heavenly Ascension were transmitted in multiple configurations.

1. **Burāq only to Jerusalem:** Muḥammad dismounts and tethers it; ascent follows by a ladder (*miʿrāj*), angelic conveyance, or unspecified divine means.
2. **Burāq continuing upward:** Some narrations and most later pictures show the creature bearing Muḥammad through the heavens.
3. **Narrative elision:** Al-Bukhārī’s report can move directly from mounting Burāq to the gate of the lowest heaven, encouraging the integrated visual interpretation.

During the ascent Muḥammad meets Adam, Jesus, John, Joseph, Idrīs, Aaron, Moses, and Abraham in successive heavens; visits the Lote Tree and the Frequented House; and receives the prayer obligation, reduced from fifty to five after repeated consultation with Moses. Burāq has little or no independent action in these celestial scenes. Later art nevertheless keeps it beneath Muḥammad because a single mount makes the entire itinerary visually intelligible.

### 5.4 Abraham’s mount

A tradition attributed to early Medinan authorities states that Abraham rode Burāq to visit Hagar and Ishmael. This retrojects the creature into Abrahamic sacred geography and makes Muḥammad’s use part of a prophetic inheritance. It is not in Genesis, the Qurʾānic Abraham narratives, or the principal basic version of Muslim 162a.

### 5.5 The prophetic tethering ring

Muslim’s report says Muḥammad tied Burāq to the ring used by prophets. Medieval Jerusalem topography localized the ring with increasing precision. A small subterranean prayer place near the southwest of the Ḥaram came to be called the **Masjid al-Burāq**. The physical ring visible there is a devotional localization; its antiquity cannot be established from the hadith.

The localization eventually contributed to the name **Ḥāʾiṭ al-Burāq**, “Burāq Wall,” for the western retaining wall of the Ḥaram. The exact historical emergence and prevalence of this name are debated; it should not be assumed to have been the universal Arabic name since the seventh century.

### 5.6 Expanded tours of heaven and hell

Medieval *miʿrāj-nāmas* turn the journey into a panoramic apocalypse. Muḥammad, often still on Burāq, sees:

- ranks of angels;
- cosmic animals;
- Paradise and its inhabitants;
- Hell and punishments fitted to sins;
- personifications or guardians;
- celestial rivers and trees;
- divine inscriptions or lights.

These works served preaching, moral instruction, courtly entertainment, mystical reflection, and visual spectacle. Their detailed punishments and creatures vary significantly across recensions. They cannot be treated as a single prophetic transcript.

### 5.7 Eschatological Burāq

Late narratives sometimes provide Burāq-like mounts for the righteous at resurrection or make Burāq seek the privilege of carrying Muḥammad. Some grant it a role in intercession or salvation. These motifs belong to expanded devotional and apocalyptic literature, not to Qurʾān 17:1 or the canonical physical description.

### 5.8 Comparative theories

Scholars have compared the *miʿrāj* complex with:

- Jewish hekhalot and merkabah ascent traditions;
- the ascensions of Enoch and Moses;
- Christian tours of heaven and hell;
- Zoroastrian *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*;
- royal and shamanic celestial journeys;
- winged mounts of Greco-Roman and Iranian art.

Such parallels illuminate the late-antique environment and later artistic vocabulary. They do not establish that Burāq is simply Pegasus, a cherub, a sphinx, or a renamed Zoroastrian animal. No ancient equation comparable to *interpretatio graeca* is documented.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### 6.1 Absence of a Burāq cult

There is no evidence for:

- sacrifice to Burāq;
- priests of Burāq;
- an independent temple;
- formal prayers addressed to Burāq;
- an oracle;
- Burāq as a recipient of obligatory worship;
- a canonical feast dedicated to the animal alone.

Calling its reception a “cult” without qualification would therefore be misleading.

### 6.2 Commemoration of the Night Journey

The broader event is commemorated as **Laylat al-Isrāʾ wa-l-Miʿrāj**, **Shab-e Miʿrāj**, or Turkish **Miraç Kandili**, commonly on 27 Rajab. Practices can include:

- Qurʾān recitation;
- sermons;
- praise poetry;
- communal prayer;
- illumination of mosques;
- narrative recitation;
- children’s instruction;
- images in regions permitting devotional figuration.

The date is traditional rather than securely fixed by the earliest sources. Burāq appears in preaching and imagery, but the observance is directed toward God and the prophetic miracle.

### 6.3 Jerusalem sites

**Masjid al-Burāq:** a small subterranean prayer space near the southwest corner of the al-Aqsa/Ḥaram compound. Tradition associates a wall ring with the tethering episode. The building is a memorial localization, not a shrine housing the being.

**Burāq/Western Wall:** the western retaining wall is sacred to Jews as the Western Wall and bound to Muslim memory of the Ḥaram and, in modern Arabic usage, Burāq. Competing religious, property, and political claims intensified under the British Mandate.

The 1928–29 dispute over screens, furniture, access, and ritual at the wall helped precipitate the violence of August 1929. The British Shaw Commission investigated the disturbances; a subsequent international commission ruled on rights and practices. The figure’s name thus entered anticolonial and nationalist vocabulary in **Thawrat al-Burāq**, the Burāq uprising. This is political reception of a sacred toponym, not worship of the animal.

### 6.4 Images as devotional and domestic objects

Medieval manuscript paintings were predominantly courtly or elite book arts. By the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, lithography, chromolithography, reverse-glass painting, posters, textiles, and calendars carried Burāq into homes, shops, processions, and informal devotional settings.

An image could function as:

- remembrance of the Prophet;
- visualization of an otherwise unseen miracle;
- blessing-bearing decoration;
- sectarian or political emblem;
- cosmographic teaching device.

These uses do not automatically amount to worship. Some Muslim authorities reject such images as fabricated or impermissible precisely because the human face and wings lack sound hadith support.

### 6.5 Javanese performance

In Brebes and Cirebon on Java’s north coast, **Buroq** performance employs a large processional puppet with a beautiful female face, wings, and equine body, accompanied by frame drums, music, dance, and community festivities. It may appear at circumcisions and other celebrations. Scholarship interprets it as a local Muslim artistic expression derived from *isrāʾ-miʿrāj* imagery. Its performative form is regional and modern; it is not evidence for seventh-century ritual.

### 6.6 Magic and talismanic use

Burāq appears in some cosmographic, lithographic, and talismanic environments alongside Qurʾānic writing, numerical diagrams, the Kaʿba, prophetic footprints, or names of sacred figures. Each artifact requires individual analysis. No general Islamic magical rite centered on summoning Burāq is securely documented.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Period I: Qurʾānic foundation, seventh century

The Qurʾān records a divinely caused nocturnal journey but does not name a vehicle. Nothing resembling the later iconography is documented. The historical date of the journey is variously placed around 620–621, but early sources disagree on timing and circumstances.

### Period II: Formation of sīra and oral-report complexes, late seventh–eighth centuries

Reports associated with al-Zuhrī, Saʿīd ibn al-Musayyab, Anas, Mālik ibn Ṣaʿṣaʿa, Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, Ḥudhayfa, Ibn ʿAbbās, and others circulate in different forms. Burāq becomes the named mount between Mecca and Jerusalem. Abrahamic prehistory and the prophets’ tethering ring connect the journey to earlier revelation.

Ibn Isḥāq’s compilation is the earliest broad narrative witness. Already there is no single story: dream and bodily-journey understandings coexist, as do distinct *isrāʾ* and ascent traditions.

### Period III: Canonization and harmonization, ninth–tenth centuries

Al-Bukhārī, Muslim, Aḥmad, al-Tirmidhī, and al-Ṭabarī preserve overlapping report families. The durable description—white, between donkey and mule, horizon-spanning stride—becomes canonical.

At the same time, compilations combine:

- opening of Muḥammad’s breast;
- Burāq;
- Jerusalem;
- prophetic prayer;
- ascent through seven heavens;
- choice of milk over wine;
- divine prescription of prayer;
- Moses’ repeated advice.

The now-familiar unified plot emerges through transmission and arrangement, though variants remain visible.

### Period IV: Exegetical, mystical, and topographical expansion, eleventh–thirteenth centuries

Commentators debate bodily versus spiritual ascent, the relationship of Q 17 and Q 53, and the location of *al-masjid al-aqṣā*. Sufi authors interpret the ascent as a paradigm for spiritual realization. Jerusalem pilgrimage literature localizes stations, rocks, gates, and tethering sites.

Persian poets incorporate *miʿrāj* praise passages into epic and romance prefaces. Burāq begins to move from hadith animal into a richly symbolic literary image.

### Period V: Illustrated court cultures, fourteenth–sixteenth centuries

Ilkhanid, Timurid, Ottoman, and Safavid ateliers produce elaborate ascension images. Burāq acquires wings and a human or feminine head. The 1436 Herat *Miʿrāj-nāma* supplies an extensive visual cycle.

By the sixteenth century, facial veiling and flame halos increasingly mark Muḥammad’s sacred status. Burāq remains fully visible, making it an especially useful surrogate where prophetic figuration becomes restricted.

### Period VI: Regional proliferation, seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

Deccani painters depict Burāq independently as a fabulous composite creature. Ottoman, Persian, Mughal, and provincial workshops repeat and vary the iconography. Peacock-tail and jewelry motifs become widespread. None is securely derived from canonical hadith anatomy.

### Period VII: Print globalization and political localization, nineteenth–mid-twentieth centuries

Cheap print and reverse-glass painting globalize the female-headed, winged form. Syrian, Egyptian, Maghribi, Iranian, Indian, and West African images circulate through colonial trade networks.

In Jerusalem, “Burāq Wall” becomes politically charged. The 1929 disturbances and subsequent reports make *al-Burāq* a name of mass mobilization, sacred geography, and competing nationalism.

### Period VIII: Contemporary vernacular and global culture, late twentieth century–present

Burāq persists in:

- Islamic children’s books and animation;
- sermon illustrations;
- Pakistani and Indian calendar art;
- Indonesian glass painting and Buroq processions;
- Filipino Muslim carving;
- modern Iranian and Arab art;
- airline and technology branding;
- fantasy encyclopedias and internet imagery.

At the same time, reformist Muslim writers increasingly emphasize that wings and a woman’s head are unattested in sound hadith. Contemporary reception therefore divides between iconographic tradition and textualist correction.

There is no demonstrable modern organized worship of Burāq as a deity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### 8.1 Literature

Jorge Luis Borges and Margarita Guerrero included “El Burak” in the expanded *El libro de los seres imaginarios* (1967; English *The Book of Imaginary Beings*, 1969). Borges’s bestiary helped detach Burāq from specifically Islamic devotional narrative and introduce it to international fantastic literature. This is literary reception, not an ancient source.

Claims that the Islamic *miʿrāj* directly caused Dante’s *Divine Comedy* have a long modern history, especially following Miguel Asín Palacios. Scholars recognize parallels and possible channels through Arabic-Latin and Iberian materials but debate direct dependence. Burāq itself cannot be identified as a character in Dante.

### 8.2 Modern art

- **Siah Armajani, *Meem* (1958):** a painted textile now in the British Museum juxtaposes Gabriel, Burāq, Qurʾānic writing, and geomantic elements.
- **A.D. Pirous, *Sura Isra II: Homage to My Mother* (1982):** Indonesian modernist engagement with the Qurʾānic journey.
- **Haryadi Suadi, *Buroq* (1986):** glass painting drawing on Cirebonese vernacular imagery.
- Contemporary artists reuse medieval *miʿrāj* imagery to address migration, revelation, colonialism, national identity, or the tension between calligraphy and figuration.

### 8.3 Transport and technology

The name’s associations with lightning speed and supernatural flight have made it commercially attractive:

- **Bouraq Indonesia Airlines**, founded in the twentieth century and closed in 2005, took its name from the flying mount.
- **Buraq Air** in Libya similarly uses the creature’s name for aviation.
- “Buraq/Burak” has been adopted for vehicles, missiles, aerospace projects, businesses, and software. Such use is modern branding, not continuation of cult.

### 8.4 Political use

The name **al-Burāq** remains embedded in Palestinian Muslim terminology for the Western Wall and in commemoration of the 1929 uprising. Conversely, Jewish discourse ordinarily uses *Ha-Kotel Ha-Maʿaravi*, “Western Wall.” Naming is therefore not neutral: it encodes different sacred histories and political claims.

The violence of 1929 should not be attributed to belief in Burāq alone. British colonial administration, property and access disputes, communal mobilization, rumors, nationalist organization, and religious symbolism interacted. The Shaw Commission is a primary colonial investigation, not an impartial final interpretation.

### 8.5 Modern inventions and recurrent errors

The following are late, regional, or unsupported when presented as universal ancient facts:

- **“The Qurʾān says Muḥammad rode Burāq.”** False as a textual claim; the Qurʾān does not name it.
- **“Burāq was a winged horse.”** Established in later art, not in the canonical physical description.
- **“Burāq had a woman’s face.”** Medieval and later iconography, not early hadith.
- **“Burāq was definitely female.”** Not established by the earliest sources.
- **“Burāq means lightning because it literally was lightning.”** The lexical derivation is plausible; ontological identity with lightning is not attested.
- **“Burāq is the Islamic Pegasus.”** A modern analogy, not a historical equation.
- **“Muslims worship Burāq.”** Unsupported and contrary to normative Islamic classification.
- **“The visible ring in Jerusalem is archaeologically proven to be Muḥammad’s tethering ring.”** Not established.
- **“Every detail of illustrated *miʿrāj-nāmas* comes from sound hadith.”** Incorrect; these works incorporate later narrative and artistic invention.
- **Psychedelic-mushroom interpretations of Timurid Burāq imagery.** A speculative modern proposal without consensus support.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 9.1 Unity or combination of two journeys

A central debate asks whether *isrāʾ* and *miʿrāj* were initially separate:

- a horizontal journey to Jerusalem;
- a vertical ascent into heaven.

Older scholarship often reconstructed their later fusion rather schematically. Brooke Olson Vuckovic and Frederick Colby stress the plurality and productive variation of reports. The manuscript and hadith record supports multiple configurations, not a single recoverable ur-text.

### 9.2 Qurʾānic interpretation

Debated questions include:

- whether Q 17:1 originally referred to Muḥammad;
- whether *al-masjid al-aqṣā* meant Jerusalem, a celestial sanctuary, or another sacred location;
- whether Q 53 describes the same event;
- whether the journey was bodily, visionary, or dreamlike.

Classical consensus strongly favored Muḥammad and Jerusalem, but early disagreement about bodily versus visionary experience is genuinely attested. Burāq belongs to the interpretive narrative tradition, not the wording of the verses.

### 9.3 Dating hadith material

Traditional hadith scholarship evaluates chains and transmitters to distinguish sound from weak reports. Modern historians additionally compare:

- variant isnāds;
- wording clusters;
- geographic transmission;
- compiler arrangement;
- doctrinal and political function;
- manuscript dates.

A report attributed to a Companion is not automatically a seventh-century document. Conversely, compilation in the ninth century does not prove that every component was invented then. The evidence supports layered transmission.

### 9.4 Origins of Burāq

Proposals include derivation from:

- prophetic donkey traditions;
- Abrahamic riding-beast lore;
- late-antique heavenly mounts;
- Iranian royal or celestial animals;
- Pegasus-like imagery;
- sphinx and harpy iconography;
- Central Asian shamanic traditions.

Suliman Bashear explored riding beasts in divine-mission narratives; Vuckovic notes that Burāq may mediate symbolic ambivalence surrounding donkey and camel imagery. These are historical interpretations, not established native identifications.

### 9.5 Development of the image

Christiane Gruber’s work is foundational for the transformation from textual riding animal to pictorial hybrid. The key finding is negative as well as positive: early hadith supplies neither wings nor human face, while Persianate art eventually normalizes both.

Art historians debate whether the human-headed form derives primarily from:

- pre-Islamic Iranian winged beings;
- classical sphinx/siren motifs;
- Central Asian visual culture;
- generalized Persianate composite-creature conventions.

The most defensible account is cumulative adaptation within manuscript workshops, not descent from one demonstrable prototype.

### 9.6 Burāq and Jerusalem politics

Heribert Busse and later historians examined the incorporation of Jerusalem into the journey tradition and the development of sacred topography. Studies of the 1929 violence analyze how the wall’s religious meanings were transformed by Mandate law, print media, nationalist institutions, and mass politics.

It is methodologically unsafe either to declare the Muslim association timeless in its modern terminology or to dismiss it as invented in 1929. The tethering tradition is medieval and earlier; the standardized political use of “Burāq Wall” intensified in the modern conflict.

### 9.7 State of the evidence

Important repositories include:

- Bibliothèque nationale de France: Supplément turc 190;
- Topkapı Palace Library: major Ilkhanid and later illustrated manuscripts;
- British Library: Or. 2265 and other Persian *Khamsa* manuscripts;
- Metropolitan Museum of Art: Persian and Deccani Burāq paintings;
- British Museum: modern and South Asian objects;
- libraries holding Arabic hadith, tafsīr, sīra, and *miʿrāj-nāma* manuscripts.

The loss of Ibn Isḥāq’s original recension and the dispersed condition of many Ilkhanid manuscripts substantially limit reconstruction.

- Metropolitan Museum of Art and British Library manuscript and object catalogues.
- Recent studies of Maghribi chromolithography, South Asian devotional print, and Indonesian Buroq performance.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not that an already complete winged, female-headed horse appears in Islam’s founding text. It is that a Qurʾānic statement about divine nocturnal transport was joined, in early transmitted narrative, to a named white riding animal. Ninth-century hadith collections stabilized its size, speed, tethering, and relationship with Gabriel and Muḥammad. Medieval exegetes connected it with Abraham and prophetic continuity; storytellers expanded its agency; Persianate artists supplied wings and a human face; regional workshops added crowns, peacock tails, and composite bodies; mass print made that late image globally familiar; and modern politics attached its name to one of Jerusalem’s most contested sacred places. Those transformations, rather than a harmonized timeless portrait, constitute Burāq’s documented history.
