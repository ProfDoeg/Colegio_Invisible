# Hermanubis: Pantheon Dossier

*Requested filename: `hermanubis_pantheon_dossier.md`*

## Documentary scope and evidentiary caution

Hermanubis is unusually easy to overconstruct. The securely attested ancient corpus is small: one doubtful Hellenistic inscriptional restoration, three reasonably secure divine-name inscriptions of the Roman period, a cluster of Roman Egyptian personal names derived from the god’s name, two philosophical descriptions, two late-antique literary satires, Alexandrian coin types, several sculptures conventionally attributed to him, and related magical gems. There is no surviving ancient narrative cycle devoted to Hermanubis, no securely identified scripture or hymn in his name, no documented genealogy peculiar to him, and no evidence for a large independent temple establishment comparable to those of Isis, Sarapis, Hermes, or Anubis.

“Hermanubis” must therefore be distinguished at every stage from:

1. Egyptian Anubis before the Hellenistic period;
2. Anubis interpreted as Hermes;
3. jackal-headed Anubis carrying Hermetic attributes;
4. the fully anthropomorphic Alexandrian youth accompanied by a dog, conventionally called Hermanubis by modern art historians;
5. human beings named Hermanubis;
6. modern occult images called Hermanubis.

These categories overlap historically, but they are not interchangeable. In particular, none of the surviving sculptures or coin images conventionally identified as the anthropomorphic Hermanubis carries an ancient identifying legend. Amin Benaissa’s demonstration that the images, divine name, and personal names share substantially the same Roman-period chronology and Alexandrian/Cynopolite geography makes the identification plausible, not epigraphically certain. [Benaissa, “The Onomastic Evidence for the God Hermanubis”](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/i/icp/7523866.0025.116/--onomastic-evidence-for-the-god-hermanubis?rgn=main%3Bview%3Dfulltext)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### 1.1 Identification

Hermanubis was a Greco-Egyptian divine form produced through the historical assimilation of Egyptian Anubis with Greek Hermes. Ancient authors do not provide one fixed classification. Plutarch treats “Hermanubis” as a name of Anubis in his relation to the lower world. Porphyry describes him as “composite” and “half-Greek.” In inscriptions he is straightforwardly a *theos*, “god.” Modern descriptions such as “fusion,” “hybrid,” and “syncretic god” are analytical shorthand; they should not be mistaken for a surviving foundation myth in which Hermes and Anubis literally combine.

The functional basis of the equation was principally psychopompic. Hermes conducted souls and crossed divine, human, and infernal boundaries; Anubis guarded and prepared the dead and conducted the deceased toward Osirian judgment. This functional resemblance enabled Greeks to interpret Anubis through Hermes without extinguishing Anubis’s Egyptian identity. Hermes was also conventionally equated with Thoth, so Egyptian, Greek, and Roman sources could sustain several overlapping but non-identical equations: Hermes–Thoth, Hermes–Anubis, and eventually Hermanubis.

### 1.2 Greek names

The principal divine name is:

- **Ἑρμάνουβις — Hermánoubis**. Benaissa, following Willy Clarysse, prefers proparoxytone *Hermánoubis* rather than the often printed oxytone **Ἑρμανοῦβις — Hermanoûbis**. Ancient accents are not written in the inscriptions themselves, so the distinction is editorial.
- **Ἑρμανούβηϲ — Hermanoubēs** occurs in the transmitted text of *Greek Anthology* XI.360. It is unattested in documents and may be a metrical or scribal form; Benaissa considers emendation to **Ἑρμάνουβιϲ** attractive.
- The dative **Ἑρμανούβιδι** appears in the Alexandrian dedication SB I 3482.
- **Ἑρμανούβιοϲ — Hermanoubios** is a derived personal name found at Dion in Macedonia, not another securely attested divine form.

The name transparently joins **Ἑρμ-**, from Ἑρμῆς (*Hermēs*), to **Ἄνουβις** (*Anoubis*), the Greek form of Egyptian Anubis. Greek compound divine names existed, but Benaissa stresses that Hermanubis differs from an ordinary personal “polytheophoric” name: it designated an independently attested god.

### 1.3 Egyptian and Coptic nomenclature

Egyptian Anubis is conventionally transliterated **jnpw**, with modern Egyptological vocalizations including *Inpu*, *Anpu*, and *Anup*. Because Egyptian scripts generally did not record vowels, no single modern vocalization can claim to reproduce the ancient pronunciation exactly. Greek **Ἄνουβις** and later Coptic forms preserve later stages of the name.

A Coptic lexical form **ϩⲉⲣⲙⲁⲛⲟⲩⲡ** (*hermanoup*) is reported in modern lexical compilations. It combines the name of Hermes with Coptic ⲁⲛⲟⲩⲡ, Anubis. Its mere lexical existence does not demonstrate a flourishing Coptic Christian-period cult, and no Coptic Hermanubis hymn or narrative has been securely established in the material surveyed here.

No hieroglyphic spelling uniquely naming the compound Hermanubis has been established. Modern internet claims that provide an elaborate hieroglyphic “Hermanubis” name are normally modern back-formations assembled from words or signs for Hermes and Anubis.

### 1.4 Secure epithets and titles

The securely documented cult titles are few:

- **θεὸς μέγας — “great god.”** Alexandrian Serapeum-area dedication, SB I 3482, broadly Greco-Roman and not more closely dated by the surviving text.
- **ἐπήκοος — “one who listens,” “hearkening [to prayer].”** Same inscription. This is a common beneficent cult epithet, emphasizing responsiveness to petition.
- **εὐχάριστος — “gracious,” “beneficent,” or “granting favor.”** Same inscription.
- **νικηφόρος — “victory-bearing.”** Restored in the fragmentary Delian inscription ID 2156: **[Ἑρ]μανού[βιδι νικ]ηφόρωι**. Both the divine name and epithet depend on restoration, so neither should be treated as secure evidence without qualification.
- **ψυχοπομπός — “conductor of souls.”** This is an appropriate modern functional description inherited from Hermes’s established Greek title, not a securely documented epithet attached to Hermanubis in an extant dedication.
- **σύνθετος — “composite”** and **μιξέλλην — “half-Greek” or “mixed-Greek.”** Porphyry’s late-third-century philosophical characterizations, preserved by Eusebius; descriptive categories rather than demonstrated temple epithets.

### 1.5 Interpretive identifications

- **Hermanubis as Anubis:** Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* 61, approximately late first or early second century CE, explicitly says the power called Anubis “is sometimes also named Hermanubis,” differentiating upper and lower orientations.
- **Anubis as Hermes:** This equation is broader and earlier than the securely attested compound name. It arises from psychopompic comparison and appears in literary, epigraphic, and iconographic material.
- **Hermanubis as “Egyptian Mercury”:** Latin and modern occult literature often uses Mercury, the Roman interpretive equivalent of Hermes. This is interpretatio Romana layered over the Greek-Egyptian formation.
- **Hermanubis and Hermes-Thoth:** Related but not identical. The well-attested Hermes–Thoth equation does not prove that Hermanubis was simply Thoth-Anubis.
- **Hermanubis and Antinous:** Some Hadrianic Alexandrian coin portraits of the deified Antinous have been described as assimilating him to Hermes or Hermanubis. Because the coins do not label the reverse figure Hermanubis, the more cautious formulation is “Antinous in a Hermetic/Anubian visual register,” not a secure cult equation.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### 2.1 Proposed Hellenistic attestation: Delos

The chronologically earliest proposed occurrence is the second-century BCE Delian dedication ID 2156.2, restored as:

> **[Ἑρ]μανού[βιδι νικ]ηφόρωι**  
> “[To Her]manubis, [vict]ory-bearing.”

This would place the name in the cosmopolitan sanctuary environment of Delos, where Egyptian cults flourished. It is not a completely preserved occurrence: crucial portions of both name and epithet are reconstructed. It therefore establishes a possible Hellenistic terminus, not a secure first attestation. Modern writers who state without qualification that Hermanubis “is first attested on Delos in the second century BCE” suppress the restoration problem.

### 2.2 Earliest secure literary occurrence: Plutarch

Plutarch of Chaeronea’s Greek treatise *De Iside et Osiride* (“On Isis and Osiris”), normally dated around the late first or early second century CE, supplies the earliest secure, connected explanation. At 375e (§61), Plutarch writes:

> ὁ δ’ ἀναφαίνων τὰ οὐράνια καὶ τῶν ἄνω φερομένων λόγος Ἄνουβις, ἔστι δ’ ὅτε καὶ Ἑρμάνουβις ὀνομάζεται, τὸ μὲν ὡς τοῖς ἄνω τὸ δ’ ὡς τοῖς κάτω προσήκων.

In sense: the *logos* that reveals heavenly things and belongs to what moves above is called Anubis, and sometimes Hermanubis, the former designation relating to things above and the latter to things below. Plutarch adds that worshippers sacrifice a white cock in the one aspect and a saffron-colored cock in the other, regarding the first as pure and manifest, the second as mixed and variegated. [Greek text of Plutarch §61](https://classics.andrewgadsden.com/library/plutarch/de-iside-et-osiride/1)

What this establishes:

- Hermanubis was known as a divine name by Plutarch’s time.
- Plutarch understood it as an aspect or designation of Anubis, not necessarily as a genealogically separate god.
- The name could be embedded in Middle Platonic allegorical theology.
- A differentiated cock sacrifice was associated with the upper/lower distinction.

What it does not establish:

- that Plutarch accurately reproduces a universally observed Egyptian ritual;
- that white and saffron became fixed “sacred colors” of an independent Hermanubis cult;
- that there was a canonical Egyptian scripture teaching precisely this doctrine.

Plutarch refers immediately afterward to “Books of Hermes,” but he does not say that a surviving Hermetic book was a scripture of Hermanubis.

### 2.3 Alexandrian dedication: SB I 3482

A white-marble votive stele from the Serapeum area of Alexandria bears the most important secure cult inscription:

> **Ἑρμανούβιδι θεῶι μεγάλωι  
> ἐπηκόωι καὶ εὐχαρίστωι …**  
> “To Hermanubis, great god, listening and beneficent …”

The dedicants are Sarapion son of Dionysius and Eukleia, identified as his sister and wife, acting for their son Dionysius. The inscription is dated internally to year 12, Hathyr 8, but the ruler’s era is not preserved, preventing a certain absolute date. It has been published as Breccia no. 120, SB I 3482, and Kayser no. 66. [PATHS Alexandria inscription dossier, pp. 12–13](https://paths-erc.eu/sacri-lapides-aegypti/alexandria/alexandria_inscription.pdf)

The inscription proves prayer-oriented devotion to Hermanubis in Alexandria. The suggestion that it specifically sought a happy afterlife for the son is an inference from the god’s funerary character; the text itself says only “for” the son.

### 2.4 Second Egyptian divine-name inscription

Benaissa records a second Roman-period Egyptian inscription naming the god, in addition to SB I 3482. The surviving published corpus is too fragmentary to construct from it a separate theology, ritual, or sanctuary history. Its importance is cumulative: Hermanubis is not an isolated literary coinage.

### 2.5 Thessalonike: an organized association

IG X.2.1 220, third century CE, records a cult association at Thessalonike calling itself:

> **θρησκευταὶ καὶ σηκοβάται θεοῦ Ἑρμανούβιδος**  
> “worshippers and *sekobatai* of the god Hermanubis.”

The association’s officers honored Claudius Auximus, also called Pieris, while Marcus Aurelius Justus served as **ἀρχινακόρος**, apparently a chief temple attendant or senior cult officer. The term *sekobatai* literally evokes those who enter or frequent a *sekos*, a sacred enclosure, but its precise ritual meaning is disputed. The inscription securely establishes an organized, officered Hermanubis association outside Egypt; it does not establish a large free-standing temple. [University of Copenhagen association database](https://assocgr.saxo.sc.ku.dk/GR/viewing.php?id=715&view=resultassoc); [Archaeological Museum of Thessaloniki](https://www.amth.gr/en/exhibitions/exhibit-of-the-month/2205)

### 2.6 Porphyry, *On Cult Images*

Porphyry wrote *Peri agalmatōn* (“On Cult Images”) in the later third century CE. The work is lost except for fragments quoted by the Christian author Eusebius of Caesarea in *Praeparatio evangelica* 3.11, written in the early fourth century.

Porphyry explains Hermes as reason and speech. Reason, he says, is composite: in the sun it is Hermes, in the moon Hecate, and in the whole Hermopan. He then adds:

> “Hermanubis also is composite, and as it were half-Greek, being found among the Egyptians also.”

This is a learned Neoplatonic systematization. It shows how an educated late-antique theologian classified the form, but it should not be retrojected into the second century BCE or treated as a popular creed. [Porphyry fragments preserved by Eusebius](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/porphyry_on_images_02_text.htm)

### 2.7 Gregory of Nazianzus, *De vita sua*

Gregory’s autobiographical poem, composed after the ecclesiastical struggle of 380 CE, mocks Egyptian sailors associated with Maximus the Cynic. Lines 838–840 list “Ammon, Apammon, Harpocras … Anubis, Hermanubis, gods of Egypt, ape-shaped and dog-like demons.”

This is Christian polemical satire. Hermanubis may be a sailor’s Egyptian personal name or a comic invention based on one. It documents the name’s intelligibility in late-fourth-century Constantinople and a hostile Christian association with Egyptian animal worship; it does not document a myth or ritual.

### 2.8 *Greek Anthology* XI.360

An undated imperial or late-antique satirical epigram describes a *strategos* “Hermanoubes” becoming a dog after seizing two “Hermeses,” thieves bound with rope and called premature Tartarean demons. The reading and syntax are uncertain. It is probably wordplay involving the personal name, Anubis’s canine form, and Hermes’s association with thieves. It supplies no secure cult narrative.

### 2.9 Papyrological onomastics

Benaissa catalogs at least twenty-two documentary occurrences of names formed from Hermanubis. They belong overwhelmingly to the Roman period, especially the second through fourth centuries CE. Forms include:

- Ἑρμάνουβις, direct use of the god’s name;
- Ἑρμανουβίων;
- Ἑρμανουβᾶς;
- Ἑρμανοβάμμων, joining Hermanubis and Ammon;
- abbreviated or damaged forms.

Notable bearers include:

- an Alexandrian female nurse named Ἑρμανουβις, perhaps accented *Hermanoubís*;
- an acting *epistrategos* of Heptanomia, documented in P.Oxy. LX 4071, approximately 241–244;
- an official in proceedings around 250;
- the father of an underage priest of “Anubis, Leto, and associated gods” in P.Oxy. X 1256, dated 282;
- persons linked with Alexandria, the Cynopolite nome, Hermopolis, Memphis, Panopolis, and elsewhere;
- Ἑρμανούβιος from imperial-period Dion in Macedonia.

These names demonstrate social familiarity and some devotional prestige. They do not prove that every bearer worshipped Hermanubis personally.

### 2.10 Magical texts and gems

No major spell in the Greek Magical Papyri securely invokes Hermanubis by the compound name in the evidence surveyed. Anubis, Hermes, Thoth, and Osiris occur abundantly, but their presence should not be relabeled Hermanubis automatically.

Magical gems present a harder attribution problem. A red jasper gem conventionally catalogued as “Hermanubis, mummy of Osiris” shows a canine/Hermetic figure attending Osiris’s mummy and carries an inscription interpreted as protection “against a daemon and fears.” The image may represent Hermeticized Anubis rather than the fully anthropomorphic Alexandrian type. Its modern catalogue title is therefore an attribution, not an ancient caption. [Campbell Bonner Magical Gems Database](https://cbd.mfab.hu/cbd/4250/?sid=2)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### 3.1 Domains securely or plausibly associated

**Psychopompic and funerary mediation.** This is the strongest reconstruction, based on the overlapping functions of Hermes and Anubis, Plutarch’s upper/lower distinction, Anubian funerary imagery, and the association with Osiris and Sarapis.

**Communication, interpretation, and revelation.** Porphyry places Hermanubis within a theology of Hermes as *logos*, reason, and speech. This is explicitly attested as late-antique philosophical interpretation, not as the wording of a cult prayer.

**Passage between upper and lower realms.** Plutarch’s distinction is the central textual evidence. Hermanubis belongs particularly to “things below,” while Anubis in the paired formulation belongs to “things above.” The passage resists a simplistic equation of Hermanubis exclusively with death: the point is mediation across cosmic levels.

**Responsiveness and beneficence.** The Alexandrian epithets *epēkoos* and *eucharistos* present him as a god who hears and grants favor.

**Victory or protection.** The restored Delian *nikēphoros* and occasional military or triumphant Anubian imagery suggest this function, but the Delian evidence is uncertain.

### 3.2 Iconographic type A: jackal-headed Anubis in Greek dress

Across the Greco-Roman world Anubis can appear with:

- human body and canine or jackal head;
- short tunic, mantle, or military dress;
- caduceus (*kērykeion*);
- palm branch;
- winged sandals;
- occasionally a sistrum or other Isiac apparatus.

Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* XI.11, describes jackal-headed Anubis in the procession of Isis, carrying a caduceus and palm. Apuleius calls him Anubis, not Hermanubis. Consequently, an image combining jackal head and caduceus is evidence for the Hermeticization of Anubis but not automatically for the compound divine name.

A marble statue in the Vatican, inventory MV.22840.0.0, is a representative Roman-period example. It was found at Anzio in the Villa Pamphilj excavations of 1749, entered papal possession in 1764, and was transferred from the Capitoline collection to the Vatican in 1838. The Vatican catalogue calls it Anubis, dates it to the first–second century CE, and notes the cloak and footwear. Modern popular captions frequently rename it Hermanubis, illustrating precisely the instability of the classification. [Vatican Museums catalogue](https://catalogo.museivaticani.va/index.php/Detail/objects/MV.22840.0.0?lang=en_US)

### 3.3 Iconographic type B: anthropomorphic Alexandrian “Hermanubis”

The type conventionally identified as Hermanubis is a fully human, youthful male:

- athletic, classicizing body;
- often wearing a chiton, himation, or chlamys;
- **calathos** or modius-like headdress, evoking Alexandrian gods such as Sarapis;
- caduceus, commonly combined with a palm;
- dog or jackal beside his feet;
- occasionally other attributes, including a club or lion skin in assimilative types.

Because the head is human, the accompanying dog becomes the principal Anubian index. The caduceus marks Hermes; the calathos locates the figure within Alexandrian divine iconography.

The most famous example is the marble statue from the Ras el-Soda sanctuary near Alexandria, approximately 1.25 metres high and dated to the early second century CE. It was excavated after the sanctuary’s discovery in 1936. The statue is now in the Graeco-Roman Museum, Alexandria, inventory 25785; it was displayed temporarily at the Bibliotheca Alexandrina between 2007 and August 2022. [Bibliotheca Alexandrina object record](https://antiquities.bibalex.org/Collection/Detail.aspx?a=1160)

The sanctuary’s statuary assemblage also included Isis, Harpocrates, and Osiris-Canopus. This demonstrates participation in an Isiac ensemble, although it does not prove that the four statues formed a narrative tableau. [Bibliotheca Alexandrina Ras el-Soda collection](https://antiquities.bibalex.org/Collection/Index.aspx?collection=45&lang=en)

### 3.4 Coin iconography

Alexandrian coinage provides the densest serial imagery. Types assigned to Hermanubis range from Trajan—or possibly Domitian—to the Tetrarchic period, with marked development under Hadrian and Antoninus Pius.

An Antoninus Pius Alexandrian bronze, RPC IV.4 562, dated to regnal year 5, 141/142 CE, shows a temple with two papyriform columns and rounded pediment. Inside stands the attributed Hermanubis, facing with head turned left, holding a palm leaf and caduceus, with a jackal at his feet. Other issues show:

- a bust with calathos, caduceus, and palm;
- the god standing alone;
- the god facing the enthroned Sarapis;
- paired or confronted divine busts;
- types connected with the Cynopolite nome.

The temple reverse is valuable evidence for an architectural cult setting, but coin buildings can be schematic. It supports, without conclusively locating, a Hermanubis shrine in Alexandria. [Roman Provincial Coinage IV.4, no. 562](https://rpc.ashmus.ox.ac.uk/coins/4.4/562)

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### 4.1 Genealogy

No surviving ancient text gives Hermanubis a distinct birth narrative, parents, consort, sibling group, or offspring. Genealogies of Anubis—such as versions making him a child of Osiris and Nephthys—belong to Anubis traditions unless a source explicitly transfers them to Hermanubis. Likewise, Hermes’s parentage from Zeus and Maia cannot simply be assigned to Hermanubis.

Porphyry’s reference to Eros as a son of Hermes follows his discussion of Hermes, reason, speech, and love. It does not unambiguously declare Eros the son of Hermanubis.

The historically warranted relational model is therefore not a family tree but a set of syncretic and cultic associations.

### 4.2 Every documented god–god interaction or joint appearance

Because no Hermanubis narrative cycle survives, most entries are equations or joint iconographic appearances, not actions in stories.

#### Anubis

- **Interaction:** Hermanubis is named as Anubis in a lower-world aspect.
- **Source/date:** Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* 61, late first–early second century CE.
- **Tier:** Secure Roman-period philosophical testimony.
- **Qualification:** Aspectual naming, not a scene in which two separate gods meet.

#### Hermes

- **Interaction:** The very name combines Hermes and Anubis; iconography joins caduceus and other Hermetic attributes to an Anubian body or dog companion.
- **Source/date:** Name inscriptions and Roman-period images; Porphyry, late third century CE.
- **Tier:** Secure syncretistic identification; mechanism reconstructed from comparative function.
- **Qualification:** No surviving myth narrates a literal fusion ceremony.

#### Sarapis

- **Interaction:** Hermanubis appears before or beside enthroned Sarapis on Alexandrian coins; paired busts also occur.
- **Source/date:** Alexandrian imperial coinage, particularly Trajanic–Antonine issues.
- **Tier:** Secure joint iconographic appearance, with the identification “Hermanubis” conventional but strongly supported.
- **Meaning:** Positions Hermanubis within Alexandria’s Sarapian/Isiac divine court.

#### Isis

- **Interaction:** Separate statues of Isis and attributed Hermanubis belonged to the Ras el-Soda sanctuary assemblage.
- **Source/date:** Ras el-Soda archaeological context, Roman period, statues commonly dated around the early second century CE.
- **Tier:** Secure joint sanctuary context; no narrative action.
- **Qualification:** Apuleius’s Isiac procession includes Anubis, not explicitly Hermanubis.

#### Harpocrates

- **Interaction:** Joint cultic context in the Ras el-Soda assemblage.
- **Source/date:** Roman-period sanctuary, Alexandria.
- **Tier:** Secure archaeological association; no surviving episode.

#### Osiris / Osiris-Canopus

- **Interaction:** Joint sanctuary context at Ras el-Soda; canine/Hermetic figures also attend Osiris’s mummy on magical gems.
- **Source/date:** Roman-period statuary and approximately second–third-century magical gems.
- **Tier:** Secure association at the level of assemblage; individual gem identifications vary.
- **Qualification:** The ancient Egyptian story of Anubis embalming Osiris is not independently retold with the name Hermanubis.

#### Heracles

- **Interaction:** Some Antonine coinage and gems pair the attributed Hermanubis type with Heracles, and a rare statue type carries club/lion-skin features.
- **Source/date:** Roman imperial iconography, principally second century CE.
- **Tier:** Regional syncretistic or associative iconography.
- **Qualification:** No textual myth of a Hermanubis–Heracles meeting survives.

#### Aphrodite

- **Interaction:** A gem shows Sarapis between attributed Hermanubis and a small Aphrodite Anadyomene, accompanied by **κατὰ χρηματισμόν**, “in accordance with an oracle.”
- **Source/date:** Roman imperial engraved gem.
- **Tier:** Secure joint image; precise ritual purpose uncertain.
- **Qualification:** Not a narrative of romance, marriage, or parentage.

#### Hecate

- **Interaction:** Porphyry places Hermes, Hecate, Hermopan, and Hermanubis within a common allegorical analysis of composite cosmic reason.
- **Source/date:** Porphyry, *On Cult Images*, late third century, preserved by Eusebius.
- **Tier:** Learned Neoplatonic systematization.
- **Qualification:** No personal encounter is narrated.

#### Hermopan

- **Interaction:** Porphyry juxtaposes Hermopan, the reason in the All, with composite Hermanubis.
- **Source/date:** Same passage.
- **Tier:** Learned systematization.
- **Qualification:** Conceptual relationship only.

#### Eros

- **Interaction:** Porphyry proceeds from Hermes as speech and its connection with love to Eros as Hermes’s son.
- **Source/date:** Same passage.
- **Tier:** Learned systematization.
- **Qualification:** Eros is not securely called Hermanubis’s son.

#### Typhon/Set

- **Interaction:** No securely attested ancient Hermanubis–Typhon scene has been identified. Their placement on opposite sides of the Wheel of Fortune originates in nineteenth-century occult Tarot.
- **Source/date:** Éliphas Lévi and later Golden Dawn/Waite traditions.
- **Tier:** Modern esoteric invention.

#### Sphinx

- **Interaction:** Hermanubis ascends beneath a sword-bearing sphinx on modern occult Wheel of Fortune designs.
- **Source/date:** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century Tarot.
- **Tier:** Modern esoteric invention.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 No independent Hermanubis myth cycle survives

The crucial negative conclusion is that there is no extant ancient “Myth of Hermanubis.” No papyrus tells his birth, exploits, descent, combat, love affair, judgment, death, or resurrection under that name.

Modern summaries frequently manufacture a biography by combining:

- Hermes’s childhood and psychopomp myths;
- Anubis’s embalming of Osiris and participation in judgment;
- Plutarch’s philosophical upper/lower distinction;
- Alexandrian iconography;
- modern occult Tarot symbolism.

Such a composite is useful only if each component remains historically labeled. Presented as one ancient story, it is modern invention.

### 5.2 The Anubis–Osiris funerary complex

**Earlier Egyptian core:** Anubis is a protector and embalmer of the dead, guardian of necropoleis, and participant in mortuary transition. In later Egyptian traditions he prepares or protects Osiris’s body and participates in judgment scenes.

**Greco-Roman transformation:** Greek observers recognized functional resemblance to Hermes Psychopompos. Jackal-headed Anubis could acquire a caduceus and winged sandals. The anthropomorphic Alexandrian figure then concentrated this equation into a new visual personality.

**Hermanubis-specific evidence:** The compound name, Plutarch’s lower-world designation, funerary visual associations, and joint appearance with Osiris/Sarapis. There is still no extant text saying, “Hermanubis embalmed Osiris.” That sentence is a modern transfer from Anubis tradition.

### 5.3 The upper and lower *logos*

Plutarch supplies the nearest thing to an explicitly Hermanubian theological myth:

- a revelatory *logos* discloses heavenly things;
- it is called Anubis in relation to things above;
- it is sometimes called Hermanubis in relation to things below;
- white and saffron cocks correspond to pure/manifest and mixed/variegated qualities.

This is not a narrative but an allegorical cosmology. It may preserve cult distinctions, Plutarch’s philosophical interpretation, or both. Its exact Egyptian antecedent cannot be reconstructed.

### 5.4 Porphyry’s composite cosmic reason

Porphyry develops a still later learned system:

- Hermes represents reason and speech;
- reason has solar, lunar, and universal expressions;
- Hermanubis is composite and “half-Greek.”

This is a third-century philosophical reclassification. It explains images and names through cosmic principles. It is neither an early Egyptian myth nor necessarily the doctrine of ordinary Alexandrian worshippers.

### 5.5 The modern Wheel of Fortune myth

Éliphas Lévi’s nineteenth-century occult reconstruction placed an ascending Hermanubis on one side of the Tarot Wheel, a descending Typhon on the other, and a sphinx above. Golden Dawn and Rider–Waite–Smith traditions repeated and elaborated it.

Later occultists interpreted Hermanubis as:

- evolution or ascent of consciousness;
- intelligence rising from animality;
- the constructive phase of cyclic motion;
- Mercury, sulfur, or active energy;
- the balancing opposite of Typhon/Set.

None of this is an ancient Hermanubis myth. It is a documented modern symbolic tradition built from Egyptomania, Hermeticism, and occult Tarot.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### 6.1 Alexandria

Alexandria is the most probable center of the specifically anthropomorphic Hermanubis form.

Evidence includes:

- SB I 3482 from the Serapeum area;
- a small anthropomorphic statuette reportedly found near the dedication;
- the Ras el-Soda statue and Isiac sanctuary ensemble;
- Alexandrian coinage;
- several Alexandrian bearers of Hermanubis-derived personal names;
- coin images apparently showing the god inside a temple.

A temple reverse and an inscription from the Serapeum precinct do not prove that Hermanubis possessed a monumental independent temple inside the Serapeum. The possibilities include a chapel, shrine, subsidiary sacred enclosure, shared temple, or conventional coin architecture.

### 6.2 Ras el-Soda sanctuary

Ras el-Soda, ancient Taposiris Parva in the eastern Alexandrian region, was discovered in 1936 and excavated under Achille Adriani. Its Roman-period assemblage included statues identified as Isis, Harpocrates, Hermanubis, and Osiris-Canopus. The Hermanubis statue is dated to the early second century CE.

This is firm archaeological evidence for the figure within a private or local healing/devotional sanctuary of the Isiac sphere. It does not yield a specific Hermanubis liturgy, priest list, or annual festival.

### 6.3 Cynopolite nome

Cynopolis, “Dog City,” was a principal traditional center of Anubis. The specifically Hermanubian evidence is primarily onomastic:

- three persons of Cynopolite origin bore names based on Hermanubis;
- one was father of an underage priest of Anubis, Leto, and associated gods in 282 CE;
- Alexandrian nome coinage may personify the Cynopolite nome through the anthropomorphic Hermanubis type.

This suggests continuity between Hermanubis and traditional Anubis worship. It argues against treating the Alexandrian form as completely detached from Egyptian Anubis.

### 6.4 Delos

The restored ID 2156 would locate Hermanubis in the Delian Egyptian-cult environment in the second century BCE and call him *nikēphoros*. Because the reading is reconstructed, Delos must remain a probable or possible early cult site, not an unquestionable birthplace.

### 6.5 Thessalonike

The third-century association of *thrēskeutai* and *sekobatai* is the clearest evidence for organized worship outside Egypt. It had presidents or patrons and an *archinakōros*. The honorific monument indicates institutional continuity, social standing, and internal hierarchy.

A *sekos* or sanctuary enclosure is likely, but neither its exact location nor architectural scale is securely known. The association belongs to the wider network of Egyptian or Isiac cult organizations in Roman Macedonia.

### 6.6 Priesthood and offices

No autonomous “high priest of Hermanubis” is securely documented in the evidence surveyed. The Thessalonian *archinakōros* and association officials are the best evidence for dedicated offices. The Cynopolite priestly family concerns Anubis, Leto, and associated gods and demonstrates relational continuity, not necessarily a Hermanubis priesthood.

### 6.7 Offerings and sacrifice

Secure or reported practices are:

- **Cock sacrifice:** Plutarch reports white and saffron-colored cocks corresponding to upper and lower/mixed aspects.
- **Votive dedication:** SB I 3482 demonstrates a family dedication for a son.
- **Honorific association monument:** Thessalonike.
- **Possible oracular dedication:** The gem with Sarapis, attributed Hermanubis, and Aphrodite bears “according to an oracle.”
- **Protective magic:** Gems combining Anubian/Hermetic figures with Osiris and apotropaic inscriptions.

No evidence establishes human sacrifice, temple prostitution, sacred marriage, self-castration, or systematic animal sacrifice peculiar to Hermanubis. Claims assigning such practices to him are unsupported.

### 6.8 Festivals and calendars

No securely named annual festival exclusive to Hermanubis survives. Anubis participated in Isiac processions, most famously in Apuleius’s literary representation, but calling every Anubis procession a “Festival of Hermanubis” is unjustified.

### 6.9 Political and economic dimensions

Alexandrian coins inserted the figure into an imperial civic medium. Under Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, and later rulers, local Egyptian divine types helped articulate Alexandria’s identity within the Roman Empire. This is political representation, but there is no evidence that Hermanubis served as an official patron of Roman conquest.

The Thessalonian association’s honorific behavior shows ordinary institutional economics—membership, office, patronage, and monument funding—but the surviving inscription gives no treasury accounts, property register, or privilege charter.

## 7. Historical evolution of the cult and figure

### Phase I: Egyptian precedents before the Ptolemies

Anubis’s mortuary role and association with guiding the dead long predate Hermanubis. Hermes’s Greek psychopompic role also predates the compound. Pre-Ptolemaic Egyptian images linking Thoth and Anubis have sometimes been proposed as antecedents, but Benaissa notes that the claim that they depict a true Thoth-Anubis fusion is disputed.

**Status:** Background conditions, not Hermanubis attestations.

### Phase II: Ptolemaic contact and possible formation, third–first centuries BCE

Greek rule and the development of Alexandrian and Delian Egyptian cult networks created the setting for Hermes–Anubis assimilation. The restored second-century BCE Delian dedication may attest the compound name.

**Status:** Historically plausible emergence; direct evidence uncertain.

### Phase III: Early Roman consolidation, first–early second centuries CE

Plutarch supplies the first secure explanatory occurrence. Roman artistic workshops produced jackal-headed Anubis in Greek dress, while Alexandria developed the fully anthropomorphic youth with caduceus, palm, calathos, and dog. The early-second-century Ras el-Soda statue is a principal monument.

**Status:** Secure emergence of the documented Roman figure.

### Phase IV: Antonine peak, second century CE

The greatest density of coins and sculptures belongs to the second century, especially Hadrian and Antoninus Pius. Temple coin types, association with Sarapis, and the expansion of Hermanubian personal names suggest peak visibility.

“Peak” here means peak surviving representational evidence, not a measurable majority share of Egyptian worship.

### Phase V: Institutional spread and diversification, third century CE

The Thessalonike association demonstrates organized extramural worship. Papyri attest officials and priestly families bearing Hermanubis-derived names. Porphyry absorbs the god into Neoplatonic image theology. Magical gems use related Anubian-Hermetic imagery.

**Status:** Secure expansion in social and intellectual settings.

### Phase VI: Christian polemic and disappearance, late fourth century onward

Gregory of Nazianzus uses Hermanubis as an Egyptian name and pagan insult around or after 380. In 391/392, the Alexandrian Serapeum was destroyed amid imperial anti-sacrifice legislation, conflict involving pagan and Christian groups, and action under Bishop Theophilus. Ancient accounts disagree sharply over provocation and violence. Archaeology confirms destruction and later Christian reuse of the site. [Judith McKenzie et al., reconstruction of the Serapeum](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-studies/article/abs/reconstructing-the-serapeum-in-alexandria-from-the-archaeological-evidence/2B41066E2FD232545126880FC6D0E5F3)

Because a Hermanubis dedication and statuette came from the Serapeum precinct, its destruction necessarily affected the setting in which his cult had existed. No source, however, says that Christians singled out Hermanubis, destroyed a named Hermanubis temple, or forcibly converted a discrete Hermanubian congregation.

The traditional cult then disappears from the record. A lexical Coptic survival of the name is not evidence of uninterrupted worship.

### Phase VII: Antiquarian rediscovery, Renaissance–eighteenth century

Renaissance mythographers knew Anubis and occasional references to Hermanubis through Plutarch, Porphyry/Eusebius, and classical lexicography. Lilio Gregorio Giraldi’s *Historiae deorum gentilium* (1548) belongs to this antiquarian recovery. Roman Egyptianizing sculptures were collected and restored, often before modern archaeological documentation. The Anzio statue now in the Vatican was excavated in 1749.

**Status:** Literary and collecting afterlife, not cult continuity.

### Phase VIII: Nineteenth-century occult reinvention

Éliphas Lévi transformed Hermanubis into a Tarot figure ascending the Wheel of Fortune opposite Typhon, beneath a sphinx. This was an imaginative esoteric synthesis, not a recovery of an Egyptian deck or ancient Hermanubian rite.

Golden Dawn teaching repeated the figure as “Anubis, the Egyptian Mercury,” often with a caduceus and cosmological interpretation. [Golden Dawn Tarot description](https://hermeticgoldendawn.org/the-tarot/)

### Phase IX: Twentieth century to the present

The Rider–Waite–Smith Wheel of Fortune, designed by Pamela Colman Smith under A. E. Waite’s direction and published in 1909–1910, gave the modern Hermanubis image enormous circulation. Waite describes “a Hermanubis ascending on the right,” with Typhon descending and a sphinx above. [Waite, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*](https://www.theology101.org/tarot/pkt/pkttp.htm)

Paul Foster Case and later occultists recast him as upward evolution of consciousness. Modern Kemetic, Hellenic, Hermetic, and eclectic practitioners sometimes address Hermanubis as a psychopomp or combined Hermes-Anubis. These practices are real modern religious phenomena, but no reliable census establishes their scale, and they are revivals or new constructions rather than demonstrable institutional continuity from antiquity.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### 8.1 Renaissance and early-modern mythography

Early-modern authors encountered Hermanubis through Plutarch and late-antique philosophical fragments. They tended to treat pagan gods as allegories, historical distortions, demons, or components of a universal ancient theology. No continuous priestly transmission underlies these accounts.

### 8.2 Éliphas Lévi

Lévi’s mid-nineteenth-century occult writings reconstructed several Tarot trumps through Kabbalistic, alchemical, Christian, and Egyptianizing symbolism. On the Wheel, Hermanubis became the ascending power while Typhon descended. This arrangement materially shaped all later occult reception.

It is a modern invention in two senses:

1. historical Tarot imagery did not originally contain a named Hermanubis;
2. no ancient source pairs Hermanubis and Typhon on a cosmic wheel.

### 8.3 Golden Dawn

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn incorporated the Lévi-derived image into its Tarot teaching. Its description calls the ascending dog-headed figure “Anubis, the Egyptian Mercury.” Later Golden Dawn-derived texts sometimes use Hermanubis explicitly.

The order’s correspondences among Tarot, Hebrew letters, planets, alchemy, and Egyptian gods are nineteenth-century occult systems, not ancient Alexandrian doctrine.

### 8.4 Rider–Waite–Smith Tarot

The Rider–Waite–Smith Wheel of Fortune shows:

- a yellow serpent descending;
- a reddish jackal-headed figure rising;
- a blue sphinx atop the wheel;
- four winged creatures in the corners;
- Hebrew and Latin letters and alchemical signs.

Waite explicitly identified the rising figure as Hermanubis. The card is the most influential modern image attached to the name, even though it resembles a jackal-headed Anubis more than the fully anthropomorphic Alexandrian type.

Modern claims that the red figure is “the ancient Egyptian god of evolution,” that he represents DNA, kundalini, a specific alchemical element, or a fixed stage of Jungian individuation are later interpretations, not ancient evidence.

### 8.5 Paul Foster Case and occult psychology

Case’s twentieth-century Tarot instruction described Hermanubis as representing evolution from lower to higher forms. This language belongs to modern evolutionary and psychological spirituality. It should not be attributed to Plutarch, Egyptian funerary texts, or Alexandrian worshippers.

### 8.6 Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Contemporary individuals identify Hermanubis as:

- a psychopomp;
- patron of spirit communication;
- guide in grief and funerary work;
- mediator between Hellenic and Kemetic practice;
- fusion or “syncretic face” of Hermes and Anubis.

No centralized church, stable liturgy, or statistically measured movement is documented. Invocations, altar practices, planetary correspondences, feast days, sigils, and offerings circulated online are normally personal or community innovations. They should be dated to their modern authors unless an ancient source is expressly supplied.

### 8.7 Popular culture and commerce

Hermanubis appears sporadically in games, fantasy art, jewelry, occult supplies, Tarot products, and internet mythology databases. Commercial imagery usually favors a muscular jackal-headed figure with caduceus, combining modern fantasy Anubis with recognizable Hermetic symbolism. This differs from the ancient anthropomorphic Alexandrian youth.

No substantial modern nationalist or extremist appropriation specific to Hermanubis is established in the sources consulted. General Egyptian, Hermetic, solar, or occult imagery has been appropriated by many political movements, but that does not justify attributing those uses to Hermanubis without direct evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### 9.1 The central identification problem

Older art history often applied “Hermanubis” broadly to any Anubis bearing Hermetic attributes and to the human youth with a dog. Jean Leclant and later scholars warned that none of the anthropomorphic images carries an identifying inscription.

Benaissa’s 2009 study supplies the strongest circumstantial defense of the conventional identification:

- divine and personal names cluster in the Roman period;
- the images likewise cluster in the second and third centuries;
- both concentrate at Alexandria and, secondarily, Cynopolis;
- the literary sources describe an actual composite deity.

This makes “anthropomorphic Hermanubis” a well-supported scholarly designation, but still not an epigraphically proven label for each object.

### 9.2 Anubis with Hermes attributes versus a new god

One position emphasizes continuity: Hermanubis is Anubis interpreted through Hermes, and Plutarch explicitly calls the word a designation of Anubis.

Another emphasizes innovation: the compound name, human-headed Alexandrian iconography, distinct cult association, and derived personal names indicate a sufficiently individualized god.

The evidence supports both continuity and innovation. It does not support a sharp universal boundary.

### 9.3 Alexandria or Delos as point of emergence

If ID 2156 is correctly restored, Delos supplies the earliest name. Alexandria nevertheless provides the strongest concentration of later cult, sculpture, coins, and names. “Birthplace” is therefore a hypothesis:

- Delos: possible earliest written name;
- Alexandria: probable center in which the fully anthropomorphic type crystallized;
- Cynopolis: traditional Anubian continuity.

### 9.4 Grenier’s differentiation

Jean-Claude Grenier distinguished traditional funerary Anubis in the Isiac cult from a more anthropomorphic and universalizing Hermanubis. Benaissa cautions against separating them too sharply, citing the Cynopolite names and the father of an Anubis priest.

### 9.5 Kákosy and a Thoth-Anubis antecedent

László Kákosy proposed that pre-Ptolemaic statuettes with jackal-headed features on Thoth’s feet or body reflected an earlier Thoth-Anubis syncretism, helping explain Hermanubis. Benaissa regards the proposed identification and relevance as unproven. Formal hybridity does not by itself disclose a divine name.

### 9.6 Textual transmission problems

- The Delian name is restored.
- Plutarch’s accentuation is editorial.
- Porphyry survives only through a hostile Christian excerptor, Eusebius.
- *Greek Anthology* XI.360 has an anomalous form, Ἑρμανούβης, and obscure syntax.
- Gregory’s Hermanubis may be a personal name, caricature, or both.
- Coin and statue names are normally supplied by modern cataloguers.

### 9.7 Archaeological discoveries

- Excavation of the Alexandrian Serapeum area produced SB I 3482 and a nearby statuette.
- The Ras el-Soda sanctuary, discovered in 1936, established a strong Alexandrian archaeological context for the anthropomorphic type.
- Systematic Roman Provincial Coinage cataloguing has clarified the chronology and variety of Alexandrian issues.
- Papyrological publications, especially the Oxyrhynchus corpus, revealed Hermanubis-derived names across administrative and priestly environments.

### 9.8 Material now held in collections

Important or representative material includes:

- Ras el-Soda Hermanubis statue, Graeco-Roman Museum, Alexandria, inv. 25785;
- Vatican Anubis in Greek/Roman dress, Museo Gregoriano Egizio, inv. 22840;
- Alexandrian coins dispersed through public and private collections and catalogued by RPC;
- SB I 3482, historically recorded from the Alexandrian Serapeum area;
- magical gems in museum collections and digital catalogues.

### 9.9 Persistent popular errors

The following claims are unsupported or misleading:

- “Hermanubis was an ancient Egyptian god worshipped from pharaonic times.”  
  The compound is Greco-Egyptian and securely documented only in the Roman period, with one disputed Hellenistic restoration.

- “Hermanubis was the son of Osiris and Nephthys.”  
  That is a genealogy transferred from some Anubis traditions.

- “He was the Egyptian equivalent of Hermes Trismegistus.”  
  Hermanubis, Hermes-Thoth, and Hermes Trismegistus overlap conceptually but are not interchangeable identities.

- “All jackal-headed caduceus bearers are Hermanubis.”  
  Ancient sources often call such a figure simply Anubis.

- “The Vatican statue is certainly Hermanubis.”  
  The Vatican catalogue calls it Anubis; “Hermanubis” is a modern syncretic classification.

- “The Tarot Wheel preserves an ancient Egyptian teaching.”  
  Its named Hermanubis–Typhon arrangement is a nineteenth-century occult reconstruction.

- “Hermanubis had a fixed ancient sigil, planet, weekday, number, color, and incense.”  
  No such coherent ancient correspondence table survives.
