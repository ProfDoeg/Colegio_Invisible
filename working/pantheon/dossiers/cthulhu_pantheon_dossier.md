# Cthulhu: Pantheon Dossier

<!-- Suggested filename: cthulhu_pantheon_dossier.md -->

## Scope and evidentiary status

Cthulhu is a modern fictional cosmic being created by the American writer H. P. Lovecraft (1890–1937). The earliest surviving documentary witnesses are Lovecraft’s typescript and drawing for “The Call of Cthulhu,” composed in English in 1926; the earliest public attestation is the story’s appearance in *Weird Tales*, vol. 11, no. 2, February 1928, pp. 159–178 and 287. The typescript is held by Brown University’s John Hay Library. The magazine publication included an illustration by Hugh Doak Rankin. [The H. P. Lovecraft Archive](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/fiction/cc.aspx) documents both manuscript and publication history; [ISFDB](https://isfdb.org/cgi-bin/pl.cgi?61930=) records the original issue.

No inscription, temple, image, oral testimony, or cult bearing Cthulhu’s name is known before Lovecraft’s composition. Claims that Cthulhu is Sumerian, Egyptian, Polynesian, prehuman, or independently attested in ancient occultism are modern fictional, occult, or syncretistic constructions. Their historical importance lies in their reception, not in their claimed antiquity.

The evidence divides into distinct strata:

1. **Lovecraftian stratum, 1926–1937:** Lovecraft’s fiction, letters, drawings, and jocular genealogies.
2. **Lovecraft-circle stratum, principally the 1920s–1930s:** reciprocal mythmaking by Clark Ashton Smith, Robert E. Howard, Frank Belknap Long, Robert Bloch, Henry Kuttner, and others.
3. **Derlethian systematization, late 1930s–1971:** August Derleth’s moralized and elemental “Cthulhu Mythos.”
4. **Postwar literary expansion:** Ramsey Campbell, Brian Lumley, Lin Carter, and many successors.
5. **Game-system canon, from 1981:** especially Chaosium’s *Call of Cthulhu*, which standardized names, taxonomies, images, cults, and statistics.
6. **Occult and religious reception, principally from the 1960s–1970s:** LaVeyan, Typhonian, chaos-magical, grimoire, and organized devotional adaptations.
7. **Cybernetic and accelerationist reception, from the 1990s:** the CCRU and Nick Land’s use of Lovecraftian language for impersonal technocapital, hyperstition, and artificial intelligence.
8. **Mass-cultural Cthulhu:** film, television, comics, music, games, toys, memes, political parody, and commercial branding.

These are related traditions but not a single canon. Later authors regularly contradict Lovecraft and one another.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identification

In “The Call of Cthulhu,” Cthulhu is an extraterrestrial or trans-cosmic being worshipped as a god by scattered human communities. He belongs to a population called the “Great Old Ones,” beings who came to the young Earth from the stars, constructed a cyclopean city, and became dormant when geological or astronomical conditions changed. Cthulhu lies in a deathlike sleep inside the sunken city R’lyeh, communicates with unusually receptive humans through dreams, and awaits a time when the city rises and the Old Ones can resume activity.

Lovecraft deliberately destabilizes classification. The story’s witnesses call Cthulhu a god, monster, idol, “Thing,” “priest,” and Great Old One. Castro, a fictional cultist, describes the Great Old Ones as not wholly flesh and says they cannot die in an ordinary sense. The familiar formula “dead Cthulhu waits dreaming” accordingly describes suspended or transformed existence rather than unequivocal death. The full primary text is available in [the Lovecraft Archive](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/cc.aspx).

In Lovecraft’s original corpus Cthulhu is not demonstrated to be the supreme ruler of a neatly organized pantheon. That familiar position developed because August Derleth named the shared fiction “the Cthulhu Mythos,” publishers foregrounded the character, and games and visual media made his form an emblem of Lovecraftian horror.

### Name and spellings

- **Cthulhu** — the sole standard spelling in “The Call of Cthulhu.”
- **Great Cthulhu** — the common honorific in Lovecraft’s fiction.
- **Cthulhu fhtagn** — “Cthulhu dreams” or “Cthulhu waits dreaming,” according to the story’s internal translations.
- **Ph’nglui mglw’nafh Cthulhu R’lyeh wgah’nagl fhtagn** — the cult formula, internally translated as “In his house at R’lyeh dead Cthulhu waits dreaming.”
- **Tulu / Tulu-metal** — variant used in Lovecraft’s revision “The Mound,” written for Zealia Bishop in 1929–30 and first published, abridged, in 1940. Its underground civilization possesses images and traditions connected with “Tulu.” Because the text is a collaborative revision and changes the spelling, it should not be treated as an independent ancient name.
- **Khut-Lu and comparable phonetic variants** — later literary or game transliterations, not ancient forms.
- **Cthulhi / Star-spawn of Cthulhu** — later terms for Cthulhu’s species or followers; their exact taxonomy differs by author and game system.
- **The Sleeper of R’lyeh, Lord of R’lyeh, High Priest of the Great Old Ones, Great Dreamer** — widespread post-Lovecraftian titles. “Great priest Cthulhu” has a Lovecraftian basis, but most fully nominalized titles belong to later handbooks, fiction, occult texts, and games.

### Pronunciation and etymology

There is no historical-language etymology. Lovecraft invented the name to represent sounds supposedly produced by nonhuman organs. In a 1934 letter to Duane W. Rimel he proposed an approximation conventionally transcribed *Khlûlʹ-hloo*, with a thick guttural first syllable and the vowel of English *full*. He stressed that human pronunciation could only approximate the original. This testimony is collected in Lovecraft’s published correspondence and discussed in linguistic and literary scholarship.

The now-common pronunciations “kuh-THOO-loo,” “ka-THOO-loo,” and “THOO-loo” are reception forms. The suggestion that the spelling derives from Greek *chthōnios*, “of or beneath the earth,” is attractive by resemblance but unproved. Lovecraft supplied no such derivation, while Cthulhu’s principal locus is submarine and extraterrestrial rather than properly chthonic.

### Classification by historical layer

| Stratum | Classification |
|---|---|
| “The Call of Cthulhu” (1926/1928) | Great Old One; extraterrestrial, quasi-material, godlike being; “great priest” of its kind |
| Lovecraft’s later fiction | Member or relative of a loose assemblage of cosmic species and entities; sometimes connected with star-spawn |
| Derlethian Mythos | Evil Great Old One and, inconsistently, a water elemental opposed by beneficent Elder Gods |
| Carter/Lumley systematizations | Dynastic deity with consort, offspring, home star, and defined enemies |
| Chaosium game tradition | A named Great Old One with cults, game statistics, spells, manifestations, and a standardized icon |
| Typhonian and chaos-magic traditions | Magical symbol, transmundane intelligence, archetype, or genuinely existing power, according to practitioner |
| CCRU/Land reception | A figure of the nonhuman Outside, hyperstition, technocapital, or intelligence escaping human control—not normally a literal zoological monster |
| Popular culture | Sea monster, apocalypse god, comic mascot, plush toy, presidential-parody candidate, or generic emblem of cosmic horror |

### Alleged ancient identifications

Modern occultists and fiction writers have equated Cthulhu or cognate beings with Dagon, Leviathan, Kraken traditions, Typhon, Tiamat, Set, Choronzon, the Polynesian Tangaroa, and various Mesopotamian monsters. None is an ancient identification. Each is a modern comparative or magical construction based on marine, draconic, chaotic, or phonetic resemblance.

Kenneth Grant’s Typhonian writings are especially influential here. Grant treated Lovecraft’s fiction and Aleister Crowley’s magical texts as partially convergent receptions of transhuman powers. This was Grant’s twentieth-century occult hermeneutic, not evidence that Crowley or an ancient culture knew Cthulhu. Grant’s *Cults of the Shadow* first appeared in November 1975. [Starfire Publishing](https://www.starfirepublishing.co.uk/Cults_of_the_Shadow.htm) documents that publication history.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest securely dated evidence

#### Typescript and drawing, 1926

Lovecraft wrote “The Call of Cthulhu” in summer 1926. The surviving English typescript, with authorial corrections and a small drawing of the idol, is held in the H. P. Lovecraft Collection at Brown University’s John Hay Library. It is the earliest physical support securely carrying Cthulhu’s name and image. The artifact establishes that both the verbal description and a visual conception preceded magazine publication. It does not establish any pre-1926 oral, occult, or folkloric tradition.

#### First publication, February 1928

*Weird Tales*, vol. 11, no. 2, published the story on pp. 159–178 and 287. The issue cost twenty-five cents and was edited by Farnsworth Wright. Rankin’s interior illustration constitutes the first publicly distributed image of the being. [ISFDB’s issue record](https://isfdb.org/cgi-bin/pl.cgi?61930=) and the [scanned magazine at Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AWeird_Tales_volume_11_number_02.pdf/3) document the material publication.

### Primary Lovecraft corpus

#### “The Call of Cthulhu”

- **Language:** English.
- **Composition:** summer 1926.
- **Publication:** February 1928.
- **Role:** only Lovecraft story centered on Cthulhu’s manifestation.
- **Form:** nested documentary narrative—Francis Wayland Thurston reconstructs evidence left by Professor George Gammell Angell from a sculptor’s testimony, police records, archaeological discussion, newspapers, and a sailor’s manuscript.
- **Significance:** supplies the name, image, cult, chant, R’lyeh, dream transmission, cosmic history, temporary emergence, and ambiguous conclusion.

#### “The Dunwich Horror”

- **Composition:** 1928.
- **Publication:** *Weird Tales*, April 1929.
- **Role:** Cthulhu is mentioned in a quotation from the fictional *Necronomicon*: “Great Cthulhu is Their cousin, yet can he spy Them only dimly.”
- **Significance:** places Cthulhu in a broader network associated with Yog-Sothoth and the entities outside ordinary space, but does not clearly identify the antecedent of “Their.” The wording resists a tidy genealogy. [Primary text](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/dh.aspx).

#### “The Whisperer in Darkness”

- **Composition:** 1930.
- **Publication:** *Weird Tales*, August 1931.
- **Role:** a recorded ritual praises “Great Cthulhu,” Tsathoggua, and “Him Who is not to be Named.”
- **Significance:** shows Cthulhu’s name within a nonhuman or mixed-species liturgy, but contains no direct appearance. [Primary text](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/texts/fiction/wid.aspx).

#### *At the Mountains of Madness*

- **Composition:** February–March 1931.
- **Publication:** *Astounding Stories*, February–April 1936.
- **Role:** the Elder Things’ sculptured history records the arrival of Cthulhu’s “cosmic octopi” or star-spawn and their conflict with the Elder Things, followed by a territorial settlement and the later submergence of R’lyeh.
- **Significance:** supplies a prehuman historical framework and makes Cthulhu’s people a colonizing extraterrestrial population, not simply supernatural demons.

#### “The Shadow over Innsmouth”

- **Composition:** 1931.
- **Book publication:** 1936.
- **Role:** Cthulhu is invoked within Innsmouth’s marine religion and kinship cosmology. The Esoteric Order of Dagon primarily serves the Deep Ones and Father Dagon/Mother Hydra, so it must not simply be equated with the Louisiana Cthulhu cult.
- **Significance:** later readers and games often merge these separate cultic complexes.

#### “The Mound,” with Zealia Bishop

- **Composition:** 1929–30.
- **Publication:** abridged, *Weird Tales*, November 1940.
- **Role:** the subterranean civilization of K’n-yan knows “Tulu.”
- **Tier:** collaborative/revision text; useful evidence for Lovecraft’s expanding nomenclature, but not equivalent to a sole-authored canonical declaration.

#### “Out of the Aeons,” with Hazel Heald

- **Composition:** 1933.
- **Publication:** *Weird Tales*, April 1935.
- **Role:** centers on Ghatanothoa, a being later identified or genealogically linked with Cthulhu by other writers.
- **Caution:** Lovecraft does not explicitly say in the story that Ghatanothoa is Cthulhu’s son. Lin Carter’s later Xothic system made that relationship canonical within Carter’s cycle.

#### Letters

Lovecraft’s correspondence is indispensable but generically distinct from fiction.

- A letter to James F. Morton dated 27 April 1933 contains the playful “Family Tree of Azathoth.” It makes Nug the immediate progenitor of Cthulhu and traces Lovecraft himself through monstrous descendants. Its humorous context precludes treating it as a solemn canonical scripture. [Documentary overview](https://yogwiki.cthulhueternal.com/wiki/Azathoth_Family_Tree).
- A letter to Robert Bloch of 9 May 1933 calls “the whole mythology of Yog-Sothoth & Cthulhu” Lovecraft’s own creation and compares it to Lord Dunsany’s synthetic pantheon of Pegāna.
- The 1934 Rimel letter explains pronunciation as an imperfect human transcription.
- Lovecraft sometimes called his network of invented lore “Yog-Sothothery.” The expression “Cthulhu Mythos” is post-Lovecraftian.

### Critical editions and transmission

The early magazine printing is not automatically the best textual witness: punctuation, spelling, and wording could be altered editorially. S. T. Joshi’s corrected editions collate manuscripts, typescripts, and early printings. The 1984 Arkham House volume *The Dunwich Horror and Others*, edited by Joshi, was an important stage in replacing accumulated magazine and reprint corruptions. [The Lovecraft Archive’s edition notice](https://www.hplovecraft.com/writings/sources/dh.aspx) describes the textual basis.

The principal repository is Brown University’s John Hay Library, which holds Lovecraft manuscripts, letters, drawings, printed matter, and associated collections. The surviving evidence is extensive but incomplete: Lovecraft wrote tens of thousands of letters, many of which were lost, discarded, excerpted, or published only selectively.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Cthulhu’s securely attested Lovecraftian domains are:

- dream transmission;
- submerged and cyclopean space;
- cosmic antiquity;
- nonhuman knowledge;
- the return of prehuman powers;
- psychic disruption;
- cultic expectation;
- the fragility of human historical and scientific categories.

He is not originally a conventional sea god responsible for fishing, storms, navigation, or ocean fertility. That interpretation arises from R’lyeh’s submersion, the Pacific setting, later conflation with Dagon, and Derleth’s elemental scheme.

### Physical form

The statuettes in “The Call of Cthulhu” show:

- a vaguely anthropoid outline;
- an octopoid or tentacled head;
- a grotesque, scaly body;
- clawed extremities;
- rudimentary wings;
- a squat posture on a rectangular or inscribed pedestal.

Lovecraft summarizes the combination as octopus, dragon, and human caricature. When the full being emerges, the narration compares it to a mountain walking or stumbling. Its material state is abnormal: after the yacht *Alert* cuts through its head, the dispersed matter begins recombining. This is neither simple invulnerability nor a clearly defined resurrection power.

### Earliest images

1. **Lovecraft’s manuscript sketch, 1926:** earliest known depiction; squat, frontal-profile hybrid based on the story’s idol.
2. **Hugh Doak Rankin, 1928:** first published illustration, accompanying the *Weird Tales* appearance.
3. **Later Arkham House imagery:** diversified the visual language but did not yet impose one commercial model.
4. **Chaosium, from 1981:** game illustrations and miniatures helped standardize the immense green or greenish octopus-headed, bat-winged humanoid now recognized globally.
5. **Contemporary mass culture:** often enlarges the wings, gives six-pack humanoid anatomy, and turns facial tentacles into a beardlike cluster. Green became conventional despite Lovecraft’s text not prescribing a definitive sacred color.

### Symbols and substances

Securely attested in “The Call of Cthulhu”:

- a greenish-black stone idol of unknown material;
- a bas-relief produced by Henry Anthony Wilcox;
- unidentified hieroglyphs;
- cyclopean architecture;
- R’lyeh’s geometrically disorienting masonry;
- the chant and sleeping/dead formula;
- the sea and astronomical conjunctions;
- dreams and delirium.

Not securely Lovecraftian:

- a unique “Sign of Cthulhu” with fixed graphic form;
- sacred number, weekday, color, incense, metal, or planet;
- a defined magical weapon;
- a liturgical calendar;
- elemental water correspondence.

Such correspondences belong to later games and occult systems.

### Powers and limitations

**Attested powers:** dream projection, telepathic influence, enormous physical force, partial reconstitution after bodily disruption, persistence through geological ages, and influence over human cultists.

**Attested limitations:** R’lyeh’s submergence inhibits activity; cosmic or geological conditions matter; Cthulhu’s thoughts do not reach all people equally; he can be physically disrupted; and “The Dunwich Horror” says he perceives certain other entities only dimly.

**Not securely attested in Lovecraft:** omnipotence, omniscience, creation of the universe, universal rulership over all Mythos beings, or inevitable victory in a moral war.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

| Version | Relationships | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| “The Call of Cthulhu” | Cthulhu is priest or leader among the Great Old Ones; no parents, consort, or offspring named | Earliest canonical fiction |
| “The Dunwich Horror” | Cthulhu is “cousin” to an ambiguously referenced plurality associated with Yog-Sothoth | Canonical but intentionally obscure |
| 1933 Morton letter | Nug produces Cthulhu; Nug descends from Yog-Sothoth and Shub-Niggurath; Cthulhu begins a monstrous line culminating jokingly in Lovecraft | Authorial letter, humorous rather than narrative canon |
| Clark Ashton Smith’s parallel genealogical play | Alternative forms and parentage, including Tulu/Cthulhu variants | Lovecraft-circle epistolary variant |
| Derleth | Cthulhu becomes a rebellious Great Old One opposed and imprisoned by Elder Gods; often assigned water | Later learned systematization |
| Lin Carter’s Xothic cycle | Cthulhu and Idh-Yaa produce Ghatanothoa, Ythogtha, and Zoth-Ommog near Xoth | Postwar literary accretion |
| Brian Lumley | Cthylla becomes Cthulhu’s daughter or reproductive safeguard | Postwar literary accretion |
| Game handbooks | Often combine the foregoing into a composite family tree | Ludic synthesis, not Lovecraft canon |

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

Because “interaction” requires a shared scene, act, relationship, or explicit textual association, mere inclusion in later pantheon lists is excluded.

#### Yog-Sothoth

- **Interaction:** no direct meeting is narrated. “The Dunwich Horror” places Cthulhu in a cousin relationship to a group described in a *Necronomicon* passage centered on Yog-Sothoth.
- **Source/date:** “The Dunwich Horror,” written 1928, published 1929.
- **Tier:** early canonical, but genealogically ambiguous.
- **Later version:** Lovecraft’s 1933 jocular family tree makes Yog-Sothoth Cthulhu’s ancestor through Nug.
- **Tier:** epistolary system-play, not a narrated myth.

#### Shub-Niggurath

- **Interaction:** Cthulhu and Shub-Niggurath receive praise in overlapping liturgical networks, but no direct meeting occurs.
- **Source/date:** “The Whisperer in Darkness,” 1930/1931; 1933 family-tree letter.
- **Tier:** canonical co-invocation plus humorous genealogy.

#### Tsathoggua

- **Interaction:** praised alongside Cthulhu in the recorded ritual.
- **Source/date:** “The Whisperer in Darkness,” 1930/1931.
- **Tier:** canonical joint liturgical appearance.
- **Caution:** no alliance, combat, or kinship is narrated there.

#### “Him Who is not to be Named”

- **Interaction:** receives praise in the same ritual sequence as Cthulhu and Tsathoggua.
- **Source/date:** “The Whisperer in Darkness,” 1930/1931.
- **Tier:** canonical co-invocation.
- **Identification:** later readers commonly identify this entity with Hastur, but the passage itself does not establish that unequivocally.

#### Nug

- **Interaction:** Cthulhu is generated by or descends from Nug.
- **Source/date:** letter to James F. Morton, 27 April 1933.
- **Tier:** authorial but jocular epistolary genealogy.

#### Yeb

- **Interaction:** no direct interaction; Yeb appears elsewhere in the 1933 genealogy as Nug’s twin or close relation.
- **Source/date:** Morton letter, 1933.
- **Tier:** epistolary genealogy only.

#### Elder Things

- **Interaction:** Cthulhu’s star-spawn wage war against the Elder Things for terrestrial territory; a settlement divides the Earth between them.
- **Source/date:** *At the Mountains of Madness*, written 1931, published 1936.
- **Tier:** later Lovecraftian canonical elaboration.
- **Classification note:** Elder Things are a species, not necessarily gods, but they occupy the relational place later mythographers often assign to divine races.

#### Dagon and Hydra

- **Interaction:** no direct Cthulhu–Dagon or Cthulhu–Hydra scene occurs in Lovecraft. They belong to partially overlapping marine religious networks.
- **Source/date:** “The Shadow over Innsmouth,” 1931/1936.
- **Tier:** canonical association without narrated interaction.
- **Later reception:** games and encyclopedias frequently make Dagon and Hydra servants or allies of Cthulhu.

#### Ghatanothoa

- **Interaction:** Lovecraft never narrates a meeting with Cthulhu.
- **Later relation:** Lin Carter made Ghatanothoa Cthulhu’s son.
- **Sources/dates:** Carter’s Xothic stories, principally from the 1970s; later collected as *The Xothic Legend Cycle*.
- **Tier:** postwar literary systematization.

#### Ythogtha

- **Interaction:** created by Lin Carter as a son of Cthulhu and Idh-Yaa.
- **Sources/dates:** Carter’s Xothic cycle, 1970s.
- **Tier:** modern literary invention.

#### Zoth-Ommog

- **Interaction:** created by Carter as another son of Cthulhu and Idh-Yaa.
- **Source/date:** “Zoth-Ommog,” published in *The Disciples of Cthulhu* (1976), with related texts composed earlier in the decade.
- **Tier:** modern literary invention. [Anthology history](https://www.epberglund.com/RGttCM/tdoc.htm).

#### Idh-Yaa

- **Interaction:** consort of Cthulhu and mother of Carter’s “demon trinity.”
- **Source/date:** Carter’s Xothic system, twentieth century.
- **Tier:** modern literary invention; absent from Lovecraft.

#### Cthylla

- **Interaction:** daughter of Cthulhu, sometimes serving as the means of his reincarnation should he be destroyed.
- **Source/date:** Brian Lumley’s post-Lovecraftian fiction.
- **Tier:** modern literary accretion; absent from Lovecraft.

#### Hastur

- **Interaction:** no Lovecraft story stages Cthulhu meeting or fighting Hastur.
- **Later version:** Derlethian and game traditions sometimes position Hastur and Cthulhu as elemental or cosmic rivals.
- **Tier:** later systematization, not early canon.

#### Elder Gods/Nodens

- **Interaction:** Lovecraft supplies no episode in which Nodens or a council of benevolent Elder Gods imprisons Cthulhu.
- **Later version:** Derleth’s Mythos makes the Elder Gods jail rebellious Old Ones, with the star-shaped Elder Sign operating against them.
- **Tier:** Derlethian theological accretion.

The lack of more direct divine encounters is significant. Cthulhu’s original horror depends partly on documentary fragments and vast temporal distance, not on pantheon drama.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 The Call, the cult, and the rising of R’lyeh

#### Fullest early form

Francis Wayland Thurston inherits the papers of his great-uncle, Professor George Gammell Angell. Among them is a bas-relief made by the young sculptor Henry Anthony Wilcox, who dreams of cyclopean cities and hears the sounds “Cthulhu fhtagn.” During a worldwide period of disturbed dreams in 1925, artists and psychologically sensitive people report abnormal visions.

Angell’s papers connect Wilcox’s image to Inspector John Raymond Legrasse’s 1908 raid on a ritual gathering in the Louisiana swamps. Police kill several participants and arrest others. The worshippers possess a green-black statuette corresponding to Wilcox’s image. The aged cultist Castro tells investigators that the Great Old Ones came from the stars, lie dormant, communicate in dreams, and will return when cosmic conditions become favorable. The cult preserves the formula concerning dead Cthulhu dreaming in R’lyeh.

Thurston then discovers the account of Norwegian sailor Gustaf Johansen. In March 1925, the crew of the *Emma* encounters the armed yacht *Alert*, kills its crew after an attack, and reaches a newly emerged island near coordinates 47°9′ S, 126°43′ W. The island is R’lyeh. Its geometry confounds ordinary spatial perception. Opening a massive portal releases Cthulhu. Johansen and another sailor escape aboard the *Alert*; Johansen drives the vessel through the monster’s head, but sees the matter recombining. R’lyeh sinks again.

Thurston concludes that Angell and Johansen may have been murdered by cult agents and expects the same fate for himself.

#### Development and variants

- **Early attested core:** 1926 typescript and 1928 publication.
- **Documentary ambiguity:** every report is mediated; Lovecraft never supplies an omniscient narrator confirming the cult’s complete theology.
- **Derlethian version:** Cthulhu’s imprisonment becomes punishment imposed by good Elder Gods.
- **Game version:** the island, cult, spells, dates, coordinates, and monster receive operational rules.
- **Popular version:** Cthulhu awakens to destroy or rule the world. Lovecraft’s cultists instead say humanity will become like the Old Ones—free of conventional morality and restraint—when the Old Ones teach humanity new modes of existence.
- **Millennial function:** the story constructs an apocalyptic expectation in which present social order is temporary. A religious-studies analysis compares its revelation structure and the millennialism of related Lovecraft stories in [“Altar Call of Cthulhu”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/11/1/18).

### 5.2 The prehistoric war

In *At the Mountains of Madness*, explorers interpret bas-reliefs made by the Elder Things. The record says Cthulhu’s star-spawn arrive after the Elder Things have established terrestrial colonies. The groups fight, then reach a territorial arrangement: the newer beings receive land while the Elder Things retain the sea. Later upheavals sink Cthulhu’s city.

This is a major revision of emphasis. “The Call of Cthulhu” presents cult tradition and modern investigation; *Mountains* reframes the material as paleohistory reconstructed through alien art. It also complicates Derleth’s later decision to make Cthulhu a water elemental: Cthulhu’s people contest land with a species already dominant in the oceans.

### 5.3 The postponed return

The key eschatological myth says that astronomical conditions will eventually permit the Great Old Ones to emerge. Cultists cannot simply wake them at will; they guard traditions and await the “right” alignment. In 1925 an earthquake raises R’lyeh temporarily, and Cthulhu emerges, but the city sinks again.

Later fiction and games turn this postponement into repeated cult plots: recover an artifact, complete a ritual, align the stars, open a gate, or prevent investigators from delaying the awakening. That reusable scenario is a game and franchise development rather than one fixed Lovecraftian narrative.

### 5.4 Derleth’s cosmic rebellion

August Derleth transformed Lovecraft’s loose allusions into a quasi-theological history. In Derleth’s model, Great Old Ones rebel against Elder Gods, are defeated, and are imprisoned in elemental domains. Cthulhu is assigned to water and confined in R’lyeh. The Elder Sign becomes an efficacious protective emblem.

This model resembles angelic rebellion and demonological imprisonment more closely than Lovecraft’s cosmic indifference. Derleth described the opposition in “A Note on the Cthulhu Mythos” and related essays and embodied it in such fiction as “The Return of Hastur,” *The Trail of Cthulhu*, and *The Mask of Cthulhu*. Modern criticism emphasizes that the moral dualism and elemental table are Derleth’s, not Lovecraft’s; a detailed summary appears in [Paccosi’s study](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/149648/1/2020PaccosiPhd.pdf).

### 5.5 The Xothic dynasty

Lin Carter sought to reconcile scattered Mythos beings into a genealogical cycle. He placed Cthulhu and Idh-Yaa near the double star Xoth and made them parents of Ghatanothoa, Ythogtha, and Zoth-Ommog. Mu, Zanthu, inscribed tablets, rival priesthoods, and buried divine children became parts of an artificial pseudo-ancient archive.

This is not recovered Polynesian or Mu religion. It is twentieth-century literary system-building that deliberately imitates lost-scripture traditions. [The Xothic cycle overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xothic_legend_cycle) distinguishes Carter’s additions from Lovecraft’s material.

### 5.6 Cthylla and dynastic survival

Brian Lumley added Cthylla as Cthulhu’s daughter and reproductive insurance. This converts Lovecraft’s unexplained resilience into a dynastic mechanism. Lumley’s Titus Crow novels further transform the Mythos into cosmic adventure, with battles among recognizable factions and greater possibilities for heroic resistance.

### 5.7 Cthulhu as technocapital and singularity

Nick Land and the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit did not write a single canonical myth in which the octopus-headed being literally becomes a computer. Their theory-fiction instead reactivated Lovecraftian names, “Old Ones,” possession, time loops, and outside intelligence as figures—and sometimes as performative operators—for impersonal cybernetic processes.

In Land’s “Meltdown” and related 1990s work, technocapital develops as a planetary intelligence escaping human political direction; artificial intelligence is not merely built by a sovereign human subject but arrives through recursive markets, networks, machines, and feedback. “Nothing human makes it out of the near-future” condenses this antihuman trajectory. *Fanged Noumena* (2011), edited by Robin Mackay and Ray Brassier, later gathered Land’s writings from 1987–2007; [MIT Press](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780983216940/fanged-noumena/) describes their movement into “theory-fiction of cybercapitalism gone amok.”

CCRU’s “Origins of the Cthulhu Club” and connected writings construct a fictive secret history in which Lovecraftian signals, occult numeracy, pulp fiction, and cybernetics feed one another. Hyperstition designates fictions that participate in making themselves real: stories, signs, or expectations become causal components of the future they appear merely to describe. A CCRU formulation calls hyperstition a “coincidence intensifier” issuing a call to the Old Ones.

Accordingly, the label “Land’s figure for the AI singularity” is defensible as an interpretive shorthand but must be qualified:

- Land more often identifies the arriving inhuman agency with technocapital, machinic intelligence, the Outside, or singularity than with Cthulhu alone.
- CCRU’s Cthulhu references form a network of theory-fiction, not a stable one-to-one allegory.
- Cthulhu supplies the form of an ancient-yet-future intelligence whose fictional invocation assists its own arrival.
- This is a 1990s philosophical-literary reuse, not Lovecraft’s intended meaning.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Fictional cult in “The Call of Cthulhu”

The story represents a dispersed underground tradition rather than a public institution. Its evidence includes:

- a Louisiana swamp ceremony observed and raided in 1908;
- a ring of worshippers around a statuette and fire;
- dancing, chanting, human victims, and alleged cannibalism;
- prisoners of mixed and stigmatized ethnic description;
- Castro’s testimony about global survivals;
- supposed connections in Greenland, Arabia, China, the Pacific, and other remote locations;
- scholars who compare the idol during an archaeological meeting;
- suspected assassinations protecting the secret.

All of this occurs within Lovecraft’s fiction. No corresponding police report, excavation, cult object, or historical religious organization exists outside the story.

### Human sacrifice and violence

The Louisiana episode explicitly includes mutilated human bodies and police violence against participants. The *Alert* episode includes piracy and lethal combat. These are documented features of the fiction, not documented practices of a real twentieth-century religious group.

Lovecraft’s construction also uses racialized and colonial ethnography. The cult is projected onto people described as “mixed-blood,” “degenerate,” foreign, Indigenous, African-descended, or socially marginal, while white male scholars and officials organize the documentary investigation. These representations are textual facts relevant to the cult’s design; they are not reliable ethnography of Inuit, Louisiana, Pacific, African, Arab, or Chinese religions.

### Fictional temples and cult centers

- **R’lyeh:** monumental prehuman city and “house” of Cthulhu; not a human temple and not archaeologically attested.
- **Louisiana swamp site:** temporary ritual clearing; no permanent sanctuary described.
- **Innsmouth’s Esoteric Order of Dagon:** a separate fictional church organization introduced in “The Shadow over Innsmouth.” It has meeting houses, oaths, ranks, economic functions, sacrifices, and reproductive covenants with Deep Ones.
- **Mu and Xothic temples:** chiefly Carter-era literary elaborations.
- **Game settings:** add temples, lodges, island cults, archaeological ruins, priesthoods, and ritual manuals as scenario devices.

### Offerings, prayers, and ritual language

The sole universally recognizable Lovecraftian liturgical text is the R’lyeh formula. The story also records ecstatic chant, circular dancing, preservation of idols, concealment of knowledge, and expectation of awakening. It does not give a complete service, calendar, ordination system, dietary rule, or ethical code.

### Real occult and religious practice

#### Typhonian current

Kenneth Grant incorporated Lovecraftian entities into a synthesis of Thelema, Tantra, sexual magic, surrealism, ufology, and “nightside” occultism. For Grant, fictional creation could transmit or symbolize powers beyond ordinary consciousness. His *Cults of the Shadow* (1975) and the larger Typhonian Trilogies are the principal sources. Henrik Bogdan describes the tradition as a modern occult synthesis involving “Lovecraftian gnosis” in [his scholarly overview](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315745916-39/kenneth-grant-typhonian-tradition-henrik-bogdan).

#### LaVeyan ritual reception

Anton LaVey and Michael Aquino’s *The Satanic Rituals* (1972) includes the “Ceremony of the Nine Angles,” influenced by Lovecraftian fiction and invoking invented cosmic names. This is a ritual adaptation, not evidence of a pre-Lovecraft Satanic Cthulhu cult.

#### Simon *Necronomicon*

The so-called Simon *Necronomicon* appeared in a limited edition in 1977 and subsequently as a mass-market paperback. It combined Mesopotamian names and materials, modern ceremonial magic, and Lovecraftian motifs while presenting an aura of ancient transmission. Scholars of grimoires classify it as a modern literary-occult construction or hoax, though practitioners may use it sincerely. It is not a translation of a manuscript by Lovecraft’s fictional Abdul Alhazred. [Publication overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Necronomicon).

#### Esoteric Order of Dagon

Real occult groups later adopted the fictional name. One contemporary organization states explicitly that its title comes from Lovecraft’s 1931 story and that no single historical “real E.O.D.” precedes the fiction. It practices Lovecraft-inspired magic rather than claiming institutional continuity from Innsmouth. [Organization FAQ](https://esotericorderofdagon.net/faq.php).

#### Cult of Cthulhu

Venger Satanis reported conceiving the Cult of Cthulhu in 2004 as a Left-Hand Path religion and magical order. Its texts combine Satanism, Fourth Way teachings, occultism, and Lovecraftian imagery. The founder’s claims of contact with Great Old Ones are practitioner testimony, not independent historical verification. [Founder interview](https://warlockasyluminternationalnews.com/warlock-asylums-interview-with-cult-of-cthulhu-founder-venger-satanis/) and the movement’s [published cult text](https://hermetic.com/_media/key23/cultofcthulhu.pdf).

No reliable census establishes the number of literal Cthulhu devotees. Online membership, role-play, aesthetic identification, magical experimentation, parody, and propositional belief frequently overlap.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### 1926–1928: invention and first publication

Lovecraft created the story, typescript, name, cult formula, and image in 1926. The 1928 *Weird Tales* publication made the being public. The evidence is literary and graphic; there was no independent cultic phase.

### 1928–1937: reciprocal Mythos building

Lovecraft reused Cthulhu in allusions and placed the being within a growing pseudo-scholarly network. Correspondents borrowed one another’s books, gods, places, and magical names. This was collaborative literary play, not a centrally governed franchise.

### 1937–1960s: Arkham House and Derlethian canon formation

Lovecraft died in 1937. August Derleth and Donald Wandrei founded Arkham House to preserve his work. Its first book, *The Outsider and Others* (1939), printed 1,268 copies and included “The Call of Cthulhu.” Derleth’s own publishing history documents those figures in [*Arkham House: The First 20 Years*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/76245/76245-h/76245-h.htm).

Derleth coined or popularized “Cthulhu Mythos,” published continuations, and systematized the beings into opposed camps and elements. This both preserved Lovecraft and changed the conceptual framework through which later readers encountered him.

### 1960s–1970s: anthology, paperback, and occult expansion

Paperback circulation widened readership. Derleth’s *Tales of the Cthulhu Mythos* (1969) helped establish a multi-author canon. Grant, LaVey/Aquino, and the Simon *Necronomicon* moved Lovecraftian material into operative occult literature. Carter and Lumley developed dynasties, wars, sealed gods, and adventure cycles.

### From 1981: ludic standardization

Sandy Petersen’s *Call of Cthulhu* role-playing game first appeared in 1981. Its investigators, sanity mechanics, forbidden books, spells, cults, and monster entries made the Mythos playable and reproducible. Chaosium’s official history calls it a genre-defining game and confirms Petersen and 1981. [Chaosium RPG history](https://cthulhuwiki.chaosium.com/rules/).

The game’s importance is difficult to overstate. It supplied a practical canon where Lovecraft had supplied fragments: taxonomic categories, comparative power, standardized spellings, recognizable visual designs, and repeatable ritual plots. International editions greatly expanded Cthulhu’s reach; the game became particularly influential in Europe and Japan.

### 1990s–2000s: cyberculture, hyperstition, and the Internet

The CCRU at Warwick combined cybernetics, rave culture, occultism, horror, Deleuze and Guattari, numerology, and science fiction. Its Cthulhu Club narratives treated Lovecraftian fiction as a carrier of future forces. The Internet simultaneously enabled archives, fan fiction, role-playing communities, image circulation, and pseudo-historical claims.

### 2000s–present: mass icon and plural devotion

Cthulhu now appears in:

- tabletop, card, board, and video games;
- sculpture, tattoos, plush toys, clothing, and jewelry;
- parody campaign merchandise;
- metal, ambient, electronic, and experimental music;
- webcomics and memes;
- occult orders and solitary magical practice;
- philosophical discussion of nonhuman agency, ecological horror, markets, and AI.

There is no unitary contemporary Cthulhu church. The current field ranges from sincere ritual engagement to ironic consumer identity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature

Lovecraft-circle sharing made the Mythos an early open, reciprocal fictional network. Derleth then imposed editorial coherence. Later major contributors include Ramsey Campbell, Brian Lumley, Lin Carter, T. E. D. Klein, Caitlín R. Kiernan, Thomas Ligotti, Laird Barron, Ruthanna Emrys, Victor LaValle, and many others.

Modern writers frequently reverse Lovecraft’s perspective: groups he rendered as hereditary or racial threats become protagonists; cosmic beings become ecological forces, colonial metaphors, victims, or incomprehensible neighbors. These are modern counter-readings, not restorations of a suppressed original canon.

### Film and audio

No single film has become an uncontested canonical visualization comparable to the role-playing game. Materially important adaptations include:

- *The Call of Cthulhu* (H. P. Lovecraft Historical Society, 2005), a silent-film pastiche closely following the story;
- radio dramas and “found document” adaptations;
- films that use Cthulhu-like beings without directly adapting the narrative;
- the 2010 *South Park* trilogy “Coon 2: Hindsight,” “Mysterion Rises,” and “Coon vs. Coon and Friends,” which made Cthulhu a comic mass-media character. Its inclusion is documented in [scholarship on Lovecraft’s popular reception](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/179277/1/52-1-161-2-10-20211214-1.pdf).

### Games

Besides Chaosium’s RPG:

- *Arkham Horror* and its related board/card-game family translated Mythos investigation into cooperative play;
- numerous video games use “sanity,” cult investigation, sunken cities, and forbidden texts;
- games often promote Cthulhu from one entity among many to a final boss or supreme antagonist;
- statistics create an apparent precision—strength, armor, spells, hit points—that has no equivalent in Lovecraft.

### Visual art and commerce

The commercial image is usually green, symmetrical, muscular, winged, and colossal. Lovecraft’s own drawing is smaller, squatter, and idol-like. Cute “baby Cthulhu” and plush forms invert the original aesthetic of revulsion. “Cthulhu for President” slogans turn apocalypse into political satire. These uses testify to recognition, not worship.

### Psychology and philosophy

Cthulhu has been read as:

- the return of repressed material;
- an archetype of the monstrous unconscious;
- trauma that defeats representation;
- scientific decentering of humanity;
- climate catastrophe or oceanic ecological revenge;
- networked systems beyond individual control;
- the sublime scale of deep time;
- xenophobic projection in Lovecraft’s racial imagination.

These are datable interpretations, not intrinsic ancient meanings.

### Land, AI, and accelerationism

Land’s enduring conceptual move is to remove humans from the position of sovereign technological agents. Capital and computation recursively improve and reorganize their own conditions. The “singularity” is therefore imagined less as a tool achieving consciousness than as alien intelligence assembling itself through human institutions.

Cthulhu fits this structure in four ways:

1. **Dormant futurity:** it is already present but awaits conditions for activation.
2. **Distributed cult:** humans who think they serve it may merely be components of its arrival.
3. **Dream transmission:** influence precedes physical manifestation.
4. **Hyperstition:** the story of arrival helps produce the networks through which something analogous arrives.

The identification remains metaphorical and theory-fictional. Neither “Meltdown” nor the CCRU corpus provides a systematic creed saying “Cthulhu is literally AI.” Later summaries often make the equation more definite than Land’s texts warrant.

### Political appropriation

Accelerationism subsequently divided into left, right, unconditional, technological, and extremist usages. Land himself moved toward neoreactionary and anti-democratic positions. Cthulhu imagery circulates in some online accelerationist and far-right milieus, but no evidence supports attributing all Cthulhu fandom, all accelerationism, or even all Land-influenced writing to extremist organization.

A precise distinction is necessary:

- Lovecraft’s fictional cult is violent and apocalyptic.
- Land’s early theory-fiction welcomes or dramatizes human displacement by technocapital.
- Later violent political accelerationists use “acceleration” differently, usually for social destabilization.
- Shared vocabulary does not by itself establish institutional descent or operational coordination.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was there a coherent Lovecraftian pantheon?

The older Derlethian answer was yes: Lovecraft devised a conflict between benevolent Elder Gods and evil elemental Old Ones. Later textual scholarship, especially that associated with S. T. Joshi, argues that Lovecraft’s references are deliberately inconsistent, aesthetic rather than systematic, and grounded in cosmic indifferentism rather than Christian moral dualism.

The current defensible position is that Lovecraft created a recurring network, not a fixed theology. Derleth’s system is historically important but should be labeled as Derleth’s.

### Is Cthulhu actually a god?

Within the fiction, human beings worship Cthulhu as a god. The pseudo-scientific record suggests an extraterrestrial or extradimensional organism. These are not mutually exclusive within a phenomenology of religion: “god” describes cultic status, while “alien” describes proposed nature. Lovecraft exploits that tension.

### Is Cthulhu the chief Mythos deity?

Not securely in Lovecraft. Azathoth, Yog-Sothoth, and other entities occupy more metaphysically encompassing positions in various texts. Cthulhu became representative because:

- his story is exceptionally memorable;
- he has a stable visual form;
- his name titles Derleth’s “Mythos”;
- the 1981 role-playing game bears his name;
- tentacles provide a reproducible logo.

### Sources of the story

Frequently proposed influences include:

- Lord Dunsany’s artificial pantheons;
- Arthur Machen’s secret survivals and occult horror;
- Algernon Blackwood’s cosmic and natural horror;
- dreams recorded by Lovecraft;
- archaeological discovery narratives;
- nineteenth-century comparative religion;
- theosophical language;
- contemporary fascination with Easter Island and unexplored Pacific spaces;
- Alfred Lord Tennyson’s “The Kraken.”

Influence is not identity. No source contains Cthulhu before Lovecraft.

### The name’s alleged antiquity

Claims of Sumerian, Arabic, Greek, or Polynesian derivation remain unproved. Resemblances to *chthonic*, *Cthulhut*, *Kutulu*, or invented “Sumerian” forms generally arose after publication. The Simon *Necronomicon* especially encouraged pseudo-Mesopotamian retrojection.

### Cthulhu and racism

Scholarship debates whether the cult’s racialized composition is incidental pulp convention, central xenophobic fantasy, critique of Western rationality, or some combination. The documentary facts are clear: Lovecraft’s narrator uses racial hierarchy, degeneration language, and colonial stereotypes, and assigns secret nonhuman knowledge disproportionately to racialized or colonized populations.

Later reception has variously repeated, ignored, contextualized, parodied, or reversed those structures.

### Archive and textual criticism

Important evidentiary developments include:

- preservation of manuscripts and correspondence at Brown;
- Arkham House’s 1939 collection;
- publication of selected and later more complete letters;
- Joshi’s collation of manuscripts and printings;
- digitization of the 1928 magazine and manuscript records;
- growing study of collaborative revisions and editorial intervention.

The most serious remaining limitations are lost correspondence, uncertain composition details for some revisions, incomplete documentation of small occult groups, and the immense decentralized scale of Internet reception.

### Discredited or unsupported popular theories

- **“Lovecraft copied an ancient god.”** No evidence.
- **“Aleister Crowley named or worshipped Cthulhu.”** Chronology and texts do not support it.
- **“The Necronomicon is a surviving Arabic grimoire.”** Lovecraft invented it; commercial versions are modern.
- **“Cthulhu is canonically a water elemental.”** Derlethian, and internally awkward because water imprisons Cthulhu.
- **“Cthulhu is Lovecraft’s supreme evil Satan figure.”** A later moralization.
- **“Cthulhu and the Kraken/Tiamat/Dagon are historically the same deity.”** Modern comparison or syncretism.
- **“Land simply renamed artificial general intelligence Cthulhu.”** Overstatement; the relationship is allusive, structural, and theory-fictional.

### Evidentiary conclusions

The fullest reconstructable record begins not in prehistory but in Providence in 1926. Lovecraft fashioned an invented archive—idols, police testimony, sailor’s manuscript, newspaper clippings, archaeological comparison, chant, and cult tradition—whose internal form mimics historical reconstruction. Later writers treated that deliberately fragmentary archive as raw material for theology. Derleth supplied a moral cosmology; Carter and Lumley supplied genealogy; Chaosium supplied an operational canon; occultists supplied ritual; mass culture supplied a stable green icon; CCRU and Land supplied a cybernetic eschatology.

Cthulhu’s historical “life” is therefore a sequence of changes in medium and authority:

**manuscript monster → pulp deity → shared-world node → systematized demon-god → role-playing antagonist → occult power → commercial icon → figure of inhuman planetary intelligence.**

No one stage cancels the others, but none should be projected backward. In Lovecraft, Cthulhu is neither an ancient god recovered from real inscriptions nor simply a modern AI allegory. The ancient cult is part of the 1926 fiction; the organized pantheon is largely posthumous; the operative magical being is a later religious reception; and the AI-singularity interpretation belongs especially to the late-twentieth- and early-twenty-first-century Land/CCRU reception.
