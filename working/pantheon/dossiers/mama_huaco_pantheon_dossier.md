# Mama Huaco: Pantheon Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary cautions

Mama Huaco—colonial spellings include *Mama Guaco*, *Mama Uaco*, *Mama Vaco*, and *Mamaguaca*—is a female founder, conqueror, agricultural ancestress, first *coya* or queen, and cult recipient in traditions concerning the foundation of Inca Cusco. “Demigod” is only an approximate modern classification. The sources variously treat her as:

- one of the superhuman Ayar siblings created by Viracocha;
- a daughter of the Sun and Moon;
- the mother, sister, or wife of Manco Cápac;
- an ancestral queen whose embalmed body received offerings;
- an effective *huaca* or sacred ancestral presence;
- and, in Christian polemic, a sorceress who instituted idolatry.

These classifications are not interchangeable. They belong to different colonial records, informants, political projects, and narrative arrangements.

No securely identified pre-Hispanic inscription names Mama Huaco. The Inca used oral specialists, ritual performances, painted boards, and *quipus*, but no surviving object can presently be read as a preconquest written attestation of her name. The earliest recoverable evidence is therefore sixteenth-century colonial writing based on Indigenous testimony. The earliest substantial text is Juan de Betanzos’s Spanish *Suma y narración de los Incas*, completed about 1551. Consequently, expressions such as “the ancient Inca believed” below mean “a colonial source reports that its Indigenous informants remembered or performed this,” not that an independent preconquest text verifies it.

The surviving accounts frequently disagree about Mama Huaco’s husband, parentage, children, and relation to the other Ayars. Those contradictions are primary evidence for changing and competing traditions.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### 1.1 Orientation

Mama Huaco belongs principally to the Cusco foundation cycle conventionally called the myth of the Ayar siblings. In its fullest colonial versions, four male and four female siblings emerge from a cave or “window” at Tambotoco/Pacarictambo and migrate toward Cusco. Through warfare, alliances, transformations into sacred stones, agricultural acts, and occupation of the landscape, they establish Inca sovereignty.

Within this cycle Mama Huaco is unusually active. She commands alongside Manco Cápac, discovers or confirms suitable soil by casting a golden object, plants the first maize, kills and dismembers a local opponent to terrify his community, and becomes the focus of a state agricultural rite. Some traditions pair her with Ayar Cachi or Ayar Uchu; others with Ayar Auca; still others make her Manco Cápac’s wife, sister, or mother. Guamán Poma makes her simultaneously mother and wife of Manco Cápac and calls her the first *coya*.

She is therefore better classified as a divine or sacralized founder-ancestress and culture heroine than as a goddess possessing a universally defined natural domain.

### 1.2 Names and spellings

| Form | Language or orthography | Attestation or use |
|---|---|---|
| **Mama Guaco** | Sixteenth-century Spanish spelling | Betanzos, Sarmiento de Gamboa, Cabello de Balboa and several later editions |
| **Mama Huaco** | Modern Hispanicized form | Dominant in twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship |
| **Mama Uaco / Mama Vaco** | Guamán Poma’s spelling and modern transcription | *Primer nueva corónica*, pp. 120–121 |
| **Mamaguaca / Mama Huaca** | Variant or scribal/ editorial spelling | Appears sporadically in colonial and later reproductions; it must not automatically be interpreted as *mama wak’a*, “mother huaca” |
| **Mama Waq(u)** | Modern normalized reconstruction | Scholarly normalization, not an attested Inca spelling |
| **Mama Vaco Coya** | Title in Guamán Poma | First queen in his succession of Inca royal women |

### 1.3 Elements and etymology

**Mama.** In Quechua and Aymara, *mama* denotes “mother” and functions honorifically for a senior, generative, or sacred female. In the founder’s name it is simultaneously kinship term and title.

**Huaco/Guaco/Uaco.** The etymology is disputed. A significant proposal derives it from Aymara *huaccu*, recorded by Ludovico Bertonio in his 1612 *Vocabulario de la lengua aymara* as a “muger varonil” who disregards cold and labor. Rodolfo Cerrón-Palomino has used this lexical evidence in arguing that *waqu* is intelligible through Aymara rather than ordinary Quechua. If accepted, “Mama Huaco” approximates “Lady/Mother [the] manly or hardy woman.” This suits the martial narratives, but suitability to a myth is not proof of etymology. Bertonio’s dictionary postdates the earliest Mama Huaco texts by about sixty years, and no source states that her name was coined from this definition. Bertonio’s original vocabulary is accessible through the [Open Library record and facsimile link](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL24354768M/Vocabulario_de_la_lengua_aymara._Primera_parte).

Older or popular equations with Spanish *huaco* as a generic archaeological pot are anachronistic. That modern archaeological usage does not explain the founder’s name.

### 1.4 Titles

- **Mama** — “mother,” “lady,” or senior sacred female; intrinsic to every early name-form.
- **Coya/Quya** — royal wife or queen. Guamán Poma explicitly captions her “Mama Vaco Coya” and makes her the first of the queens.
- **Founder or founding mother** — a modern analytical label accurately describing her narrative role, but not a quoted ancient epithet.
- **Warrior queen** — modern descriptive usage grounded in Betanzos, Sarmiento, Cabello, and Murúa.
- **Goddess of strength, war, fertility, or maize** — common modern cataloguing labels, but none is a securely attested exclusive Inca cult title. Her martial and agricultural functions are documented; a formally delimited divine “portfolio” is not.

There is no documented pre-Hispanic interpretatio equating Mama Huaco with a European deity. Comparisons with Athena, Amazons, fertility goddesses, or Jungian archetypes are modern analogies.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 Earliest securely recoverable evidence

The earliest substantial named attestation is Juan de Betanzos’s *Suma y narración de los Incas*, composed in Spanish around 1551 for Viceroy Antonio de Mendoza. Betanzos knew Quechua and was married to Doña Angelina Yupanqui, formerly associated with Atahualpa and Francisco Pizarro, giving him unusual access to elite Inca testimony. The original complete manuscript is not preserved as an autograph; the work’s transmission is complicated, and the first part was published only in the nineteenth century. A fuller manuscript was identified in the twentieth century.

Betanzos places Mama Guaco in the Ayar migration. She is the wife of the Ayar who disappears in the cave—called Ayar Oche in relevant textual traditions—and participates in taking Cusco. She kills a man of the local settlement with *ayllos*, opens his body, removes heart and lungs, and inflates the lungs in sight of his companions. The terrified inhabitants flee. This is the earliest securely datable literary witness to her martial-terror episode. A digitized Spanish edition is available through the [Biblioteca Antológica](https://www.biblioteca-antologica.org/es/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/BETANZOS-Suma-y-narraci%C3%B3n-de-los-Incas.pdf).

What Betanzos does **not** provide is a pre-Hispanic date for the act, an independent physical depiction, or evidence that every Inca community knew the same genealogy.

### 2.2 Principal colonial corpus

#### Juan de Betanzos, *Suma y narración de los Incas* — c. 1551, Spanish

**Tier:** earliest substantial attestation based on colonial-period Indigenous testimony.

Mama Guaco appears in the Paqaritampu migration, the defeat of the pre-Inca inhabitants, and the foundation of Cusco. Betanzos preserves the visceral terror tactic and connects the founders with seeds brought from their place of emergence. His source is particularly important because it precedes Viceroy Toledo’s systematic historical inquiries.

#### Pedro Cieza de León, *Crónica del Perú*, Part II — composed c. 1550–1553, Spanish

**Tier:** early comparative witness.

Cieza records traditions of Manco Cápac, Pacaritambo, and the Inca beginnings, but does not give Mama Huaco the elaborate autonomous role found in Betanzos and Sarmiento. His relative silence demonstrates that the four-pair cycle was not transmitted identically by every early compiler.

#### Cristóbal de Molina “el Cuzqueño,” *Relación de las fábulas y ritos de los Incas* — c. 1573–1575, Spanish with Quechua ritual vocabulary

**Tier:** early colonial ritual testimony.

Molina’s surviving text derives from information obtained in Cusco among ritual specialists and elders. Its original is lost; the principal copy is Biblioteca Nacional de España MS 3169. The work associates Mama Huaco with the first maize and describes the annual harvesting of maize from Sausero/Sahuasera for chicha used in service of her embalmed body. A modern English critical translation is catalogued by the [Smithsonian Institution](https://www.si.edu/object/account-fables-and-rites-incas-cristobal-de-molina-introduction-brian-s-bauer-translated-and-edited%3Asiris_sil_968265); an early Spanish edition is accessible through [CSIC Simurg](https://simurg.csic.es/view/990000117390204201/relacion-de-las-fabulas-y-ritos-de-los-incas-relacion-de-la-conquista-y-poblacion-del-peru).

#### Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia de los Incas* — 1572, Spanish

**Tier:** systematic Toledan compilation; indispensable but politically interested.

Sarmiento wrote under Viceroy Francisco de Toledo to demonstrate that the Incas were recent tyrants rather than legitimate immemorial sovereigns. He says Viracocha produced the Ayars from Cápac-toco without ordinary parents. He names the women Mama Ocllo, Mama Guaco, Mama Ipacura/Mama Cura, and Mama Raua. Mama Guaco and Manco Cápac become military leaders. She tests the land, helps direct the migration, and commits the gruesome killing of a Hualla/Gualla opponent. His version emphasizes conquest, terror, massacre, and usurpation in keeping with the Toledan argument. A digitized edition is available from [Intiyana](https://intiyana.org/bibliodig/files/original/944d24060d55c779ac0747ce880a0ebad1deed50.pdf).

#### Miguel Cabello de Balboa, *Miscelánea antártica* — completed 1586, Spanish

**Tier:** later sixteenth-century synthesis/regional variant.

Cabello reproduces the conquest episode with *ayllo* or sling/boleadora and knife. Mama Huaco removes the victim’s organs, inflates the lungs, places them across her mouth, and has her companions smear themselves with blood. The Huallas flee because they believe the newcomers eat human flesh. This is a more elaborated form than Betanzos’s already violent account.

#### Martín de Murúa, *Historia del origen y genealogía real de los reyes ingas del Pirú* and *Historia general del Piru* — c. 1590 and 1611–1616, Spanish

**Tier:** late colonial elaboration with illustrated manuscript evidence.

Murúa calls Mama Huaco a woman of great courage, judgment, and discretion and attributes to her the killing and inflation of the lungs. In the later Getty manuscript she is represented as “Coya Mamahuaco,” in pigment and ink on paper, fol. 23. The object is J. Paul Getty Museum MS Ludwig XIII 16, accession 83.MP.159. The [museum-linked image record](https://aaeportal.com/images/65886/coya-mamahuaco) dates the manuscript image to 1611–1613; the Getty’s manuscript studies reproduce and discuss it in [*The Getty Murúa*](https://www.getty.edu/publications/resources/virtuallibrary/0892368945.pdf).

#### Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, *Comentarios reales de los Incas*, Part I — 1609, Spanish

**Tier:** influential learned harmonization.

Garcilaso foregrounds the Titicaca version in which the Sun sends Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo to civilize humanity. His form “Mama Ocllo Huaco” has encouraged conflation of Mama Ocllo and Mama Huaco. Garcilaso’s elegant two-founder account became disproportionately influential in education and nationalist retellings. It should not be retrojected as the single original version.

#### Joan de Santa Cruz Pachacuti Yamqui Salcamaygua, *Relación de antigüedades deste reyno del Pirú* — c. 1613, Spanish and Quechua

**Tier:** Indigenous-Christian colonial synthesis.

Pachacuti Yamqui records a founder migration and supplies significant landscape and religious material, but his configuration of Manco Cápac and the female founders is not identical to Sarmiento’s. It is important evidence that seventeenth-century Indigenous authors were actively reorganizing older narratives within Christian universal history.

#### Felipe Guamán Poma de Ayala, *El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno* — completed c. 1615, Spanish with Quechua

**Tier:** Indigenous colonial reinterpretation and Christian polemic.

On manuscript pages 120–121 Guamán Poma identifies Mama Uaco as the first *coya*. He says she was beautiful, dark-complexioned, dressed in rose, and wore large silver *tupus*. He makes her daughter of Sun and Moon, mother and eventual wife of Manco Cápac, a worker of wonders through demons, and one who made stones, crags, idols, and *huacas* speak. He says she governed more effectively than Manco, aided the poor, lived 200 years, and died in Cusco during Sinchi Roca’s time. These particulars are unique or strongly distinctive to Guamán Poma and cannot be treated as the common Ayar version.

The autograph, Royal Danish Library GKS 2232 4º, contains the drawing captioned “Mama Vaco Coya / Reyno en el Cuzco.” The complete manuscript is described and digitized by the [Royal Danish Library](https://www.kb.dk/en/find-materials/collections/manuscript-collection/chronicle-guaman-poma); a searchable transcription begins at the [Guamán Poma digital research site](https://poma.kb.dk/). A complete textual reproduction of the relevant pages is also available in this [facsimile/transcription PDF](https://docs.enriquedussel.com/txt/Textos_200_Obras/Relacion_critica_oprimidos/Primer_cronica.1-Guaman_Poma.pdf).

#### Bernabé Cobo, *Historia del Nuevo Mundo* — completed 1653, Spanish

**Tier:** seventeenth-century synthesis drawing on earlier records.

Cobo preserves extensive information about Cusco’s shrines, ceques, calendar, and royal cult. He identifies Sausero as a field of the Sun and describes solemn cultivation there. This partly conflicts with Molina, who directly associates its produce with Mama Huaco’s body. Cobo also reports a female maize effigy or vessel, *Mama Sara*, dressed as a woman and holding seed. Tom Zuidema proposed a close ritual connection between this vessel, Sausero, and Mama Huaco; that identification is a scholarly reconstruction, not an explicit statement that the two were the same being.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### 3.1 Domains securely supported by the sources

**Foundation and territorial seizure.** Betanzos and Sarmiento make her an active agent in occupying Cusco. She does not merely accompany Manco Cápac.

**Warfare and intimidation.** Her killing and display of a local victim’s organs is narrated by Betanzos, Sarmiento, Cabello, and Murúa. Sarmiento calls her fierce and cruel; Cabello further ritualizes the display with blood-smearing.

**Agriculture and first maize.** Molina associates her with sowing the first maize near Cusco and with the Sausero harvest maintained for her body.

**Royal and ancestral authority.** Guamán Poma calls her the first *coya* and states that Cusco obeyed her more than her husband.

**Divination or vitality through lungs.** The inflation of the victim’s lungs resembles *callpa/kallpa* inspection, in which inflated lungs were read for strength or omens. The chronicles narrate the act; interpreting the killing as a formal instance of *callparicucuy* is scholarly inference.

**Creation or institution of huacas.** Only Guamán Poma explicitly makes her the first institutor of *huacas*, idols, sorcery, and enchantments. His language is Christian demonological polemic, not a neutral transcript of Inca theology.

### 3.2 Powers and limitations

The texts credit her with:

- knowledge of fertile land;
- successful casting of the settlement marker;
- military leadership;
- exceptional bodily courage;
- terror-inducing manipulation of a corpse;
- agricultural innovation;
- communication with animate sacred landscape;
- longevity and political supremacy in Guamán Poma.

No source gives her omnipotence, a cosmic throne, a fixed celestial body, or a universal empire-wide priesthood. Modern lists assigning her a sacred color, weekday, planet, animal, or numerical correspondence lack early documentation.

### 3.3 Weapons and regalia

- **Ayllo/haybinto:** a stone attached to cords, broadly comparable to a bola or sling-like weapon, used against the Hualla victim in Sarmiento and related accounts.
- **Knife/tumi:** explicitly or implicitly used to open the body in Cabello’s elaboration.
- **Golden staff or marker:** in Sarmiento she, rather than Manco, casts the object that penetrates suitable soil. Modern retellings frequently transfer this act to Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo.
- **Rose-colored dress and large silver tupus:** Guamán Poma, p. 121.
- **Coya costume:** Murúa’s portrait gives her the *lliclla* mantle over an *anacu* tunic.

### 3.4 Surviving images

There is no securely identified pre-Hispanic portrait.

1. **Guamán Poma, “Mama Vaco Coya,” c. 1615.** Ink drawing in the autograph *Nueva corónica*, GKS 2232 4º, pp. 120–121. This is a colonial Indigenous image, not an Inca-period cult statue.

2. **Murúa manuscript, “Coya Mamahuaco,” c. 1611–1613/1616.** Pigment and ink on handmade laid paper, J. Paul Getty Museum MS Ludwig XIII 16, fol. 23. It is a postconquest royal portrait embedded in a Spanish clerical history.

3. **Tadeo Escalante, Mama Huaco mural, early nineteenth century.** At the so-called Molino de los Incas in Acomayo, Escalante depicted her among Inca dynastic and Christian-historical subjects, accompanied by a camelid. The documented mural is reproduced in a [Bibliographica Americana study](https://fierro.bn.gov.ar/micrositios/admin_assets/issues/files/83329229af273e94f6805c238da7dd6b.pdf). The llama belongs to Escalante’s colonial-republican visual program; no sixteenth-century source establishes it as Mama Huaco’s sacred animal.

Modern statues showing an athletic warrior with a spear, shield, or standardized Inca crown are retrospective creations rather than copies of a known cult image.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### 4.1 Competing genealogies

| Source | Parentage | Partner | Relation to Manco Cápac | Children |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Betanzos, c. 1551 | Not specified conventionally; emerges with Ayars | Wife of the Ayar lost in the cave, commonly aligned with Ayar Uchu/Oche in this recension | Later companion in the founding group, not clearly mother | None securely assigned |
| Sarmiento, 1572 | Produced from Cápac-toco by Ticci Viracocha; no human parents | Pairings vary in readings and later summaries; she ultimately acts with Manco | Sister and co-commander; later accounts may make her his wife | None securely specified |
| Cabello, 1586 | Ayar founder | Variant pairing | Co-founder and possible wife | Not securely specified |
| Murúa, c. 1590–1616 | Founder ancestress | Manco in royal genealogy | Coya and martial partner | Varies by manuscript arrangement |
| Garcilaso, 1609 | His Mama Ocllo is sent by the Sun | Manco Cápac | Sister-wife; “Ocllo Huaco” partly conflates figures | Dynastic mother |
| Guamán Poma, c. 1615 | Daughter of Sun and Moon | Manco Cápac | Both mother and wife | Names Inca Yupanqui, Pachacuti Inca, and Chimbo Urma; a unique, chronologically compressed genealogy |
| Molina/Cobo ritual tradition | Primordial founder | Not central to the ritual notice | Sister of first Inca | No children relevant to Sausero rite |

The neat modern arrangement “Ayar Cachi–Mama Huaco, Ayar Uchu–Mama Cura, Ayar Auca–Mama Rahua, Manco–Mama Ocllo” represents one harmonized arrangement, not a universal colonial consensus.

### 4.2 Every documented divine or sacral interaction

#### Ticci Viracocha

**Interaction:** Viracocha creates or causes Mama Guaco and the other Ayars to emerge from Cápac-toco to become rulers.

**Source/date:** Sarmiento de Gamboa, *Historia de los Incas*, 1572.

**Tier:** early colonial systematic version, shaped by the Toledan inquiry.

#### Inti, the Sun

**Interaction A:** In Guamán Poma, Mama Uaco claims the Sun as her father and requests a dowry from him before marrying Manco Cápac; the Sun supplies it.

**Source/date:** Guamán Poma, *Nueva corónica*, c. 1615, p. 121.

**Tier:** late Indigenous-Christian colonial elaboration.

**Interaction B:** In Molina’s ritual account, maize associated with her is incorporated into the wider state ceremonial economy that also serves the Sun.

**Source/date:** Molina, c. 1573–1575.

**Tier:** early colonial ritual attestation; association, not identity.

#### The Moon

**Interaction:** Guamán Poma calls Mama Uaco daughter of the Sun and Moon.

**Source/date:** *Nueva corónica*, c. 1615.

**Tier:** late Indigenous-Christian colonial genealogy; not supported by Betanzos or Sarmiento.

#### Manco Cápac/Ayar Manco

**Interaction:** Brother, commander, husband, or son-husband depending on the source. They jointly lead the migration and conquest in Sarmiento. Guamán Poma makes her govern more effectively than him.

**Sources/dates:** Betanzos c. 1551; Sarmiento 1572; Murúa c. 1590–1616; Guamán Poma c. 1615.

**Tier:** core interaction, with mutually incompatible genealogical elaborations.

#### Ayar Cachi

**Interaction:** In some arrangements Mama Huaco is his sister-wife; in Sarmiento-derived narrative she participates in shaming or persuading the dangerously powerful Ayar to return to the cave, enabling his confinement.

**Source/date:** Sarmiento, 1572, with differing editorial pairings.

**Tier:** early colonial variant. The husband-identification is unstable.

#### Ayar Uchu/Ayar Oche/Huanacauri

**Interaction:** Betanzos associates her with the Ayar who disappears or becomes fixed in the sacred landscape; later textual systems identify this Ayar with Huanacauri.

**Sources/dates:** Betanzos c. 1551; Molina c. 1573–1575; later syntheses.

**Tier:** early but textually unstable. The equivalence among names varies by recension.

#### Ayar Auca

**Interaction:** Sister and fellow migrant; some genealogical arrangements pair him with Mama Huaco. He becomes fixed at or takes possession of the future Cusco in the Ayar cycle.

**Sources/dates:** Sarmiento 1572; Cabello 1586; later harmonizations.

**Tier:** regional/textual variant; no independent episode of combat or romance between them is consistently narrated.

#### Mama Ocllo

**Interaction:** Fellow female founder and sister. The two occupy complementary places in many modern readings: Mama Ocllo as dynastic/domestic and Mama Huaco as martial/agricultural. Garcilaso’s “Mama Ocllo Huaco” partly fuses their identities.

**Sources/dates:** Betanzos c. 1551; Sarmiento 1572; Garcilaso 1609.

**Tier:** early co-appearance; fusion is a learned colonial harmonization.

#### Mama Cura/Ipacura and Mama Rahua/Raua

**Interaction:** Fellow female Ayar founders and sisters; no securely attested independent confrontation or alliance beyond collective migration.

**Sources/dates:** Sarmiento 1572 and later Ayar accounts.

**Tier:** early group association. More specific interactions should not be invented.

#### Mama Sara

**Interaction:** Cobo describes the female maize vessel or “Mother Maize”; Zuidema suggested that Sausero’s seed vessel and rites illuminate Mama Huaco’s agricultural identity.

**Source/date:** Cobo, 1653; Zuidema, twentieth–twenty-first-century scholarship.

**Tier:** scholarly reconstruction, not an explicit ancient equation.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 Emergence from Tambotoco

**Early attested cycle:** Betanzos, c. 1551; systematized by Sarmiento, 1572.

Four male and four female Ayars emerge from Cápac-toco, the central opening in the hill Tambotoco near Pacaritambo. In Sarmiento they have no ordinary parents: Viracocha produces them to become lords. Other peoples emerge from the adjacent windows Maras-toco and Sutic-toco. The Ayars carry valuables, insignia, clothing, and seed and gather ten *ayllus*.

The group is already adult and socially organized. The cave is a *paqarina*, a place of collective emergence and identity, not a biological birthplace in the ordinary sense.

**Variant:** Garcilaso replaces the four-pair emphasis with Sun-sent Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo emerging near Lake Titicaca. This more compact version became pedagogically dominant but should not erase the Pacaritambo cycle.

### 5.2 Removal of the dangerous Ayar

One male Ayar possesses frightening power, hurling slingstones that split hills and create ravines. The others fear that followers will transfer allegiance to him. They arrange for him to return to the origin cave for ritual objects or seeds, where an accomplice seals him inside.

In Sarmiento-derived retellings, Mama Huaco challenges his courage, helping induce him to go. The episode makes her a political strategist, but the precise male founder’s name and her marital connection to him differ among sources.

**Function:** Scholars have interpreted the sequential disappearance or petrification of the male Ayars as the transformation of rival founders into territorial shrines and subordinate descent groups. That is structural reconstruction, not an explanation offered by the sixteenth-century characters themselves.

### 5.3 Discovery of the proper land

The founders test prospective settlement sites. In the famous golden-staff motif, a gold object must sink into fertile soil. In Sarmiento, Mama Huaco casts the marker and identifies the place of settlement. Later popular versions usually give the act to Manco Cápac or divide it between Manco and Mama Ocllo.

This transfer is historically significant: it reduces the martial female founder’s autonomous initiative and fits the more symmetrical civilizing couple popularized through Garcilaso.

### 5.4 Killing of the Hualla/Gualla opponent

**Earliest form:** Betanzos, c. 1551.

Near Cusco, Mama Guaco strikes and kills a local man with *ayllos*, opens him, removes his heart and lungs, and inflates the lungs before the inhabitants. They flee in terror.

**Sarmiento, 1572:** She kills and dismembers a Gualla, takes the heart and lungs in her mouth, and advances with a stone-and-cord weapon. Sarmiento calls the act fierce and “diabolical”; his political narrative also reports broad slaughter, including violence against pregnant women.

**Cabello, 1586:** She or the founding party opens the victim with a knife, inflates and wears the organs, and smears companions with blood. The residents conclude that the invaders are cannibals and abandon the settlement.

**Murúa, c. 1611:** He credits her courage and says her display caused horror.

The sources report mutilation and terror. They do not document routine Mama Huaco cult cannibalism. The supposed cannibalism is the frightened opponents’ inference within Cabello’s narrative. The act may evoke lung divination or the display of *kallpa*, “force,” but a formal ritual classification remains interpretive.

### 5.5 First maize and the twofold conquest

Molina says Mama Huaco planted the first maize at the field called Sausero/Sahuasera below an arch near Cusco. Each year maize from this field was collected to make chicha for her embalmed body. Later structural interpretation, especially by Zuidema, reads the planting and the next year’s harvest as two stages of conquest: possession through sowing and reaffirmation through reaping.

A modern discussion of Cusco irrigation places Sausero within channels descending toward the city and notes the tradition of Mama Huaco’s first maize in an [Allpanchis study](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/8463429.pdf).

Molina’s association is explicit; the exact calendrical-astronomical reconstruction of her route is scholarly.

### 5.6 Guamán Poma’s first queen

Guamán Poma detaches Mama Uaco from the Ayar narrative and gives her a royal dossier. She is daughter of Sun and Moon, mother and wife of Manco Cápac, first queen, wonder-worker, benefactor of the poor, superior governor, and founder of the Incas’ idolatrous order. She dies aged 200 in Sinchi Roca’s reign.

This is not a simple preservation of “the original myth.” It combines Indigenous royal memory, Christian demonology, biblical-style longevity, polemic against Inca legitimacy, and admiration for forceful female government.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### 6.1 Embalmed body

Molina provides the strongest evidence for a specific cult. During the harvest cycle, newly initiated noblemen went to Sausero to collect maize from the field where Mama Huaco supposedly planted the first crop. The field was cultivated annually for her body; its maize made the chicha required for serving that embalmed body. Persons were specifically responsible for it.

This is direct textual testimony to an organized ancestral cult, though the body itself has not been securely identified archaeologically. Whether it was understood as the physical corpse of a human forebear, a constructed ancestral bundle, a substitute effigy, or a ritual object cannot now be established.

### 6.2 Sausero/Sahuasera

Sausero lay near or south of Cusco and was integrated into the capital’s agricultural-ritual landscape. Molina calls it Mama Huaco’s first-maize field. Cobo instead identifies it as a field of the Sun whose produce supplied ordinary solar sacrifice. Both may reflect overlapping claims or later reassignment; harmonizing them as a single uncontested institution would conceal the source conflict.

Cobo describes the solemn sowing of Sausero, including sacrifices distributed among surrounding shrines, chicha libations, solar women or *mamaconas*, and a white camelid decorated with gold ear ornaments. These rites belong securely to Sausero’s state ceremonial complex, but Cobo does not make every component an offering exclusively to Mama Huaco.

### 6.3 Calendar

Molina associates the recovery of Sausero maize with **Ayrihua**, approximately April, following the maize harvest. Zuidema reconstructed an eight-day procession and calendrical route, relating it to solar positions and Mama Huaco’s conquest. The date range and astronomical interpretation depend on his model of the Cusco calendar and ceques; they are not stated in a surviving Inca calendar document.

### 6.4 Offerings

Documented or strongly supported offerings include:

- maize from Sausero;
- chicha brewed for her embalmed body;
- labor devoted to maintaining the field and body;
- participation by initiated nobles and ritual attendants.

There is no secure evidence for an independent Mama Huaco temple network, dedicated empire-wide priestly order, temple prostitution, or regular human sacrifice made specifically to her. Human sacrifice occurred in Inca state religion, particularly *capacocha*, but attaching all such sacrifice to Mama Huaco would be unsupported.

### 6.5 Cult center and archaeology

Her most securely attested cultic geography is Cusco and its southern agricultural approaches. Pacaritambo/Tambotoco and Huanacauri belong to the founder route. Archaeology has investigated the Pacariqtambo region and early Cusco settlement, but no excavated structure can be securely labeled “Temple of Mama Huaco” from an inscription.

Her reported embalmed body has not been found. Spanish authorities systematically seized, concealed, or destroyed royal mummies and sacred objects during colonization and campaigns against “idolatry.” The absence of the body today does not verify or disprove Molina’s report; it limits material confirmation.

### 6.6 Political economy

The ritual converted agriculture into a charter of sovereignty. The founder planted; state labor maintained the field; noble initiates retrieved its crop; specialists brewed chicha; and the ancestral body received continuing service. The rite joined conquest, control of irrigated land, royal initiation, maize surplus, and ancestral legitimacy.

Zuidema’s detailed reconstruction is summarized in the [open-access BIFEA article](https://journals.openedition.org/bifea/pdf/3193). Its historical value lies in connecting dispersed colonial notices, but its calendrical precision remains a scholarly model.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before 1532: reconstructable preconquest horizon

Mama Huaco was probably embedded in Cusco founder ritual before the Spanish invasion because Molina reports a developed annual service involving a named field, noble participants, chicha, and an embalmed body. That institutional complexity is difficult to explain as a purely literary colonial invention.

Nevertheless, no pre-1532 object names her. The date when her cult began—early Cusco, imperial expansion, or a late dynastic reform—cannot be securely determined. Proposals linking the myth’s final form to Pachacuti’s fifteenth-century state-building remain reconstructions.

### 1530s–1550s: conquest and first textualization

The Inca state collapsed amid civil war, Spanish invasion, epidemic disease, and factional realignment. Royal descendants and ritual experts continued transmitting origin accounts. Betanzos recorded Mama Guaco as a founding warrior about 1551. This earliest written figure is neither merely a mother nor a generalized fertility goddess.

### 1570s: Toledan systematization and ritual testimony

Two sharply different colonial records emerged:

- Sarmiento organized the founders into a political history of recent Inca conquest and tyranny.
- Molina documented surviving ritual knowledge and Mama Huaco’s agricultural-body cult.

Together they preserve the two principal dimensions of the figure: conquest and ceremonial maize.

### 1580s–1610s: narrative and visual elaboration

Cabello intensified the visceral terror episode. Murúa integrated Mama Huaco into illustrated royal genealogy. Garcilaso’s Manco–Mama Ocllo account displaced her in a more harmonious solar civilization story. Guamán Poma reintroduced her as first queen but interpreted her sacred power through Christian demonology.

The period therefore did not produce one “canonical” Mama Huaco. It produced competing colonial canons.

### Seventeenth century: antiquarian preservation and suppression

Cobo preserved ceque and agricultural data while Christian institutions continued extirpating *huacas*, confiscating sacred bundles, and redirecting ceremonial labor. No evidence demonstrates uninterrupted public institutional worship of Mama Huaco after the colonial eradication of royal mummy cults.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: dynastic memory and visual nationalism

Inca royal portrait cycles remained important in colonial and early republican Andean culture. Tadeo Escalante’s early nineteenth-century Acomayo mural placed Mama Huaco within a visual sequence combining Inca ancestry, Christian subjects, local memory, and emerging patriotic meanings. This was remembrance and reinvention, not straightforward continuation of her state cult.

### Twentieth century: scholarly recovery

The publication and rediscovery of colonial manuscripts shifted Mama Huaco from the margins of Garcilaso’s dominant Manco–Mama Ocllo story back toward the center of Inca studies.

- R. Tom Zuidema interpreted her through the ceque system, moiety organization, maize rites, and ritual calendar.
- María Rostworowski emphasized her as a forceful female leader and evidence for gender complementarity and female authority in Andean society.
- Irene Silverblatt placed female founders within the gendered politics of Inca empire and colonial transformation.
- Mariusz Ziółkowski and others examined her in relation to elite rivalries and the differentiated Ayar descent groups.
- Brian Bauer’s archaeological and textual work reconsidered Pacariqtambo and the sacred landscape rather than treating the legend as placeless fantasy.

### Twenty-first century

Mama Huaco appears in Peruvian education, feminist and Indigenous cultural commentary, museum interpretation, public art, online myth catalogues, and historical fiction. Some contemporary Andean spiritual or neopagan communities may invoke her, but no reliable demographic data establish a large, continuous, separately organized modern cult.

Claims that she was universally worshipped from Quito to Chile, had a fixed horoscope, was identical to Pachamama, or headed an ancient “Amazon priestess order” are not supported by the colonial corpus.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### 8.1 National and feminist reception

Modern authors frequently oppose Mama Huaco to the domesticated pedagogical image of Mama Ocllo: Huaco becomes warrior, strategist, sexually autonomous woman, and proof that Andean gender history cannot be reduced to passive queenship. This recovery has textual support, particularly in Betanzos and Sarmiento, but the binary “Huaco equals warrior/Ocllo equals homemaker” is a modern simplification of more fluid colonial traditions.

Recent scholarship continues using her to investigate female participation in warfare and parallel gender institutions. A 2025 study explicitly treats her with Chañan Cori Coca in relation to women and Inca war ritual: [Flores Mejía, “Rompiendo esquemas”](https://revistas.cultura.gob.pe/index.php/historiaycultura/article/view/581).

### 8.2 Literature, murals, and graphic reuse

Escalante’s mural is the most important later visual reinvention. Modern books, comics, educational posters, tourist sculpture, and digital art commonly derive her appearance from generic Inca noblewomen or modern warrior iconography rather than a pre-Hispanic prototype.

Guamán Poma’s drawing is also repeatedly detached from its hostile Christian textual frame. A modern graphic may reproduce his dignified queen while omitting his claim that her power came from demons. The resulting image is not fraudulent if presented as adaptation, but it is historically misleading when labeled an unchanged ancient icon.

### 8.3 Esoteric and New Age use

No Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or classical European grimoire tradition concerning Mama Huaco has been documented. Modern websites sometimes place her in universal “goddess” taxonomies or assign elemental and psychological correspondences. Such systems are twentieth- or twenty-first-century inventions unless a specific modern author and publication can be demonstrated.

### 8.4 Political appropriation

She has been used positively as an emblem of Indigenous sovereignty, Peruvian ancestry, female political authority, and resistance to patriarchal historical narratives. The colonial sources also show the opposite political use: Sarmiento’s account made her violence part of the Toledan case that Inca rule originated in tyranny, while Guamán Poma made her the institutor of a demonically deceived dynasty.

No significant modern extremist organization centered on Mama Huaco was identified in the reviewed evidence.

### 8.5 Commercial use

Her name and imagery occur in tourism, hospitality, handicrafts, educational media, and commercial “Inca goddess” lists. Commercial descriptions frequently combine the golden staff, first maize, warrior identity, and Manco–Mama Ocllo legend into a seamless biography. That composite has no single colonial source.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 9.1 Historical founder versus mythic office

Some older scholarship asked whether Mama Huaco was a historical woman whose deeds were mythologized. The evidence cannot resolve that question. “Mama” is a title; the narrative has patterned groups of four, supernatural emergence, petrification, ritual geography, and impossible genealogies. The most defensible historical object is not an individual biography but a founder position remembered through ritual and competing descent traditions.

### 9.2 Aymara, Puquina, and Quechua linguistic strata

The apparent Aymara explanation of *waqu* has contributed to arguments that Inca elite terminology preserves Aymara or Puquina strata beneath later Quechua dominance. Bertonio’s *huaccu* supports the lexical possibility. It does not by itself establish Mama Huaco’s ethnicity, birthplace, or a specific migration from Tiwanaku.

### 9.3 Gender complementarity

Zuidema, Rostworowski, Silverblatt, and later scholars treat Mama Huaco as evidence that female authority in Inca thought could be military, ritual, and productive rather than simply consort-based. Critics caution that colonial chroniclers imposed European categories—queen, wife, witch, virago—upon Andean relations and that no single myth maps cleanly onto actual gender institutions.

### 9.4 Ritual murder and *callpa*

The lung episode may encode:

- psychological warfare;
- sacrificial display;
- divination through inflated lungs;
- public manifestation of *kallpa*, vigor or force;
- or colonial exaggeration of Inca cruelty.

The earliest account already contains killing and inflation, so the entire episode cannot be dismissed as Cabello’s late invention. Conversely, no source gives a technical ritual commentary sufficient to choose one interpretation with certainty.

A useful comparison of Betanzos, Sarmiento, Cabello, and Murúa appears in Luis Ramos Gómez’s study of Mama Huaco and Chañan Cori Coca, available through the [Revista Española de Antropología Americana PDF](https://storage.dtelab.com.ar/uploads/620b9bc507aa28280f0d93ce/620c03adacda7d61bc6f873a.pdf).

### 9.5 Sausero and Mama Sara

Molina says Sausero’s maize served Mama Huaco’s body; Cobo calls the field solar. Zuidema links the cycle to Mama Sara and reconstructs calendrical processions. Three possibilities remain open:

1. the field served overlapping solar and ancestral cults;
2. its institutional attribution changed;
3. Molina and Cobo drew on different informants or organizational schemes.

Identifying Mama Huaco directly with Mama Sara goes beyond the wording of either chronicler.

### 9.6 Source politics

- **Betanzos** had privileged Cusco elite access but was entangled in conquest-era factional politics.
- **Sarmiento** collected testimony under official conditions designed to delegitimize Inca sovereignty.
- **Molina** wrote for ecclesiastical purposes while preserving unparalleled ritual detail.
- **Murúa** was a Mercedarian friar whose illustrated histories developed through multiple manuscripts.
- **Garcilaso** wrote from exile, defended Inca civilization, and imposed a learned coherent narrative.
- **Guamán Poma** criticized both Spanish colonial abuse and Inca idolatry while advancing his own Andean-Christian political vision.
- **Cobo** synthesized earlier testimony decades after public royal cult had been dismantled.

Their disagreements cannot be repaired simply by selecting the most vivid details from each.

### 9.7 State of the evidence

Surviving key objects and repositories include:

- Guamán Poma autograph, GKS 2232 4º, Royal Danish Library, Copenhagen;
- Getty Murúa, MS Ludwig XIII 16, J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles;
- Galvin Murúa manuscript, privately owned but studied through facsimile and technical research;
- Molina copy, Biblioteca Nacional de España MS 3169;
- colonial editions and copies of Betanzos and Sarmiento in European and American collections.

No securely identified statue, mummy, temple inscription, theophoric name series, or pre-Hispanic painted image can presently be assigned to Mama Huaco.

### 9.8 Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- that “Mama Huaco” simply means “Mother Pot”;
- that every image of an armed Inca woman is her;
- that she was universally the wife of Ayar Cachi;
- that she and Mama Ocllo were always one goddess;
- that she was the exclusive goddess of maize throughout the empire;
- that the llama was her canonical sacred animal;
- that she led a documented female priestly order;
- that modern occult correspondences reproduce an ancient initiatory system;
- that the chronicles constitute eyewitness reports of the foundation of Cusco.
