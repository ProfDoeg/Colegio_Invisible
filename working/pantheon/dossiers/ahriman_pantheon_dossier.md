# Ahriman: Pantheon Dossier

*Filename: `ahriman_pantheon_dossier.md`*

## Scope and evidentiary conventions

“Ahriman” is the conventional English form of Middle Persian *Ahreman/Ahriman*, the Zoroastrian adversary of Ohrmazd. Its older Avestan antecedent is *Aŋra Mainiiu* (“destructive/hostile spirit”). The figure has no single, timeless identity. In the Old Avestan Gāthās the hostile spirit belongs to a pair of primordial “twin” or paired spirits distinguished by their choices. In the Younger Avesta he is chief among the *daēvas*, a counter-creator, tempter, and cosmic enemy. Middle Persian priestly books turn Ahreman into Ohrmazd’s ignorant, temporally limited antagonist in a systematic world-history of creation, mixture, and final separation. Zurvanite narratives make Ohrmazd and Ahriman brothers born from Time. Manichaean writers appropriate his name for powers in their own cosmology. Greek and Latin sources transmit *Areimanios/Arimanius*, sometimes inaccurately and, in Roman Mithraic dedications, in a setting whose relation to Iranian Ahriman remains disputed. Modern occultists, especially Rudolf Steiner, reinvent “Ahriman” as a spirit of materialism and technological-intellectual hardening.

Dating labels used below:

- **Early attested:** Old Avestan material, conventionally placed around the late second or early first millennium BCE, but transmitted orally and preserved only in medieval manuscripts.
- **Younger Avestan/canonical:** texts composed over the first millennium BCE, often impossible to date narrowly, and fixed in the Sasanian written canon.
- **External ancient testimony:** Greek, Latin, Armenian, Syriac, or Arabic evidence, which may report Iranian beliefs selectively or polemically.
- **Middle Persian systematization:** substantially Sasanian traditions preserved in books compiled or redacted chiefly in the ninth–tenth centuries CE.
- **Regional or sectarian variant:** a form not demonstrably normative throughout Zoroastrianism.
- **Late accretion:** medieval Persian, Parsi, or Rivāyat material substantially later than the Avesta.
- **Modern reinvention:** a modern literary, occult, political, or commercial construction, not evidence for ancient Iranian religion.

“Every interaction” in §4 means every discrete interaction recoverable from the principal surviving Avestan and Middle Persian dossiers consulted here. The Avesta is fragmentary, much of the Sasanian canon is lost, and no claim to exhaustive recovery of every occurrence in every manuscript can be made.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orienting identification

Ahriman is not straightforwardly a “god of evil” who received ordinary Zoroastrian worship. He is the personal and cosmic agent of destructive choice, falsehood, death, disease, pollution, predation, and disorder. In Zoroastrian classifications he is normally a *mainiiu/mēnōg*, “spirit,” and in the Younger Avesta also the preeminent *daēva*, a hostile supernatural being. Middle Persian theology calls him the *gannāg mēnōg*, “foul/stinking/destructive spirit,” and makes him head of the demonic counter-creation. He is real and effective during the mixed condition of the present world, but ignorant, limited, and ultimately defeasible. He is not the equal of Ohrmazd in knowledge or final power.

This distinction is crucial. Modern summaries often call Zoroastrianism a system of two coequal gods. The surviving priestly cosmologies instead distinguish Ohrmazd’s omniscience and enduring creation from Ahriman’s “after-knowledge,” blindness, destructiveness, and finite career. Whether the earliest Gāthic doctrine already opposed Ahura Mazdā directly to Angra Mainyu, or paired Angra Mainyu with Spənta Mainyu beneath or within Ahura Mazdā’s theology, remains one of the central interpretive disputes.

### Names and scripts

| Period/language | Form | Meaning and evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Old/Younger Avestan | *Aŋra Mainiiu*, conventionally *Angra Mainyu*; Avestan script 𐬀𐬢𐬭𐬀 𐬨𐬀𐬌𐬥𐬌𐬌𐬎 | Usually “destructive,” “hostile,” “constricting,” or “malign spirit.” The exact semantic history of *aŋra-* is debated; “evil spirit” conveys function but not the literal morphology with certainty. |
| Avestan variant description | *Aka Mainiiu* | “Evil spirit,” notably in Yasna 30.3 and 32.5. Whether every occurrence is a proper name is disputed. |
| Younger Avestan title | *daēvanąm daēvō* | “Daēva of daēvas,” Vendīdād 19.1, 43–44. This establishes leadership or preeminence, not fatherhood of all demons. |
| Middle Persian, Book Pahlavi | *Ahreman*, *Ahriman*, *Ahrēmen*; heterographic spellings vary | Regular Middle Iranian development of the Avestan name. “Ahriman” is the familiar New Persian and European form. |
| Middle Persian epithet | *gannāg mēnōg* | “Foul/stinking spirit,” also translated “destructive spirit.” A standard appellation in the *Bundahišn* and related works. |
| Middle Persian description | *pettyārag/patyārag* | “Adversary,” “counter-agent,” or “antagonist,” especially in accounts of his assault upon creation. |
| Greek | Ἀρειμάνιος, *Areimánios*; also *Ariemanios* | Used in Greek reports of Iranian religion, notably Aristotle as quoted by Diogenes Laertius and Plutarch. |
| Latin | *Arimanius*, *Areimanius* | Found in several Roman-period dedications associated archaeologically with Mithraic contexts. The dedications prove a cult-title or divine name, but not that Roman initiates reproduced Zoroastrian theology. |
| Armenian | commonly rendered *Ahrmn/Ahriman* in translation | Eznik of Kołb reports Zurvanite stories in the fifth century CE. His purpose is Christian refutation, not neutral ethnography. |
| Manichaean Middle Persian | *Ahrmēn* | Name applied within Manichaean cosmological vocabulary, where it no longer maps exactly onto Zoroastrian Ahreman. |
| Sogdian Manichaean | *Šimnu* | A different name used for the evil power; its conceptual equivalence to Ahriman is contextual rather than etymological. |
| New Persian | اهریمن, *Ahriman/Ehriman* | Literary and religious continuation, often generalized to “demon,” “devil,” or personified evil. |

The older derivation of *Aŋra Mainiiu* as simply “Evil Spirit” remains serviceable but conceals a philological distinction: *aka-* is the direct Avestan adjective “bad/evil,” whereas *aŋra-* probably conveys damaging, constricting, or destructive hostility. “Destructive Spirit” is therefore preferable when the distinction matters.

### Classification through time

1. **Old Avestan:** one of two primordial spirits differentiated by choice. Yasna 45.2 is the sole Gāthic passage using the full name *Aŋra Mainiiu*. Yasna 30.3 speaks instead of the better and worse of two paired spirits.

2. **Younger Avestan:** cosmic adversary, counter-creator, tempter of Zarathustra, chief *daēva*, inhabitant of darkness and the demonic north.

3. **Classical Greek accounts:** opponent of Oromazes/Oromasdes, sometimes treated as a god responsible for destructive natural and social forces.

4. **Middle Persian orthodoxy:** independent, uncreated antagonist in darkness, ignorant until he sees Ohrmazd’s light, architect of a demonic counter-creation, destined to lose all effective existence.

5. **Zurvanite accounts:** son of Zurvan and brother of Ohrmazd, born from Zurvan’s doubt and temporarily granted sovereignty.

6. **Manichaeism:** Iranian name applied to a ruler or principle within a distinct myth of Light and Darkness.

7. **Islamic-period Persian literature:** devil-like emblem of evil, often rhetorically assimilated to *Eblīs* without becoming historically identical to the Qurʾānic figure.

8. **Anthroposophy:** an extra-Zoroastrian being opposed to Lucifer, representing materialistic fixation and intellectual hardening.

### Identifications and non-identifications

- **Oromazes versus Areimanios:** Aristotle’s testimony, preserved by Diogenes Laertius, shows that by the fourth century BCE Greek observers understood Persian theology as an opposition between Oromazes and Areimanios. It is evidence for reception and perhaps for a developed Iranian opposition, not a verbatim Iranian creed.

- **Hades:** Plutarch reports associations between Areimanios and Hades or the underworld. This is Greek interpretive framing; no Avestan equation “Ahriman = Hades” exists.

- **Typhon:** ancient comparativists could place Iranian dualism beside Egyptian Osiris–Typhon opposition. This is analogy, not Iranian cultic identity.

- **Satan/Eblīs:** Christian, Muslim, and modern writers routinely translate Ahriman as “Satan.” Functional parallels exist, but their origins, metaphysics, and eschatological careers differ. Ahriman is not a fallen angel in the surviving Avesta or orthodox Pahlavi cosmology.

- **Roman Mithraic Arimanius:** Latin dedications to *Deus Arimanius* are real. Identifying the Mithraic lion-headed figure with Arimanius is not secure: the name does not unambiguously label the surviving lion-headed images, and the York inscription is fragmentary. The connection is a scholarly hypothesis, not an iconographic fact.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The earliest recoverable evidence

The earliest linguistic stratum is Old Avestan, especially the Gāthās embedded in Yasna 28–34, 43–51, and 53. Their absolute date is disputed. Linguistic comparison with Vedic Sanskrit and the social world presupposed by the poems makes a date around 1200–1000 BCE common in current scholarship, although proposals range earlier and later. “Around 1000 BCE” is a working estimate, not an externally anchored year.

The full name occurs securely in **Yasna 45.2**. The “more bounteous” spirit declares that he and the hostile spirit do not agree in thought, teaching, intention, belief, speech, action, self, or soul. Yasna 30.3–6 describes two primordial spirits, often understood as twins, whose choices establish the opposition between better and worse existence. Yasna 32.5 addresses the evil spirit with the *daēvas*. These passages establish an ethical-cosmic antagonist but do not yet supply the elaborate biography of the *Bundahišn*.

No securely identified Achaemenid inscription names Angra Mainyu. Old Persian royal inscriptions name Auramazdā and, later, other gods; the absence of Angra Mainyu prevents using those inscriptions as direct attestations. It does not prove that Achaemenid Persians did not know the concept.

The physical evidence is much later than the language. The Avesta was transmitted orally before being written in the highly differentiated Avestan alphabet under the Sasanians, probably in the fourth century CE. All surviving manuscripts descend from a medieval archetype. K7a, sometimes dated 1268 or 1288, is often described as the oldest surviving Avestan manuscript; J2 and related securely dated codices belong to 1323. Thus “a text composed around 1000 BCE” and “a manuscript copied in the thirteenth or fourteenth century CE” must never be conflated. The transmission problem is surveyed in the [Encyclopaedia Iranica article on the Avesta](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/avesta-holy-book/) and its account of the [Copenhagen codices](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/codices-hafnienses/).

### Principal Avestan corpus

#### Gāthās and Yasna Haptaŋhāiti

- **Language:** Old Avestan.
- **Composition:** commonly late second/early first millennium BCE; exact date and place unproved.
- **Surviving manuscripts:** medieval, principally from the thirteenth century onward.
- **Role:** Yasna 30 supplies the paired-spirit doctrine; 45.2 names Angra Mainyu; 32.5 associates the evil spirit and *daēvas*. Yasna 44.5 attributes light and darkness to Ahura Mazdā, a passage difficult to reconcile mechanically with later cosmogony.
- **Historical caution:** the claim that Angra Mainyu is literally Ahura Mazdā’s son depends on combining “twins” in Yasna 30 with Ahura Mazdā’s paternity of the beneficial spirit in Yasna 47. Later orthodoxy rejected any doctrine making Ohrmazd and Ahriman brothers. Jacques Duchesne-Guillemin defended the sonship reading, but it is an interpretation, not an uncontested sentence in the text.

#### Younger Yasna

- **Language:** Younger Avestan with embedded older material.
- **Composition:** first millennium BCE in layers.
- **Role:** Yasna 9.8 makes Angra Mainyu creator of Aži Dahāka; Yasna 19.15 recounts Ahura Mazdā’s recitation of the Ahuna Vairya prayer, which stuns or drives off the enemy; Yasna 57.17 associates the two spirits with opposed creative activity.

#### Yašts

- **Language:** Younger Avestan, with archaic poetic material.
- **Composition:** layered; much of the major Yašt material is often placed in the first half of the first millennium BCE, but redaction is later.
- **Key passages:**
  - **Tištar Yašt 8.44:** Angra Mainyu cannot destroy Tištrya.
  - **Mihr Yašt 10.97, 134:** he fears Mithra’s mace.
  - **Fravašī Yašt 13.12–13, 71, 77–78:** his assault upon creation is resisted by the Fravašis.
  - **Ard Yašt 17.19:** he flees when Zarathustra is born.
  - **Zamyād Yašt 19.46–54:** the two spirits and their allies contend for the *xᵛarənah*, the royal/divine Glory.
  - **Zamyād Yašt 19.95–96:** final victory over the evil spirit.
  - **Yasht 15.12–13:** Taxma Urupi rides Angra Mainyu as a horse for thirty years.

The Yašts’ transmission and disputed chronology are summarized in the [Iranica survey](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yashts/).

#### Vidēvdād/Vendīdād

- **Language:** Younger Avestan.
- **Composition:** heterogeneous and generally later than the oldest Yašts; precise dates cannot be demonstrated.
- **Name:** *Vī-daēva-dāta*, approximately “law given against the daēvas.”
- **Key passages:**
  - **Chapter 1:** Ahura Mazdā creates sixteen excellent lands; Angra Mainyu answers each with a counter-creation—winter, disease, pests, social evils, or environmental affliction.
  - **Chapter 2:** he brings the fatal winter against Yima’s world.
  - **Chapter 19:** he inhabits the north and darkness, tempts Zarathustra to renounce the religion in return for worldly sovereignty, and sends demons against him. Zarathustra defeats them through sacred speech.
  - **19.1, 43–44:** “daēva of daēvas.”
- **Function:** ritual purity and demon-repulsion rather than worship of Ahriman. The text’s liturgical use makes its recitation an act against demonic pollution.

#### Aogəmadaēca and liturgical fragments

The *Aogəmadaēca*, a composite text on death incorporating Avestan citations and Middle Persian explanation, places Angra Mainyu in darkness. Its textual history is complex and later than the oldest Avestan corpus.

### Classical external witnesses

#### Aristotle/Eudoxus traditions

Diogenes Laertius 1.6, transmitting a report attributed to Aristotle, opposes Oromazdes and Ariemanios. Although Diogenes wrote in the third century CE, his cited source is fourth-century BCE. The report is the earliest externally dated testimony to an Ohrmazd–Ahriman opposition.

Eudemus of Rhodes, also fourth century BCE, reportedly knew accounts in which Time or Space preceded Oromazes and Ahriman. Because the material survives indirectly, it is evidence for an early genealogy of ideas, not a fully recoverable Zurvanite scripture.

#### Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* 46–47

- **Language/date:** Greek, late first or early second century CE.
- **Content:** Zoroaster is said to teach two opposed makers, Oromazes and Areimanios. Oromazes creates six beneficent gods; Areimanios creates six contrary beings. Magi make offerings connected with each side. A final time will come when pestilence- and famine-producing Areimanios is annihilated, the earth becomes level, and humanity becomes unified.
- **Value:** an important non-Iranian account containing recognizably Iranian eschatology.
- **Limitations:** Plutarch arranges Persian material for a Greek philosophical argument and probably misunderstood or reinterpreted ritual details. His account of pounding a blood-colored plant in a dark place and invoking Hades and Darkness must not automatically be called “Ahriman worship.” The text is accessible in the [Loeb-derived online edition](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/plutarch/moralia/isis_and_osiris%2A/c.html).

### Middle Persian Zoroastrian corpus

#### *Bundahišn*

- **Language:** Middle Persian in Book Pahlavi.
- **Compilation:** chiefly ninth century CE, drawing on Sasanian Zand and older traditions.
- **Manuscripts:** Indian recension in manuscripts of the fourteenth century and later; the fuller Iranian recension survives chiefly in late manuscripts, including one copied in 1597.
- **Role:** the fullest Ahreman cosmology. Ohrmazd exists in endless light; Ahreman in deepest darkness; the Void lies between. Ahreman initially knows nothing of Ohrmazd. After glimpsing the light he rushes upward, rejects peace, makes demonic creatures, accepts a limited contest, is stunned by the Ahunwar, invades creation, and mixes death and corruption into it. The final renovation renders him powerless.
- **Critical issue:** the work is a late compilation, not a verbatim Sasanian book, though much of its Zand material is demonstrably older. Manuscript history and recensions are detailed in the [Iranica article](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bundahisn-primal-creation/); an older complete translation is available through the [Greater Bundahišn text](https://www.avesta.org/mp/grb1.htm).

#### *Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram* (“Selections of Zādspram”)

- **Date:** late ninth century CE.
- **Role:** parallel cosmology, Ahreman’s penetration of the sky, attacks upon the primordial creatures, and final defeat. It sometimes preserves arrangements different from the *Bundahišn* and is therefore an independent witness to priestly systematization, not merely a summary.

#### *Dēnkard*

- **Date:** ninth–tenth centuries CE, with earlier strata.
- **Role:** theological defense, summaries of lost Avestan books, demonology, arguments against doctrines that compromise Ohrmazd’s goodness or Ahreman’s responsibility.
- **Caution:** its descriptions of the lost twenty-one *nasks* cannot be treated as surviving Avestan quotations unless explicitly marked as such.

#### *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*

- **Date:** ninth century CE; answers by the priest Manuščihr.
- **Role:** explains the origin and function of evil, the mixture, punishment, and final removal of Ahreman’s effects.

#### *Škand Gumānīg Wizār*

- **Date:** ninth century CE, by Mardānfarrox son of Ohrmazddād.
- **Role:** apologetic proof that a good and omniscient creator cannot be the source of evil; therefore Ahreman’s destructive principle must be distinct. It attacks Christianity, Judaism, Islam, and Manichaeism as well as rival explanations of evil.

#### *Mēnōg ī Xrad*

- **Date:** probably sixth-century material in a later redaction.
- **Role:** wisdom dialogue cataloguing demonic dangers and ethical resistance; Ahreman appears as head of forces defeated through wisdom and right action.

#### *Ardā Wīrāz Nāmag*

- **Date:** redaction uncertain, generally early Islamic-era Middle Persian from older traditions.
- **Role:** visionary journey through heaven and hell. Near the conclusion Wīrāz sees Ahreman in hell mocking or tormenting the wicked. This is a later visionary elaboration, not an Avestan scene.

#### Pahlavi Rivāyat and Persian Rivāyats

- **Dates:** Middle Persian compilations around the ninth–tenth centuries; Persian correspondence between Iranian and Indian Zoroastrians mainly fifteenth–eighteenth centuries.
- **Role:** supplementary cosmology, ritual rules, eschatology, demonology, and answers to communal questions.
- **Status:** important evidence for medieval Zoroastrian belief, not proof that every detail is ancient.

### Zurvanite external corpus

#### Eznik of Kołb, *Refutation of the Sects*

- **Language/date:** Classical Armenian, around the 440s CE.
- **Narrative:** Zurvan sacrifices for a son who will create the world. A moment of doubt conceives Ahriman, while the sacrifice conceives Ohrmazd. Zurvan promises kingship to the first-born; Ahriman tears his way out first and claims the promise. Zurvan grants him dominion for nine thousand years, after which Ohrmazd will rule.
- **Other material:** Ahriman displays creative ability, including producing the peacock in one polemical story, and gives obscene or transgressive instruction concerning generation.
- **Caution:** Eznik is a hostile Christian controversialist. His account probably preserves Iranian traditions, but its wording and selection serve refutation. An English retelling is available online in the [Refutation of the Sects](https://www.tertullian.org/rpearse/scanned/yeznik_refutation.htm); a French version preserves fuller detail in [Réfutation, Book II](https://remacle.org/bloodwolf/eglise/eznik/refutation2.htm).

#### Theodore bar Kōnay, *Book of Scholia*

- **Language/date:** Syriac, 791–792 CE.
- **Role:** transmits another Zurvanite birth narrative and Manichaean cosmogonic material.
- **Status:** late external witness; useful for comparison, not proof of a single organized “Zurvanist church.”

Modern scholarship increasingly treats “Zurvanism” cautiously. The evidence may represent a family of exegetical solutions and regional teachings rather than a neatly bounded denomination. The problem is reviewed in the [Iranica article on Zurvanism](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/zurvanism/).

### Manichaean corpus

Mani’s *Šābuhragān*, composed in Middle Persian for Šāpūr I in the third century CE, and later Iranian Manichaean fragments employ Zoroastrian vocabulary to translate a different cosmology. In some Middle Persian fragments *Ahrmēn* names the evil ruler; elsewhere Āz, the demon of desire, has a central generative role. A fragment declares that Āz, Ahrmēn, demons, and female demonic beings will be imprisoned forever. In Sogdian the corresponding power is called Šimnu.

The Iranian terminology was adaptive and missionary. It does not demonstrate that Zoroastrians adopted the Manichaean myth in which human bodies derive from evil matter while light-substance is trapped within them. The textual setting is described in the articles on the [Šābuhragān](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sabuhragan/) and [Manichaean cosmology](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/cosmogony-iii/).

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

Ahriman’s sphere is not a morally neutral portfolio such as sea, craft, or kingship. It is the active spoiling of Ohrmazd’s good creation:

- falsehood and bad choice;
- death and mortality;
- disease and bodily deformity;
- decay, stench, corpse pollution, and contamination;
- destructive animals and organisms;
- drought, excessive cold, and hostile weather;
- violence, wrath, predation, and social disorder;
- sorcery and false religion;
- demonic invasion of thought;
- obstruction of water, vegetation, cattle, fertility, and ritual;
- political tyranny when hostile rulers are cosmologically demonized.

This catalogue grew over time. The Gāthic core is ethical and eschatological: wrong thought, speech, action, and allegiance. The Younger Avesta increasingly assigns physical and geographical counter-creations to Angra Mainyu. Pahlavi texts make the system encyclopedic, pairing almost every good creation with a destructive opponent.

### Powers and limitations

**Powers attested in the Younger Avesta and Pahlavi books:**

- counter-creation of plagues, pests, harmful creatures, climatic afflictions, and moral vices;
- creation or commissioning of Aži Dahāka;
- mustering of *daēvas*;
- temptation through promises of sovereignty;
- penetration and contamination of the material world;
- causing death to the primordial bull and primordial human;
- obscuring celestial light through demonic planets;
- attacking waters, plants, fire, cattle, and human bodies.

**Limitations:**

- he lacks Ohrmazd’s foreknowledge;
- sacred speech can stun, repel, or defeat him;
- he cannot destroy Tištrya or the Fravašis;
- he fears Mithra’s weapon;
- his creatures depend parasitically upon the good creation they corrupt;
- he cannot make an enduring, ordered world;
- the duration of mixture is bounded;
- the final renovation makes further aggression impossible.

Some Pahlavi theologians sharpened the asymmetry by saying Ahreman “is not”—not necessarily absolute nonexistence, since he acts in narrative, but lack of enduring, truthful, properly created being. This metaphysical language should not erase the books’ equally concrete depiction of him as an invader.

### Physical form

The Avesta gives no stable portrait. Ahriman is primarily a spirit, direction, voice, commander, or adversarial agency. Metamorphosis is possible: in the Taxma Urupi episode he becomes or is made into a horse and is ridden.

Middle Persian literature supplies grotesque and demonic imagery but no single canonical anatomy. Darkness, stench, lizard-like or serpentine associations, and downward orientation belong to his symbolic environment.

### Sasanian image at Naqš-e Rostam

The most important probable depiction is the investiture relief of Ardašīr I at Naqš-e Rostam, carved around 224–242 CE. Ardašīr and a divine figure identified by inscription as Ohrmazd face each other on horseback and exchange a diadem. Beneath Ardašīr’s horse lies the defeated Arsacid Ardawān; beneath Ohrmazd’s horse lies a corresponding enemy, conventionally identified as Ahriman. The latter has serpentine legs and a headdress or hair formed by reptiles.

The figure is not individually labeled “Ahriman.” Identification follows the visual parallel: the earthly king defeats his dynastic enemy as Ohrmazd defeats the cosmic enemy. It is therefore strong but inferential. The relief turns dualist cosmology into royal propaganda, presenting Sasanian victory as the terrestrial analogue of Ohrmazd’s triumph. The monument and conventional identification are discussed in [Ardašīr I’s rock reliefs](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/ardasir-ii/) and the survey of [Sasanian art](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/art-in-iran-v-sasanian/).

### Roman Arimanius and the lion-headed figure

Latin dedications include forms such as *Deo Arimanio*. Examples catalogued in the *Corpus Inscriptionum et Monumentorum Religionis Mithriacae* include finds from Ostia, Rome, Carnuntum, and possibly York. These are genuine votive acts in Roman religious contexts.

Older scholarship often identified the enigmatic Mithraic lion-headed, snake-entwined figure with Arimanius. That conclusion is insecure:

- most *Arimanius* inscriptions do not accompany a surviving lion-headed image;
- the York inscription is damaged;
- the lion-headed being has also been identified as Aion, Chronos, a cosmic guardian, or another power;
- worshipful dedication to a Roman *deus Arimanius* need not reproduce the Iranian moral status of Ahriman.

The York evidence and catalogue references are assembled in the [CIMRM/RIB record](https://mithras.tertullian.org/display.php?page=cimrm833). The safe formulation is: Roman Mithraic communities sometimes invoked a god named Arimanius; his precise iconography and doctrinal relationship to Zoroastrian Ahriman are unresolved.

### Symbols and associations

No ancient Zoroastrian source gives Ahriman a fixed color, weekday, number, planet, sacred animal, temple emblem, or approved cult statue.

Documented associations include:

- **direction:** north, especially the demonic north in Vendīdād 19;
- **place:** deepest or endless darkness, the abyss, and later hell;
- **substances:** stench, decay, pollution, poison;
- **animals:** snakes, lizards, scorpions, frogs, flies, wolves, and other *xrafstar*, but classification differs by text and ecology;
- **celestial forces:** in Pahlavi astrology the wandering planets are assigned to Ahreman’s side, while zodiacal constellations serve Ohrmazd;
- **time:** his effective sovereignty is finite—nine thousand years in one Zurvanite scheme, or a bounded portion of the twelve-thousand-year cosmic drama in priestly cosmology;
- **number:** three-thousand-year epochs structure some Pahlavi histories, but “3” is not his exclusive sacred number.

Modern lists that give Ahriman black candles, Saturday, particular crystals, sigils, tarot correspondences, or a fixed planetary ruler are modern occult constructions unless they cite a demonstrable historical source.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing origin models

| Model | Source and date | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| Paired primordial spirits | Yasna 30.3–6; Old Avestan | Better and worse spirits are “twins” or paired counterparts distinguished through choice. Their precise relation to Ahura Mazdā is disputed. |
| Possible sons of Ahura Mazdā | Reconstruction combining Yasna 30 and 47 | Some scholars infer that both spirits are sons of Ahura Mazdā because one is called his offspring and they are twins. This is not later orthodoxy and is not uncontested. |
| Independent antagonist | Younger Avesta; systematic in the *Bundahišn* | Angra Mainyu/Ahreman exists separately in darkness. No parent or creator is named. |
| Son of Zurvan | Eznik, Theodore bar Kōnay, related external reports; fifth–eighth centuries CE | Ahriman is the premature or dark son generated by Zurvan’s doubt, brother of Ohrmazd, and temporary ruler. |
| Manichaean ruler of Darkness | Third-century and later Manichaean sources | Iranian name absorbed into a different genealogy of evil matter and demonic rulers. |

Orthodox Middle Persian theology generally rejects making Ohrmazd responsible for Ahreman. The Zurvanite brotherhood model probably became objectionable precisely because it subordinated both spirits to a common origin.

### Consort, offspring, servants, and hierarchy

Ahriman has no securely attested consort or biological children in the Old Avesta. The Younger Avesta calls him chief of the *daēvas* but does not generally call every demon his child. Yasna 32.3 instead associates the *daēvas* with Evil Mind.

Middle Persian texts organize a counter-hierarchy parallel to Ohrmazd’s creations. Major archdemons include:

- Akōman, Evil Mind, opponent of Vohuman;
- Indar, opponent of Ardawahisht;
- Sāwul/Saurva, opponent of Shahrewar;
- Nāŋhaiθ/Nāŋhaithya, opponent of Spandarmad;
- Tauriz and Zairiz, opponents of Hordad and Amurdad;
- Ēšm/Aēšma, Wrath;
- Āz, Greed or devouring desire;
- Jeh/Jahī, the Whore or female demon of pollution;
- Astwihād, the demon of death;
- Apōš, opponent of the rain-star Tištar;
- Bušāsp, sloth and excessive sleep;
- Aži Dahāka, monstrous tyrant and destroyer.

These are best called creations, allies, emanations, or subordinates according to the individual text—not uniformly biological offspring.

### Discrete divine-being interactions

#### Ahura Mazdā/Ohrmazd

- **Interaction:** cosmic opposition; Ahura Mazdā’s good creations are attacked by Angra Mainyu’s counter-creations.
- **Sources:** Vendīdād 1; Yasna 19; *Bundahišn* 1–5.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical; later systematic elaboration.
- **Conflict:** in the Gāthās the formal opposite of Angra Mainyu is more directly Spənta Mainyu; by classical and Pahlavi times Ohrmazd himself occupies that position.

#### Spənta Mainyu

- **Interaction:** the better spirit declares absolute opposition to Angra Mainyu in thought, word, action, self, and soul.
- **Source:** Yasna 45.2; compare 30.3–6.
- **Tier:** earliest attested.
- **Development:** later theology often identifies Spənta Mainyu so fully with Ohrmazd that the original pairing recedes.

#### Vohu Manah/Vohuman

- **Interaction:** good thought and the ordered creation oppose Ahrimanic deception; Pahlavi cosmology assigns Akōman as Vohuman’s demonic counterforce.
- **Sources:** Gāthic ethical system; *Bundahišn* demon lists.
- **Tier:** early conceptual opposition; later personified systematization.

#### Aša Vahišta/Ardawahisht

- **Interaction:** Ahriman breaks into the creation of Aša; Ardawahisht is paired against Indar in Pahlavi demonology.
- **Source:** Yašt 13.77; *Bundahišn*.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical; Middle Persian systematic.

#### Xšaθra Vairya/Shahrewar

- **Interaction:** no extended personal encounter survives; his good sovereignty and metallic creation are opposed by Ahrimanic rule and Sāwul.
- **Source:** Pahlavi creation and demon lists.
- **Tier:** later priestly systematization.

#### Spənta Ārmaiti/Spandarmad

- **Interaction:** Ahreman attacks the earth under Spandarmad’s protection; Nāŋhaiθ is assigned as her counter-demon.
- **Source:** *Bundahišn*.
- **Tier:** Middle Persian systematization.

#### Haurvatāt/Hordad and Amərətāt/Amurdad

- **Interaction:** their waters and plants are obstructed or polluted by Ahriman’s forces; Tauriz and Zairiz are their assigned opponents.
- **Sources:** Younger Avestan theology; Pahlavi demon lists.
- **Tier:** canonical conceptual conflict; systematic pairing is later.

#### Mithra/Mihr

- **Interaction:** Angra Mainyu recoils from or fears Mithra’s heavy mace.
- **Source:** Mihr Yašt 10.97, 134.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical.

#### Tištrya/Tištar

- **Interaction:** Angra Mainyu attempts but fails to destroy Tištrya; in later accounts his demon Apōš combats the rain-star.
- **Sources:** Tištar Yašt 8.44; *Bundahišn*.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical, with Pahlavi elaboration.

#### The Fravašis

- **Interaction:** the guardian spirits defend waters, plants, creation, and Zarathustra’s mission against Angra Mainyu.
- **Source:** Fravašī Yašt 13.12–13, 71, 77–78.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical.

#### Ātar/Fire

- **Interaction:** Fire, son or creation of Ahura Mazdā, contests Aži Dahāka—Angra Mainyu’s creature—for the unattainable Glory.
- **Source:** Zamyād Yašt 19.46–54.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical.
- **Caution:** the immediate duel is Fire versus Aži Dahāka; Ahriman directs the demonic side rather than necessarily fighting Fire hand-to-hand.

#### Xᵛarənah, the Glory

- **Interaction:** Spənta Mainyu and Angra Mainyu seek possession of the Glory and send their respective allies after it.
- **Source:** Zamyād Yašt 19.46 onward.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical.
- **Classification:** the Glory is a divine power or hypostasis, not merely an object.

#### Arədvī Sūrā Anāhitā

- **Interaction:** no direct narrative meeting with Ahriman is securely attested in the surviving principal corpus. Her waters belong to the good creation that demonic forces attempt to obstruct.
- **Tier:** absence of discrete interaction noted.

#### Vāyu/Wāy

- **Interaction:** later cosmology distinguishes beneficent and harmful domains of Wind; Wāy’s ambiguous or intermediary character has prompted comparative theories about pre-Zoroastrian roots of the paired spirits.
- **Source:** Younger Avesta and Pahlavi cosmology.
- **Tier:** attested association plus modern reconstruction; no secure origin myth deriving Ahriman from Vāyu.

#### Sraoša/Srōš

- **Interaction:** Sraoša protects ritual and souls against the demonic host; the recitation of sacred words defeats Ahriman’s attacks.
- **Sources:** Yasna 57; Vendīdād 19; Pahlavi eschatology.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical; later elaboration.

#### Rašnu and Mihr

- **Interaction:** as judges of souls they belong to the order that prevents Ahreman’s definitive possession of the dead.
- **Sources:** Younger Avestan and Pahlavi judgment traditions.
- **Tier:** canonical/later systematic.
- **Caution:** no single dramatic courtroom confrontation with Ahriman is required by the texts.

#### Aži Dahāka

- **Interaction:** Angra Mainyu creates or commissions the three-headed dragon/tyrant as a weapon against the ordered world.
- **Source:** Yasna 9.8; developed in later epic tradition.
- **Tier:** Younger Avestan canonical.

#### Akōman

- **Interaction:** chief demonic intellect and agent of evil thought; in later accounts attempts to approach the infant Zarathustra but is repelled by Vohuman.
- **Sources:** Pahlavi Zarathustra legends and demonologies.
- **Tier:** late canonical elaboration.

#### Indar, Sāwul, Nāŋhaiθ, Tauriz, and Zairiz

- **Interaction:** Ahriman’s principal archdemons, deployed as counterparts of the Amahraspands.
- **Source:** *Bundahišn* and related Middle Persian lists.
- **Tier:** priestly systematization based partly on Younger Avestan demon names.

#### Ēšm/Aēšma

- **Interaction:** Ahriman’s force of wrath and uncontrolled violence; fights the order guarded by Srōš.
- **Sources:** Younger Avesta and Middle Persian demonology.
- **Tier:** canonical and later systematic.

#### Āz

- **Interaction:** devouring greed acts as Ahriman’s ally; in Manichaean Iranian texts Āz may assume a role more generative and cosmologically central than in Zoroastrianism.
- **Sources:** Pahlavi books; Manichaean Middle Persian fragments.
- **Tier:** Zoroastrian systematization; Manichaean syncretistic transformation.

#### Jeh/Jahī

- **Interaction:** when Ahriman lies stunned, Jeh revives and encourages him by promising injury to the primal man and bull; he kisses her, after which menstruation or pollution is produced in some versions.
- **Sources:** Iranian *Bundahišn* 3 and related Pahlavi material.
- **Tier:** Middle Persian canonical elaboration.
- **Note:** this gendered pollution myth is explicit and should not be retrojected into the Gāthās.

#### Astwihād

- **Interaction:** Ahriman’s death-demon casts mortality upon Gayōmard.
- **Source:** *Bundahišn*.
- **Tier:** Middle Persian systematization.

#### Apōš

- **Interaction:** Ahrimanic drought demon sent against Tištar.
- **Sources:** Tištar traditions and *Bundahišn*.
- **Tier:** canonical/later elaboration.

#### Zurvan

- **Interaction:** Zurvan generates Ahriman through doubt, is deceived or compelled by his first-born claim, and grants him finite kingship.
- **Sources:** Eznik, Theodore bar Kōnay, and related external testimonies.
- **Tier:** regional/sectarian variant reported externally.
- **Conflict:** absent from orthodox Pahlavi cosmogony.

#### Manichaean Father of Greatness and powers of Light

- **Interaction:** Ahrmēn or the powers represented by his Iranian name belong to Darkness opposing the Manichaean Light-realm.
- **Sources:** Iranian Manichaean fragments and *Šābuhragān* traditions.
- **Tier:** syncretistic identification, not Zoroastrian canon.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 The primordial choice of the two spirits

**Earliest form:** Yasna 30.3–6 and 45.2.

Two primordial spirits confront one another. They are alike in being paired but opposite in thought, word, action, and choice. The better chooses truth or right order; the worse chooses the worst acts. Humans and divine beings must likewise choose. Death and the “worst existence” follow the evil side; best consciousness and final restoration belong to the truthful.

The text does not narrate a birth, battle, or fall from heaven. Evil is dramatized as choice. Whether the spirits existed within Ahura Mazdā, were created by him, or were independent is not settled by a single unambiguous line.

**Development:** Younger Avestan and Middle Persian texts move from this compressed ethical myth toward a spatial cosmogony. Ahriman ceases to be merely the “worse chooser” and becomes a ruler in darkness with armies and counter-creations. The earlier freedom of choice remains visible in some later and Zurvanite traditions but is subordinated to his fixed destructive nature.

### 5.2 The counter-creation of the sixteen lands

**Source:** Vendīdād 1.

Ahura Mazdā successively makes excellent lands. After each creation Angra Mainyu produces a corresponding affliction: severe winter, disease, pests, hostile peoples, burial practices, sexual or bodily disorders, sorcery, or other evils. The geography combines eastern Iranian landscapes with mythic moral topography.

The chapter is not a neutral atlas. It explains why even good lands contain affliction and incorporates regional judgments into cosmic history. Some counter-creations reflect priestly purity categories rather than universally held Iranian attitudes. Older translations also use stigmatizing medical language; such renderings should be checked against the Avestan.

**Development:** The *Bundahišn* universalizes this structure. Every major Ohrmazdian creation—sky, water, earth, plants, cattle, humanity, fire—receives a demonic assault or counterpart.

### 5.3 The pact and bounded cosmic war

**Fullest source:** *Bundahišn* 1–3; parallels in Zādspram.

Ohrmazd dwells above in endless light, omniscient and good. Ahreman exists below in endless darkness, characterized by destructive desire and “after-knowledge.” A void separates them. Ohrmazd knows Ahreman and the final outcome; Ahreman initially does not know Ohrmazd.

Ahreman rises, sees the light, and wishes to seize or destroy it. Ohrmazd offers peace and proposes that Ahreman praise the good creation. Ahreman interprets the offer as weakness and refuses. Ohrmazd then fixes a limited time for combat, knowing that limitation makes victory possible. Ahreman agrees because he lacks foreknowledge.

Ohrmazd recites the Ahunwar/Ahuna Vairya, revealing the outcome. Ahreman collapses into darkness for three thousand years. During this interval Ohrmazd completes spiritual and material creation. Ahreman later revives, breaches the sky, and begins the age of mixture.

**Variants:**

- Some schemes total nine thousand years; others twelve thousand.
- The allocation and order of three-thousand-year epochs differ.
- Zurvanite accounts explain the nine-thousand-year rule as Zurvan’s promise to Ahriman rather than a pact between the antagonists.
- The Avesta contains pieces of the Ahunwar victory but not this continuous narrative.

**Function:** the myth explains why an omniscient Ohrmazd permits evil temporarily: the material world is a deliberately bounded battlefield and trap in which evil can be exhausted.

### 5.4 The assault on creation

**Sources:** *Bundahišn* 3–5; Zādspram 2–4.

Ahreman and the demons pierce the sky. Darkness spreads through the cosmos. The waters become salty or disturbed; mountains erupt from the formerly level earth; plants wither; the primordial bull is poisoned or killed; Gayōmard, the primordial human, receives mortality. Fire is mixed with smoke. Harmful creatures multiply.

This is the decisive transition from *bundahišn*, creation, to *gumēzišn*, mixture. Yet Ahreman’s apparent success entraps him. The sky closes like a fortress, and the generative potential of the slain bull and man produces the later multiplicity of animals, plants, metals, and humans. Destruction is converted into further life.

**Historical layer:** this integrated seven-creation narrative is Middle Persian systematization drawing on scattered Avestan motifs. It should not be quoted as if it appeared whole in Zarathustra’s hymns.

### 5.5 Jeh awakens Ahriman

**Source:** Iranian *Bundahišn* 3.

After the Ahunwar leaves Ahreman incapacitated, his demons fail to restore him. Jeh, a female demon described with the polemical epithet “Whore,” promises to afflict the primordial man and bull and to corrupt the good creation. Her boast revives Ahreman. He rewards or embraces her; a kiss or gaze produces female pollution in some recensions.

This is a late priestly myth linking menstruation and female sexuality to demonic contamination. It is absent from the Gāthās and should be labeled Middle Persian gendered demonology, not a universal statement of all Zoroastrian practice.

### 5.6 The temptation of Zarathustra

**Source:** Vendīdād 19.

Angra Mainyu approaches Zarathustra and promises him worldly sovereignty if he abandons the Mazda-worshipping religion, invoking the precedent of an earlier ruler. Zarathustra refuses. Angra Mainyu unleashes demons, but Zarathustra recites sacred formulas and defeats or disperses them. The hostile beings plunge back toward darkness.

The episode resembles a religious founder’s temptation narrative, but direct borrowing from later Christian temptation stories cannot be assumed: the dating and transmission of Vendīdād 19 are too uncertain. Conversely, claims that the Gospel temptation necessarily derives from this chapter exceed the evidence.

### 5.7 Zarathustra’s birth drives back evil

**Sources:** Ard Yašt 17.19 and later Pahlavi Zarathustra literature.

At Zarathustra’s birth, Angra Mainyu and his demons flee or are terrified because the future prophet will proclaim the religion that destroys their power. Pahlavi works such as the *Dēnkard* and *Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram* enlarge this into prenatal and childhood attacks: demons attempt to kill the infant but fail through divine protection.

The compact Younger Avestan motif is early canonical; the detailed miracle cycle is later hagiographic elaboration.

### 5.8 Taxma Urupi rides Ahriman

**Sources:** Yasht 15.12–13 and 19.28–29; expanded in Middle Persian and Persian tradition.

Taxma Urupi, corresponding to later Tahmuras, subdues Angra Mainyu and rides him as a horse around the earth for thirty years. In the later *Bundahišn* and epic tradition, Ahriman discovers that Tahmuras fears him, throws him, and kills him after information is extracted through deception or through the king’s wife.

The Avestan core is the humiliating mastery of the evil spirit by a heroic king. The domestic deception and death narrative are later. Ferdowsi’s *Shāhnāma* preserves a transformed Tahmuras cycle in which the king binds demons and receives writing from them, but it is not a verbatim retelling of the Avestan Ahriman-ride.

### 5.9 The struggle for the Glory

**Source:** Zamyād Yašt 19.46–54.

Spənta Mainyu and Angra Mainyu compete for the unattainable *xᵛarənah*. Their allies pursue it. Fire confronts Aži Dahāka; each threatens the other, and the Glory escapes into the cosmic waters. The episode integrates Ahriman with Iranian kingship ideology: legitimate Glory cannot be securely possessed by the demonic tyrant.

Later royal theology, especially Sasanian imagery, turns this cosmic opposition into a model for political victory.

### 5.10 The fatal winter

**Source:** Vendīdād 2.

Ahura Mazdā warns Yima of a devastating winter in which snow, flood, and cold will destroy ordinary life. Yima constructs the *vara*, an enclosure preserving exemplary humans, animals, and plants.

Angra Mainyu is associated with destructive winter in the broader Vendīdād scheme, although the chapter’s precise syntax does not make every feature a narrated personal act by him. Later summaries often overstate his direct role. The myth is an Iranian preservation narrative, not securely a borrowing from or source for any single Near Eastern flood story.

### 5.11 Final defeat and renovation

**Avestan core:** Yasna 30; Zamyād Yašt 19.89–96; scattered eschatological passages.

**Middle Persian synthesis:** *Bundahišn* 34, Zādspram, *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*, Pahlavi Rivāyat.

The dead rise. Humans undergo judgment and purification. Molten metal flows over the earth; for the righteous it feels like warm milk, while it burns away impurity. The final savior and helpers complete the renovation. Demons are defeated by their good counterparts. Ahriman and Āz retreat or are rendered powerless; hell is purified and reintegrated into the perfected world. Death, hunger, aging, and falsehood cease.

Sources vary over whether Ahriman is annihilated, driven back into darkness, stripped of power, or reduced to nonbeing. Plutarch says Areimanios will be annihilated and disappear. Some Pahlavi formulations emphasize expulsion through the aperture by which he entered. Theological interpretation often prefers total incapacity over an ordinary creature’s “death.”

A concise account of the evidence is given in the [Iranica article on *frašō.kərəti*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/frasokrti/).

### 5.12 Zurvan’s twin sons

**Sources:** Eznik of Kołb, fifth century; Theodore bar Kōnay, eighth century; related Syriac and Armenian witnesses.

Zurvan sacrifices for a son. His sacrifice produces Ohrmazd, but his doubt produces Ahriman. Learning that sons have been conceived, Zurvan promises sovereignty to whichever appears first. Ahriman tears open the womb and presents himself. Zurvan recognizes him as dark and foul but must honor his promise, limiting his rule to nine thousand years. Ohrmazd thereafter receives eternal kingship.

Variants differ over the duration, mechanism of birth, Zurvan’s exact promise, and whether Ahriman is evil by nature or choice. The narrative explains both common origin and unequal destiny. It is not found in the surviving Avesta or normative Pahlavi cosmogony.

### 5.13 The peacock

Eznik reports that Ahriman created or displayed the peacock as evidence of creative ability. This story is sometimes circulated online as ancient Zoroastrian scripture. It is not Avestan. It is a fifth-century Armenian polemical report about a Persian doctrine or argument. Its theological point concerns whether a destructive being can create something beautiful and whether apparent beauty can mask defects or vanity.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Was Ahriman worshipped?

Within normative Zoroastrianism, the evidence is overwhelmingly for opposition, expulsion, containment, and refusal—not devotional worship. No securely identified Zoroastrian temple of Ahriman, priesthood of Ahriman, festival in his honor, or ordinary corpus of votive dedications is known.

Ritual engages him negatively:

- reciting the Avesta strengthens the good creation;
- purity rules restrict the spread of corpse pollution and other demonic contamination;
- the Vendīdād ceremony is directed “against the daēvas”;
- prayers invoke Ohrmazd, the Amahraspands, yazatas, and protective spirits to defeat Ahriman;
- killing specified *xrafstar* could be classified as participation in the struggle against his harmful creation;
- funerary practices seek to keep *nasu*, corpse contamination personified as a demon, from earth, fire, and water.

Calling this “cult of Ahriman” would misuse “cult.” He is a ritually addressed enemy, not normally a recipient of reverent service.

### Plutarch’s alleged sacrifice to Areimanios

Plutarch says the Magi made offerings to destructive powers, describing a plant pounded with wolf’s blood and taken to a sunless place while Hades and Darkness were invoked. Several interpretations are possible:

1. an authentic propitiatory or apotropaic rite;
2. ritual disposal of an evil-dedicated substance;
3. a Greek misunderstanding of demon-expulsion;
4. a literary construction shaped by Plutarch’s dualist argument.

The testimony cannot be discarded, but neither can it establish a widespread Iranian Ahriman cult. It is a single external report without matching Iranian liturgy or archaeology.

### Roman dedications to *Deus Arimanius*

Roman inscriptions differ fundamentally because they are positive votive dedications. Known examples occur in or near Mithraic settings at Ostia, Rome, Carnuntum, and York. Their dedicators include Mithraic office-holders such as *pater patrum*. These inscriptions demonstrate that some Roman initiates treated Arimanius as a god capable of receiving vows.

What they do not establish:

- that the cult came directly from Iranian Zoroastrians;
- that Arimanius was understood as absolutely evil;
- that he was identical with the lion-headed figure;
- that Roman Mithraism preserved a secret Avestan theology.

This is a regional Roman religious development, perhaps stimulated by an Iranian name and dualist associations.

### Temples and cult centers

No confirmed Ahriman temple is known in Iran. Zoroastrian fire temples were dedicated to the maintenance of sacred fire and the good creation, not to Ahriman. Claims for “Ahriman temples” generally result from sensational reinterpretation of caves, Mithraea, or demon imagery.

Roman Mithraea containing Arimanius dedications are archaeological cult contexts, but they are temples of the Mithraic mysteries, not demonstrably temples devoted primarily to Arimanius.

### Priesthood

No Ahriman-specific Zoroastrian priesthood is attested. Zoroastrian priests—*āθravans*, later *hērbed*, *mōbed*, and *dastūr*—perform ceremonies that protect creation from demonic forces. Roman dedicators to Arimanius sometimes held Mithraic grades, but that is evidence for Roman Mithraic organization.

### Offerings, magic, and exorcism

Avestan and Middle Persian ritual combats evil through:

- consecrated speech;
- fire and light;
- water and haoma;
- barsom twigs;
- purification with water and, historically, *gōmēz* or consecrated cattle urine;
- confession and expiation;
- watchfulness against corpse pollution;
- protective invocation of Srōš, Mihr, Rašnu, Wahrām, and other yazatas.

Later Persian and Parsi manuscripts contain charms and demonological prescriptions, but attributing every anti-demon formula specifically to Ahriman would be inaccurate.

### Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and cult violence

No reliable evidence establishes human sacrifice or temple prostitution in a Zoroastrian cult of Ahriman. Greek and Christian authors make polemical claims about Persian sexuality and sacrifice, but none supplies secure archaeological confirmation of an Ahrimanic institution.

Violence does enter the record in other forms:

- Sasanian kingship could depict political enemies as counterparts of Ahriman, as at Naqš-e Rostam.
- Zoroastrian imperial policy under some Sasanians repressed rival communities, though responsibility, scale, and motive varied by reign and region.
- Christian polemic cast Iranian religion as service to the devil; Zoroastrian polemic cast religious rivals as agents of the Lie.
- After the Muslim conquest, Zoroastrian institutions declined under changing political, fiscal, and legal conditions. No single empire-wide event “abolished the cult of Ahriman,” since no independent cult existed to abolish.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Old Avestan formation, approximately 1200–1000 BCE

The earliest reconstructable figure is the hostile spirit of the Gāthās. The evidence is linguistic and textual, not archaeological. Angra Mainyu is defined through opposition and wrongful choice. There is no developed hell-court, monstrous body, independent temple, or detailed creation narrative.

The proposition that Zarathustra personally “invented” Angra Mainyu is plausible but unprovable. Indo-Iranian traditions already possessed hostile supernatural powers, death demons, and paired oppositions. What appears distinctive is the ethical concentration of cosmic conflict into truth versus lie and better versus worse choosing.

### II. Younger Avestan expansion, approximately first millennium BCE

Angra Mainyu becomes a named cosmic enemy with residence, armies, and counter-creations. He creates Aži Dahāka, attacks the lands, opposes waters and fertility, tempts Zarathustra, and commands *daēvas*. Heroic and ritual traditions bring him into conflict with Mithra, Tištrya, Fire, the Fravašis, and Iranian kings.

This is not necessarily a linear development preserved in composition order. The Younger Avesta contains archaic poetry alongside later ritual compilation.

### III. Achaemenid period, 550–330 BCE

No Old Persian royal inscription names Angra Mainyu. Darius and successors speak of Auramazdā, the Lie, hostile rebels, and divine favor. Conceptual affinities with Avestan *druj* exist, but identifying every royal “Lie” with Ahriman would exceed the inscriptions.

By the fourth century BCE, Greek testimony attributed an Oromazes–Ariemanios opposition to the Magi. This establishes that a recognizable dualist scheme was known to learned Greeks before the Sasanian period.

### IV. Hellenistic and Arsacid periods, 330 BCE–224 CE

Direct Iranian documentation is sparse. Greek reports, especially Plutarch, show elaborated cosmology and eschatology. Roman Mithraic communities later adopted the name Arimanius in limited dedications.

Claims that Roman Mithraism was simply transplanted Zoroastrianism are discredited. Iranian vocabulary and imagery could be borrowed, reinterpreted, and embedded in a new Roman mystery religion.

### V. Early Sasanian consolidation, 224–fourth century CE

The probable Ahriman figure beneath Ohrmazd’s horse at Naqš-e Rostam gives the adversary a rare monumental body. The image serves dynastic ideology: Ardašīr defeats Ardawān as Ohrmazd defeats Ahriman.

Sasanian rulers and priestly elites collected, classified, and eventually wrote the Avesta in a specially designed alphabet. The traditional account credits Tansar, Ādurbād ī Mahraspandān, and royal patrons; its details are partly legendary. The fourth-century fixation of the Avestan canon is philologically much more secure than the story of an Achaemenid written Avesta burned by Alexander.

Zurvanite interpretations probably circulated in this period, but their institutional status remains contested.

### VI. Manichaean appropriation, third–seventh centuries CE

Mani and his successors translated their opposition of Light and Darkness into Iranian vocabulary. *Ahrmēn* became one name for the dark ruler or power. This represents religious competition and semantic adaptation. Manichaean myth intensified material dualism in ways Zoroastrian polemicists rejected.

### VII. Late antique external polemic, fourth–eighth centuries CE

Armenian and Syriac Christian writers documented—and attacked—Persian dualism. Eznik’s Zurvan myth is indispensable because no native Zurvanite scripture survives. Yet these authors often framed Iranian gods as demons and selected doctrines that aided Christian apologetic arguments.

Political conflict shaped the record. In Sasanian Armenia, religious policy and resistance made accounts of Persian doctrine part of disputes over imperial allegiance and Christian identity.

### VIII. Early Islamic-era priestly systematization, ninth–tenth centuries CE

The *Bundahišn*, *Dēnkard*, *Dādestān ī Dēnīg*, *Škand Gumānīg Wizār*, and related works provide the most extensive record. Their authors inherited Sasanian Zand but wrote under Islamic rule, when Zoroastrian communities faced demographic decline, conversion, legal subordination, and intellectual competition.

Ahreman becomes the central explanatory figure in a complete cosmic history:

1. separation in spirit;
2. limited pact;
3. creation;
4. invasion and mixture;
5. human participation in battle;
6. final separation and renovation.

These books defend Ohrmazd against the charge of creating evil and reject theological models that make Ahreman his product.

### IX. Medieval and early-modern Iranian and Parsi transmission

New Persian increasingly uses *Ahriman* as a literary synonym for devil, demon, tyrant, or evil. Ferdowsi’s *Shāhnāma*, completed around 1010, transmits Iranian legendary material but does not reproduce the Pahlavi cosmic system intact. Islamic vocabulary, especially *dīv* and *Eblīs*, overlaps with older Iranian demonology.

Zoroastrians migrated to western India, where Parsi communities preserved liturgy and manuscripts. From the fifteenth century onward, Indian Parsis sent questions to Iranian priests; the Persian Rivāyats document living medieval and early-modern doctrine about demons, purity, eschatology, and ritual.

### X. European discovery, seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

European travelers saw Sasanian monuments before their inscriptions could be read. Abraham Hyacinthe Anquetil-Duperron obtained Zoroastrian manuscripts in India and published the first European Avesta translation in 1771. Skeptics initially accused the corpus of forgery. Rask, Burnouf, Westergaard, Spiegel, Haug, Geldner, Bartholomae, and others established Avestan as an ancient Iranian language and created critical editions.

During this period “Ahriman” became a European philosophical and literary emblem of radical evil. These uses often collapsed Gāthic, Pahlavi, Manichaean, and Christian categories.

### XI. Twentieth-century philology and comparative religion

Scholars increasingly separated:

- Old from Younger Avestan;
- Avestan composition from medieval manuscript date;
- Mazdean from Zurvanite cosmogony;
- Zoroastrian Ahriman from Manichaean Darkness;
- Iranian Ahriman from Roman Arimanius;
- primary evidence from Greek and Christian reports.

Mary Boyce emphasized continuity and living Zoroastrian practice. Jacques Duchesne-Guillemin traced Ahriman’s changing theology. R. C. Zaehner reconstructed Zurvanism, sometimes more confidently than later scholars accept. Shaul Shaked analyzed Ahreman’s paradoxical ontology and the nature of evil creation. Jean Kellens and other philologists complicated inherited assumptions about the Gāthās and Avestan ritual.

### XII. Present-day Zoroastrianism

Ahriman remains part of Zoroastrian scripture, liturgy, catechesis, and theological discussion, but not a worshipped deity. Interpretations vary:

- conservative dualist readings treat him as a real independent destructive spirit;
- reformist or modernist readings emphasize evil mentality, moral choice, or the psychological force of *angra mainyu*;
- some monotheistic presentations subordinate him sharply to Ahura Mazdā or deny him enduring ontological status;
- traditional eschatology retains his final defeat.

There is no reliable census of adherents to each interpretation. Claims that all modern Zoroastrians understand Ahriman merely as a metaphor—or all as a literal coequal god—are both false generalizations.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### European literature and philosophy

Ahriman entered European discourse through classical authors and early translations of Zoroastrian texts. Romantic and post-Romantic writers used the name as an exotic equivalent of Satan or as one pole of metaphysical dualism. These literary Ahrimans are evidence for European reception, not Iranian cult.

Friedrich Nietzsche’s *Also sprach Zarathustra* (1883–1885) invokes Zarathustra as the imagined speaker who overturns conventional morality. Ahriman is not the book’s developed antagonist. Claims that Nietzsche founded an “Ahrimanic” philosophy are later interpretation, not textual description.

### Rudolf Steiner and Anthroposophy

Rudolf Steiner made the most influential modern esoteric reinvention. In lectures culminating in 1919, he placed humanity between “Luciferic” and “Ahrimanic” forces:

- Lucifer represents premature spiritualization, fantasy, pride, and escape from earthly reality.
- Ahriman represents materialism, mechanization, calculative intellect, rigidification, and denial of spirit.
- Christ or balanced spiritual development mediates between them.
- Steiner predicted or described a future incarnation of Ahriman in the West during the third millennium.

This system combines Zoroastrian names with Christian esotericism, Goethean ideas, Theosophy, and Steiner’s own spiritual history. It is not recoverable from the Avesta. The relevant lecture cycle is preserved in the [Rudolf Steiner Archive](https://rsarchive.org/Lectures/GA191/English/SBC1976/) and dated lecture listings for [1919](https://www.rudolfsteinerelib.org/Lectures/Dates/index.php?yn=1919).

Modern anthroposophical discourse continues to call excessive bureaucracy, digital technology, artificial intelligence, reductionist science, or finance “Ahrimanic.” Such applications are twentieth- and twenty-first-century extensions of Steiner, not ancient attributes.

### Theosophy, occultism, and ceremonial magic

Theosophical and post-Theosophical writers treated Ahriman as a cosmic intelligence, planetary spirit, or pole balancing Lucifer. Ceremonial-magical websites now offer Ahrimanic sigils, invocations, planetary correspondences, and initiation systems. Unless traceable to an actual Iranian or Parsi manuscript, these are modern inventions.

No ancient Zoroastrian grimoire authorizes devotional invocation of Ahriman. Contemporary “Ahrimanian magic” is usually influenced by Satanism, Left-Hand Path occultism, fantasy literature, and modern demonological publishing.

### New religious movements and self-described Ahrimanism

Small online groups and individual occult authors have adopted Ahriman as a positive rebel, liberator, anticosmic power, or patron of self-deification. This reverses the valuation of every normative Zoroastrian source. It is a modern counter-reading, often modeled on romantic Satanism.

Reliable membership figures, institutional continuity, and lineage claims are generally unavailable. Assertions of an unbroken secret Ahriman cult from ancient Iran are unsupported by inscriptions, manuscripts, or archaeology.

### Psychology

Psychological readings treat Ohrmazd and Ahriman as archetypes of integration and destruction, ethical orientation and shadow, or creativity and entropy. These can be meaningful modern interpretations, but they are neither translations of *mainiiu* nor reconstructions of ancient Iranian psychology. A specifically Jungian Ahriman belongs to twentieth-century comparative symbolism.

### Visual arts and popular culture

Modern artists frequently depict Ahriman as:

- a horned Satanic humanoid;
- a black-armored king;
- a dragon or serpent;
- a winged demon;
- a flaming or shadow-like cosmic entity.

Only the serpentine, defeated figure at Naqš-e Rostam has a plausible ancient Iranian iconographic basis. Horns, bat wings, pentagrams, and Christian hellfire usually derive from European demonology.

Names based on Ahriman or Angra Mainyu occur in novels, comics, animation, role-playing games, and video games. Notable patterns include:

- fantasy villains named Ahriman;
- *Warhammer 40,000*’s Ahzek Ahriman, a fictional sorcerer whose biography is unrelated to Zoroastrian texts;
- *Final Fantasy* monsters named Ahriman, usually floating demonic eyes;
- *Fate/Grand Order* and related Japanese media using Angra Mainyu as a transformed fictional antihero or “world’s evil”;
- tabletop demonologies that merge Ahriman with Satanic or Lovecraftian imagery.

These works have materially shaped internet image searches but are not evidence for ancient attributes.

### Nationalist and political appropriation

Iranian nationalist discourse sometimes uses Ahriman metaphorically for foreign enemies, tyranny, clericalism, imperialism, or perceived cultural corruption. Opposed movements can apply the same symbolism to one another.

The Sasanian precedent is structurally relevant: Naqš-e Rostam already aligned a political enemy beneath the king with Ahriman beneath Ohrmazd. Nevertheless, a modern slogan is not proof of doctrinal continuity.

No well-documented, large modern extremist movement centered on literal worship of Ahriman was established in the sources consulted. Isolated online claims should not be inflated into an ancient survival or mass movement.

### Commercial use

“Ahriman” appears as a brand or title for music, games, fictional characters, software handles, and occult merchandise. Commercial sigils marketed as “ancient Ahriman symbols” are especially suspect: Ahriman has no securely attested personal sigil in the Avesta or Pahlavi books.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Ahriman Zarathustra’s invention?

One school treats the ethical opposition of two spirits as Zarathustra’s distinctive solution to evil. Another stresses inherited Indo-Iranian demonology, twin motifs, and the possibility that the Gāthās reformulated existing concepts. There is no pre-Gāthic inscription of Angra Mainyu to decide the issue.

The defensible minimum is that the Gāthās contain the earliest surviving formulation and that its combination of choice, truth, and cosmic consequence is distinctive.

### Who are the “twins”?

Yasna 30’s *yəmā* has produced several readings:

- two literal twin spirits;
- paired mentalities;
- mythic prototypes of human choice;
- Ahura Mazdā’s two sons;
- Ahura Mazdā/Spənta Mainyu versus Angra Mainyu.

Later texts cannot simply be used to settle Old Avestan grammar. The movement from Spənta Mainyu–Angra Mainyu opposition to Ohrmazd–Ahreman opposition is historically real even if theologians understand it as clarification rather than change.

### Is Zoroastrianism monotheistic or dualistic?

The binary question obscures several systems:

- one supreme Ahura Mazdā with opposed spirits;
- cosmic ethical dualism with asymmetric final outcome;
- ontological dualism between uncreated good and destructive principles;
- Zurvanite derivation of both from Time;
- reformist modern monotheism interpreting Angra Mainyu psychologically.

“Dualist” accurately describes the tradition’s irreducible opposition of good and evil. “Two equal gods” inaccurately describes most surviving formulations.

### Did Ahura Mazdā create Ahriman?

The Gāthic evidence is disputed. Orthodox Pahlavi apologetics generally refuses to make Ohrmazd creator of Ahreman because that would implicate perfect goodness in evil. Zurvanite stories give both a common father but make Ahriman’s evil arise from doubt or choice. Modern catechisms differ, partly under the pressure of Abrahamic monotheistic categories.

### Did ancient Persians worship Ahriman?

Plutarch’s ritual report and Roman *Deo Arimanio* inscriptions prompted theories of propitiatory worship. Native Zoroastrian evidence instead emphasizes repulsion. The likely historical picture is differentiated:

- no ordinary Zoroastrian devotional cult is demonstrated;
- hostile powers may have been ritually addressed, contained, or sent away;
- Roman Arimanius received positive dedications in a transformed cultic environment.

### Was Zurvanism an organized sect?

Zaehner reconstructed a major Zurvanite movement, sometimes approaching the status of a Sasanian state religion. Later scholars emphasize that most evidence is external, polemical, and taxonomic. “Zurvanism” may collect multiple speculations about Time rather than name a centralized church.

The twin-birth myth itself is well attested externally. The organizational conclusions drawn from it remain uncertain.

### Roman Mithraism and Arimanius

Older Iran-centered theories treated Mithraism as a western branch of Iranian religion. Current scholarship generally understands Roman Mithraism as a Roman mystery cult using selected Iranian names and imagery.

The *Arimanius* dedications are therefore significant but ambiguous. The equation Arimanius = lion-headed god = Iranian Ahriman is possible, not proved.

### Ahriman and Satan

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century comparativists frequently proposed direct Zoroastrian influence on Jewish, Christian, and Islamic demonology. Contact under Achaemenid and later Iranian rule makes influence historically possible. Specific claims require textual chronology and transmission mechanisms.

Similarities—cosmic adversary, demons, resurrection, judgment, final defeat—do not by themselves prove direct borrowing. Ahriman is never a rebellious angel in the Avesta; Satan’s scriptural history cannot be reduced to a Persian loan.

### Dating the Avesta

The texts have no contemporary colophons. Dating rests on:

- linguistic comparison with Vedic and Old Persian;
- internal social and geographical evidence;
- relative linguistic layers;
- external Greek testimony;
- reconstruction of oral and Sasanian transmission.

The Gāthās are commonly placed near 1000 BCE, but this remains approximate. Younger Avestan texts span a long period. The Sasanian written archetype belongs around the fourth century CE. Extant manuscripts are medieval.

### Manuscript discoveries and editions

Major stages include:

- Anquetil-Duperron’s acquisition and 1771 publication;
- Rask and Burnouf’s demonstration of the language’s Iranian character;
- Westergaard’s nineteenth-century edition;
- Karl Friedrich Geldner’s *Avesta* edition, 1886–1896, based on more than 120 manuscripts;
- Bartholomae’s *Altiranisches Wörterbuch* (1904);
- discovery and study of Iranian Manichaean fragments from Turfan;
- recognition of the Sasanian phonetic script and common manuscript archetypes;
- arrival in India around 1870 of a fuller Iranian *Bundahišn* manuscript, transforming knowledge of its recension.

Digital projects now make manuscript images and transcriptions more accessible, but no single online English translation supersedes modern critical philology.

### State of the evidence

What survives:

- Old and Younger Avestan liturgical material in medieval manuscripts;
- Middle Persian books in late manuscripts;
- one probable Sasanian monumental image;
- Greek, Latin, Armenian, Syriac, Arabic, and Persian testimonies;
- Roman inscriptions to Arimanius;
- Iranian Manichaean fragments.

What is lost:

- most of the Sasanian Avesta and Zand;
- any independent Zurvanite scripture;
- the original manuscripts of Mani’s major Iranian writings;
- most pre-Sasanian Iranian theological documentation;
- context for many Roman Mithraic monuments.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Ahriman was the universally worshipped “dark god” of ancient Persia.
- Achaemenid kings built temples dedicated to him.
- The Mithraic lion-headed figure is certainly Ahriman.
- Ahriman’s ancient sigil is a pentagram or modern occult seal.
- He was always Ahura Mazdā’s equal twin brother.
- He is simply the Persian name for Satan.
- The peacock story occurs in the Avesta.
- Rudolf Steiner’s predicted incarnation is an ancient Zoroastrian prophecy.
- Modern “Ahrimanic orders” preserve an unbroken pre-Islamic priesthood.
- Every harmful animal was universally hated or ritually exterminated by all Zoroastrians.
- The entire *Bundahišn* was written in the Sasanian period exactly as it survives.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the evidentiary record |
|---|---|
| c. 1200–1000 BCE, highly approximate | Old Avestan Gāthās composed orally. Yasna 45.2 names Aŋra Mainiiu; Yasna 30 develops the paired-spirit choice. |
| Early–mid first millennium BCE | Major Younger Avestan traditions develop: counter-creation, conflict for the Glory, fear of Mithra, resistance by Tištrya and the Fravašis, Taxma Urupi’s mastery. |
| c. sixth–fourth centuries BCE | Younger Avestan corpus continues to form; Vendīdād materials cannot be narrowly dated. |
| 550–330 BCE | Achaemenid inscriptions name Auramazdā but not Angra Mainyu. |
| Late fourth century BCE | Aristotle/Eudemus traditions, preserved later, attest Greek knowledge of Oromazes and Ariemanios and possibly Time/Space speculation. |
| First century BCE–first century CE | Iranian eschatological ideas circulate in Hellenistic and Roman intellectual environments; exact textual channels are uncertain. |
| c. 100 CE | Plutarch’s *De Iside et Osiride* 46–47 gives the fullest classical account of Oromazes and Areimanios, alleged Magian rites, and final annihilation of evil. |
| Second–fourth centuries CE | Latin *Arimanius* dedications appear in Roman Mithraic contexts at sites including Ostia, Rome, Carnuntum, and possibly York. |
| 224–242 CE | Ardašīr I’s investiture relief at Naqš-e Rostam depicts a serpent-associated enemy beneath Ohrmazd’s horse, conventionally identified as Ahriman. |
| c. 240s CE | Mani composes the *Šābuhragān* for Šāpūr I; Manichaeism adapts Iranian names including Ahrmēn. |
| Third–fourth centuries CE | Sasanian priestly and royal consolidation of Zoroastrian institutions; traditional accounts associate Tansar and Ādurbād with canon formation. |
| c. fourth century CE | Avestan canon probably written in the newly devised phonetic Avestan alphabet. |
| c. 440s CE | Eznik of Kołb records and refutes Zurvan’s generation of Ohrmazd and Ahriman. |
| Fifth–seventh centuries CE | Armenian, Syriac, Greek, and Christian polemical sources continue to describe Persian dualism. |
| 651 CE and afterward | End of Sasanian rule; Zoroastrian institutions contract gradually under Islamic governance. |
| 791–792 CE | Theodore bar Kōnay’s *Book of Scholia* preserves Zurvanite and Manichaean accounts. |
| Ninth century CE | Zādspram, Manuščihr, Mardānfarrox, and other Middle Persian authors systematize the origin and defeat of Ahreman. |
| Ninth–tenth centuries CE | Principal redaction of the *Bundahišn* and *Dēnkard*; common medieval archetypes of surviving Avestan manuscript families likely date to this period. |
| c. 1000–1010 CE | Ferdowsi’s *Shāhnāma* reshapes Iranian demon and royal traditions in New Persian epic. |
| 1268/1288 CE, disputed | K7a, often described as the oldest surviving Avestan manuscript, is copied. |
| 1323 CE | J2 and other securely dated early Avestan/Pahlavi manuscripts are copied. |
| 1397 CE | H6/M51, an important Indian *Bundahišn* manuscript, is copied at Bharuch. |
| Fifteenth–eighteenth centuries | Persian Rivāyats record correspondence between Iranian priests and Indian Parsi communities, including demonological and eschatological doctrine. |
| 1597 CE | Manuscript DH preserves the Iranian *Bundahišn* and related material. |
| 1771 CE | Anquetil-Duperron publishes the *Zend-Avesta* and a European translation of the *Bundahišn*, bringing Ahriman into modern European scholarship. |
| 1820 CE | Rasmus Rask brings important Pahlavi manuscripts, including K20, to Copenhagen. |
| 1851 CE | Westergaard publishes a lithographed Indian *Bundahišn*. |
| 1860s CE | European philological editions and translations multiply; Avestan’s Iranian linguistic position is firmly established. |
| 1886–1896 CE | Geldner publishes his critical edition of the Avesta. |
| 1904 CE | Bartholomae publishes the foundational *Altiranisches Wörterbuch*. |
| Early twentieth century | Turfan expeditions recover Middle Iranian Manichaean fragments using Ahrmēn and related Iranian cosmological vocabulary. |
| 1919 CE | Rudolf Steiner delivers major lectures on Lucifer and Ahriman and predicts a future Ahrimanic incarnation. |
| 1955 CE | R. C. Zaehner publishes *Zurvan: A Zoroastrian Dilemma*, strongly shaping modern discussion of Zurvanism. |
| 1967 CE | Shaul Shaked publishes a major study of Ahreman and his creation, sharpening debate over the ontological status of evil. |
| 1984 CE | Jacques Duchesne-Guillemin’s “Ahriman” article appears in *Encyclopaedia Iranica*, providing a still-influential chronological synthesis. |
| Late twentieth century | Scholarship increasingly separates Roman Mithraic Arimanius from automatic identification with Iranian Ahriman. |
| Twenty-first century | Digital manuscript archives and online text corpora expand access; modern occult, anthroposophical, gaming, and internet images increasingly diverge from Iranian evidence. |
| Present | Ahriman remains a real doctrinal adversary, metaphysical problem, or ethical symbol in different Zoroastrian interpretations; no normative devotional cult exists. |
