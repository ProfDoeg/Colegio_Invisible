# The Apkallu: Pantheon Dossier

> **Scope and method.** “The Apkallu” are a class or collective, not one god with a continuous biography. This dossier therefore distinguishes:  
> **[ED]** securely attested Early Dynastic evidence; **[S/O]** Sumerian or Old Babylonian literary evidence; **[MB]** Middle Babylonian/Kassite evidence; **[1M]** first-millennium Assyrian-Babylonian systematization; **[Sel]** Seleucid scholarly tradition; **[Gr]** Greek/Hellenistic transmission; **[Comp]** scholarly comparison rather than direct identity; **[Mod]** modern reception or invention.  
> Dates assigned to literary compositions are approximate; the date of a surviving tablet is not automatically the date of the tradition copied on it.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

The **apkallū** are Mesopotamian sages: in different sources the word designates learned human specialists, legendary culture heroes, semidivine or supernatural beings associated with Enki/Ea and the subterranean freshwater ocean, and protective figures employed in ritual and royal imagery. The familiar synthesis—“seven antediluvian fish-men sent by Ea to civilize humanity”—is substantially a first-millennium and Hellenistic construction assembled from several older strands. Early Sumerian evidence establishes *abgal* as “sage” or a learned/priestly profession, but it does not yet document the complete canonical list of seven named prediluvian beings. The seven-member series, their pairing with antediluvian kings, and their differentiation from post-Flood human scholars are products of later learned historiography and ritual scholarship.

### Names and spellings

- **Sumerian:** *abgal*, conventionally “sage,” “great wise one,” or “master-sage.” Common spellings include **AB.GAL** and **NUN.ME**; Unicode approximations are 𒀊𒃲 and 𒉣𒈨, although sign forms and readings vary by period. The Electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary records approximately 125 attestations across periods from Early Dynastic through Hellenistic texts, including professional and priestly uses; it does not imply that every occurrence denotes the Seven Sages. [ePSD2 lexical entry](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0023369.html)
- **Akkadian singular:** *apkallu*; **plural:** *apkallū*. It means “sage,” “expert,” or exceptionally learned person and can denote humans, supernatural sages, or an epithet of gods.
- **Logographic Akkadian writing:** often **NUN.ME**, read *apkallu* from context.
- **Human scholarly counterpart:** *ummânu* (plural *ummânū*), “expert/master scholar.” First-millennium lists exploit the contrast between primordial *apkallū* and later human *ummânū*, but usage is not absolutely consistent.
- **Greek:** **Ὀάννης**, *Oannes*, the first marine culture hero in Berossus; **Ἀννήδωτος / Annēdōtos**, “repulsive/strange one,” or a name/title applied to later marine beings in the surviving Greek transmission. The phonological and textual relation of Oannes to cuneiform **U-An/Uanna** is strong; equation with the god Dagon is an early-modern and modern comparativist development, not an ancient cuneiform identification.
- **Modern forms:** Apkallu, Apkallū, Abgal, Seven Sages, Seven Wise Men, fish-sages, fish-men, winged genii. “Anunnaki” is not a synonym: the Anunnakkū are a different divine collective.

### Etymology

The Sumerian *abgal* is traditionally analyzed as **ab/aba + gal**, producing translations such as “great father,” “great elder,” or “great sage,” but the exact historical morphology is not certain. Akkadian *apkallu* is generally treated as a Sumerian loan or adaptation.

Modern interpretations of **U-An/Uanna** as “light of An” are possible learned spellings, not a secure original etymology. The equation **Uanna = Adapa = Oannes** rests on first-millennium associations, orthographic play, shared Eridu/Ea affiliations, and Berossus—not on an early tablet straightforwardly saying that these are three spellings of one individual. Amar Annus emphasizes that first-millennium texts themselves preserve conflicting placements of Adapa: the Uruk list assigns Nungalpiriggal, not Adapa, to Enmerkar. [Annus, *The Overturned Boat*](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Amar-Annus/publication/303286930_The_Overturned_Boat_Intertextuality_of_the_Adapa_Myth_and_Exorcist_Literature/links/5ae09a00aca272fdaf8c85b4/The-Overturned-Boat-Intertextuality-of-the-Adapa-Myth-and-Exorcist-Literature.pdf)

### The canonical seven and their interpretive titles

The spellings differ among *Bīt mēseri*, related incantations, and the Uruk King List. A conventional reconstruction is:

| Order | Name | Interpretive description in first-millennium tradition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | U-An, Uanna, Uan-Adapa | “who completed the plans/designs of heaven and earth”; often associated with Adapa and Greek Oannes |
| 2 | U-An-dugga / Uannedugga | “endowed with comprehensive understanding” |
| 3 | Enmedugga / Enmeduga | “to whom a favorable destiny was allotted” |
| 4 | Enmegalamma / Enmegalama | “born in a house” |
| 5 | Enmebulugga / Enmebuluga | “who grew up in pastureland” |
| 6 | An-Enlilda / Anenlilda | “conjurer of Eridu” |
| 7 | Utuabzu / Utu-abzu | “who ascended to heaven” |

The apparently meaningful Sumerian names may be first-millennium learned constructions. Wiggermann argues that several were fashioned from literary titles or reinterpreted antediluvian royal names; consequently they cannot simply be projected into third-millennium Sumer. [Wiggermann, *Mesopotamian Protective Spirits*](https://mc.dlib.nyu.edu/files/books/brill_awdl000079/brill_awdl000079_lo.pdf)

### Classification by period and context

- **[ED]** *Abgal* can be a human profession or priestly designation.
- **[S/O]** Sumerian compositions use *abgal* for sages or priests and occasionally for a collective of sages; they do not provide the later complete biography of seven fish-beings.
- **[MB]** Fish-cloaked supernatural figures are securely represented in Kassite-period glyptic, showing that important elements of the later conception existed by the later second millennium.
- **[1M]** The sages become a named antediluvian septad, creatures of the Apsû, cosmic experts, ritual protectors, and ancestors or prototypes of scholarly professions.
- **[Sel]** Learned lists connect them to antediluvian kings and continue the sequence through legendary and historical scholars.
- **[Gr]** Berossus turns the first sage and related beings into amphibious teachers who reveal the arts of civilized life.
- **[Mod]** Occult and ancient-astronaut literature frequently converts them into extraterrestrials, “Anunnaki,” genetic engineers, or bearers of technology. No cuneiform or Greek source supports those classifications.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

The earliest secure evidence is for the **word and office**, not for the canonical supernatural Seven. The ePSD corpus records *abgal* already in **Early Dynastic I–II and III** documents, approximately the early third millennium BCE. Such administrative and lexical occurrences designate sages, priests, or officials. They do not say that seven amphibious beings emerged from the sea or served antediluvian kings. [ePSD2](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/epsd2/cbd/sux/o0023369.html)

This is the crucial evidentiary boundary:

1. **Early existence of the term *abgal***: secure.
2. **Early third-millennium existence of the later named Seven**: not demonstrated.
3. **Older oral prototypes behind the first-millennium lists**: possible but not datable.
4. **Fully developed fish-sage tradition by the Kassite period**: supported by iconography and probably by a bilingual incantation whose language may preserve a Middle Babylonian formulation. [Wiggermann](https://mc.dlib.nyu.edu/files/books/brill_awdl000079/brill_awdl000079_lo.pdf)

### Principal textual dossier

#### Sumerian literary references

**Enki and the World Order** — Sumerian; surviving chiefly in Old Babylonian school tablets, early second millennium BCE. The composition mentions *abgal* among the personnel or specialists ordered within Enki’s cosmos. It supports the association of sages with Enki but not the canonical seven-name list. [ETCSL glossed text](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/edition2/etcslgloss.php?charenc=&fp=ON&lookup=c113.414)

**Temple hymn or liturgical composition ETCSL 4.80.1** — Sumerian, known through later copies; it declares that the Seven Sages enlarged or established a sanctuary/territory. This is evidence for a seven-member building collective in Sumerian learned tradition, but its tablet history and composition date must be distinguished. [ETCSL 4.80.1](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/cgi-bin/etcsl.cgi?text=t.4.80.1)

Other Sumerian texts employ *abgal* as a title for unusually wise humans, ritualists, or gods. These generic uses cannot automatically be counted as appearances of the antediluvian group.

#### Adapa tradition

**Adapa and the South Wind** — Akkadian. The best-known early manuscript is **EA 356**, recovered at Amarna in Egypt and copied in the fourteenth century BCE; first-millennium fragments come from Assyria. Adapa, priest or sage of Eridu and favorite of Ea, breaks the South Wind’s wing, is summoned before Anu, and refuses food and water offered in heaven because Ea has warned that they are death. The text calls him extraordinarily wise while leaving him mortal.

Adapa is not named as one of a septad in the Amarna manuscript. His merger with Uanna, the first apkallu, belongs to later scholarly tradition. Even the outcome is debated: the offered food may be life-giving, as the standard reading holds, but the text’s damaged state and divine deception complicate reconstruction. [Annus, discussion of the Adapa manuscripts and exorcistic tradition](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Amar-Annus/publication/303286930_The_Overturned_Boat_Intertextuality_of_the_Adapa_Myth_and_Exorcist_Literature/links/5ae09a00aca272fdaf8c85b4/The-Overturned-Boat-Intertextuality-of-the-Adapa-Myth-and-Exorcist-Literature.pdf)

#### *Bīt mēseri*

**Bīt mēseri**, “House of Confinement/Enclosure” — Akkadian with bilingual Sumerian-Akkadian incantations; organized as a four-tablet first-millennium apotropaic series. Neo-Assyrian witnesses come from Ashurbanipal’s library at Nineveh; a fourth- or third-century BCE recension was recovered from a priestly house at Uruk. The work prescribes making, naming, purifying, clothing, painting, and burying groups of protective figures around a building or sickroom.

Tablet III preserves the most important list of the seven antediluvian sages and then four sages “of human descent.” Its incantation describes the Seven as brilliant or holy *purādu*-fish, associated simultaneously with sea and river, who maintain the correct designs of heaven and earth. Other sections assign anthropomorphic, fish-cloaked, and bird-headed apkallū to positions at doors, walls, the head or foot of a bed, and foundations. [Wiggermann’s critical collection](https://mc.dlib.nyu.edu/files/books/brill_awdl000079/brill_awdl000079_lo.pdf)

The series was part of the āšipu/exorcist’s professional curriculum. It is direct evidence for ritual manufacture and deployment, not merely a mythological story.

#### LKA 76 / VAT 13839

**LKA 76**, museum number **VAT 13839**, from Assur — Late Assyrian; two bilingual Sumerian-Akkadian compositions. One section was incorporated into *Bīt mēseri* and recounts episodes involving four post-Flood sages of human descent. It connects primeval wisdom with contemporary exorcistic scholarship. [Frahm and Jiménez, “The Sages and the Sons of Nippur”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/719864)

The post-Flood figures are:

- **Nungalpiriggaldim**, linked elsewhere with Enmerkar and Uruk;
- **Piriggalnungal**, whose offense angers Adad/Iškur and brings three years without rain or vegetation;
- **Piriggalabzu**, who provokes Ea through an improper act involving his seal and a goat-fish;
- **Lu-Nanna**, “two-thirds apkallu,” who drives an *ušumgallu* dragon from a temple associated with Ištar and Šulgi.

Textual damage and variant recensions prevent a seamless narrative; their “human descent” marks a categorical difference from the antediluvian sages.

#### Epic of Erra

**Erra and Išum**, Tablet I — Standard Babylonian Akkadian; probably composed in the early first millennium BCE and attributed by its colophon to Kabti-ilāni-Marduk. Marduk asks where the seven sages of the Apsû are: pure *purādu*-fish, perfected in lofty wisdom like Ea, formerly able to purify Marduk’s body or image. Marduk says that after the Flood he sent them down or banished them to the Apsû and prevented their return.

The passage presupposes:

- a septad;
- aquatic nature;
- Ea as their lord or model;
- cult-image purification;
- their removal from the human world after the Flood.

It does not narrate their original arrival or enumerate their names. [Rochberg on apkallū and the maintenance of cosmic designs](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/knpp/downloads/rochberg_aoat267.pdf)

#### Epic of Gilgamesh

The Standard Babylonian prologue credits the **Seven Sages** with laying the foundations or plans of Uruk’s wall. The passage locates them in primeval monumental construction and validates Uruk through antediluvian wisdom. It does not describe fish bodies or list individual names. The Standard Babylonian recension is first-millennium in its surviving form, though it reworks much older Gilgamesh traditions.

#### Catalogue of Texts and Authors

The first-millennium **Catalogue of Texts and Authors** attributes learned compositions to Ea, Uan-Adapa, other sages, gods, and legendary or historical scholars. It is intellectual genealogy, not a contemporary record of authorship. Its function was to give scholarly corpora primordial and divine pedigrees. Alan Lenzi interprets such genealogies and “secret knowledge” colophons as strategies by which specialists authorized restricted knowledge. [Lenzi, *Secrecy and the Gods*](https://www.eisenbrauns.org/books/titles/978-952-10-1330-0.html)

#### Uruk List of Kings and Sages

**W 20030,7**, excavated in the Bīt Rēš temple complex at Uruk; Akkadian; copied by Anu-bēlšunu on 10 Ayyar in Seleucid year 147, **165 BCE**. It pairs seven antediluvian kings with seven sages, marks the Flood, and continues with Nungalpiriggal and then named *ummânū* associated with Gilgamesh and historical kings. Its colophon identifies a scholarly owner descended from Sîn-lēqi-unninni and warns against removal.

The list reads in substance:

| King | Sage |
|---|---|
| Ayalu | U-An |
| Alalgar | U-An-duga |
| Ameluana | Enmeduga |
| Enmeušumgalana or a variant royal sequence | Enmebuluga |
| Dumuzi the Shepherd | An-Enlilda |
| Enmeduranki | Utuabzu |
| other damaged/variant antediluvian pairing | Enmegalama |

After the Flood, **Nungalpiriggal** serves **Enmerkar**; Ištar brings him down from heaven to Eanna, and he manufactures or installs an elaborate bronze-and-lapis lyre using Ninagal’s craft before Anu. Later entries replace *apkallu* with *ummânu*, including Sîn-lēqi-unninni, Kabti-ilī-Marduk, Esagil-kīn-apli, and an Assyrian scholar whom Arameans called Ahiqar. [Edition and translation of the list](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004520264/BP000013.pdf)

The tablet is late evidence about imagined deep antiquity. Its value lies in documenting Seleucid Uruk’s learned historical system, not in proving that named advisers served actual prehistoric kings.

#### Enmeduranki revelation

A first-millennium account makes **Enmeduranki**, antediluvian king of Sippar and sometimes paired with Utuabzu, the recipient of divine instruction from **Šamaš and Adad**. They reveal oil divination, extispicy, celestial observation, and a “tablet of the gods,” after which he transmits this expertise to learned men of Nippur, Sippar, and Babylon. This tradition overlaps structurally with apkallu ideology but does not consistently make Enmeduranki himself one of the Seven. [Rochberg](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/saao/knpp/downloads/rochberg_aoat267.pdf)

#### Berossus

**Berossus, *Babyloniaca*, Book I** — Greek, composed early in the third century BCE by a Babylonian priest writing under Seleucid rule. The original is lost. The Oannes narrative survives indirectly through Alexander Polyhistor, Abydenus, Eusebius, Syncellus, and an Armenian version of Eusebius.

Oannes emerges from the Persian Gulf/Erythraean Sea in the first year of the first king. He has a fish body with a human body, head, feet, and voice associated with or beneath it. By day he teaches humans writing, sciences, crafts, city-building, temples, law, surveying, agriculture, and everything needed for civilized life; by night he returns to the sea. Later comparable beings appear during the antediluvian reigns. Berossus says Oannes also communicated a cosmogony.

This is the fullest culture-bringer narrative but also the latest ancient formulation. It cannot be retrojected word-for-word into Sumerian religion. [Discussion of Berossus and Greek cultural translation](https://etheses.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/5679/1/Oll15PhD.pdf)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

The sources assign the apkallū several overlapping functions:

- **Wisdom and technical mastery:** scribal arts, ritual knowledge, crafts, divination, medicine, temple construction, and civilization.
- **Mediation:** conveying divine plans from Ea’s sphere to kings and human specialists.
- **Cosmic maintenance:** ensuring correct performance of the “designs of heaven and earth.”
- **Purification:** purifying divine images, kings, rooms, beds, doors, and ritual spaces.
- **Protection:** repelling disease, witchcraft, demons, and hostile forces through images buried or stationed in buildings.
- **Royal legitimation:** surrounding the king and stylized tree in palace art; placing Assyrian kings within an ideal system of supernatural knowledge and order.
- **Scholarly ancestry:** legitimizing āšipū, bārû-diviners, physicians, lamentation priests, and scribes as heirs to primeval revelation.

The idea that their defining task was to teach all civilization is clearest in **Berossus**. Earlier cuneiform material emphasizes wisdom, ritual purity, building, and cosmic order but does not provide Berossus’s exhaustive curriculum.

### Three principal iconographic forms

Ritual texts and labeled or contextually identified figurines support three main types:

1. **Human or anthropomorphic apkallu** (*ūmu-apkallu* in modern discussion): bearded male, sometimes winged, often horned or crowned, carrying a bucket and purifier.
2. **Fish-cloaked apkallu:** a human body covered by a fish skin; the fish head forms a hood above the human head, while the fish body hangs down the back.
3. **Bird-headed winged apkallu:** muscular human body, bird or eagle head, wings, bucket, and purifier.

The types are united functionally in ritual texts, but individual palace figures are not always securely distinguishable from gods, “genii,” or other protective beings. Stephanie Dalley stresses that iconographic identification is probabilistic when inscriptions or archaeological context are absent. [Dalley, IDD “Apkallu”](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_apkallu.pdf)

### Chronological development

- Fish-cloaked figures occur in **Kassite glyptic**, demonstrating a second-millennium precursor to first-millennium Assyrian forms.
- Early monumental examples include the fish-cloaked figure on the **Terqa stela of Tukulti-Ninurta II** (890–884 BCE) and figures at the **Ninurta temple at Kalḫu/Nimrud**, probably installed under Ashurnasirpal II (883–859 BCE).
- Ashurnasirpal’s Northwest Palace made winged human and bird-headed protective figures a monumental visual program.
- Comparable figures appear on wall paintings, seals, ivory, metalwork, Urartian objects, amulets, and buried clay figurines. [IDD iconographic catalogue](https://www.religionswissenschaft.uzh.ch/idd/prepublications/e_idd_apkallu.pdf)

### Objects and regalia

- **Banduddû:** handled ritual bucket. Calling it a modern “handbag” is visually understandable but archaeologically misleading.
- **Mullilu:** purifier, conventionally called a cone; it may represent a conifer cone, date-palm inflorescence, or stylized ritual implement. A simple “pollination device” interpretation is not established.
- **Sprig or branch:** held by some figures.
- **Horned crown:** may signal supernatural or divine status, but not every figure wears one.
- **Wings:** mark supernatural capacity; they are not universal.
- **Fish skin and bird head:** typological forms, not evidence of biological species as understood by modern zoology.
- **Garments and jewelry:** elite or divine dress links the figures to court and temple visual language.

Scenes often show pairs flanking the king or a stylized tree, apparently performing purification or protection. The exact meaning of the so-called **Sacred Tree/Tree of Life** remains debated; “Tree of Life” is a modern descriptive label, not its securely recovered Assyrian name.

### Archaeological examples

- **Metropolitan Museum 54.117.26**, bird-headed, four-winged clay apkallu with bucket, Nimrud, ninth–eighth century BCE. It was found below a palace floor, directly supporting an apotropaic function. [Met object record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/324339)
- **British Museum 90992/DT 389**, clay bird-headed apkallu, Nimrud Temple V, 800–600 BCE, discovered in a brick receptacle beneath the floor and probably belonging to a set of seven. [British Museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_DT-389)
- **VA Ass 03595**, fish-cloaked foundation figurine from the house of a priest at Assur, Neo-Assyrian, approximately 1000–612 BCE. [ISAW object record](https://isaw.nyu.edu/exhibitions/ishtar-gate/objects/figurine-apkallu)
- **Northwest Palace reliefs, Nimrud**, reign of Ashurnasirpal II, 883–859 BCE, now divided among museums. The princely and royal setting monumentalized the figures beyond the small buried form. [Princeton relief record](https://artmuseum.princeton.edu/art/collections/objects/34202)
- **Walters 21.8**, winged genius/apkallu relief from the Northwest Palace. [Walters record](https://art.thewalters.org/object/21.8/)

### Sacred correspondences

Secure associations are:

- **Number:** seven, particularly in first-millennium lists and deposits.
- **Element/location:** Apsû, rivers, sea, and Eridu.
- **Patron:** Enki/Ea.
- **Materials in ritual:** clay, tamarisk, cornel wood, gypsum, bitumen/black paste, white paste, and other materials specified by figure and location.
- **Times:** *Bīt mēseri* ritual traditions assign performances to months including Tašrītu and Araḫsamna.

No ancient source establishes a canonical apkallu color, weekday, planet, gemstone, or zodiacal sign comparable to later occult correspondence tables. Modern lists supplying these are reconstructions or inventions.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin and hierarchy

No source gives the collective ordinary biological parents. First-millennium ritual texts call them creations or creatures of the **Apsû**, “grown” or created in the river, and make **Ea** their lord. Their origin is therefore cosmogonic and institutional rather than genealogical.

They are below the major gods but above ordinary humans. Their divine status is fluid:

- antediluvian sages are represented as fully supernatural;
- post-Flood sages are “of human descent”;
- Lu-Nanna is only “two-thirds apkallu”;
- historical *ummânū* are learned humans who inherit their authority.

They have no securely attested collective consorts, children, mounts, or independent court. The claim that apkallū intermarried with human women is a modern inference from “human descent” and from comparison with the Enochic Watchers; no surviving Mesopotamian narrative explicitly tells such a mating episode.

### Every documented named divine interaction

Because many texts mention the collective rather than individual sages, the following list separates direct textual episodes from scholarly equations.

#### Ea/Enki

- **Relation:** lord, creator, model, and source of the sages’ wisdom.  
  **Source/date:** *Bīt mēseri* and *Erra and Išum*, first-millennium witnesses.  
  **Tier:** **[1M canonical systematization]**.
- **Relation:** Ea perfects the four post-Flood sages of human descent with broad understanding.  
  **Source/date:** LKA 76/*Bīt mēseri* tradition, Late Assyrian.  
  **Tier:** **[1M ritual tradition]**.
- **Relation:** Piriggalabzu offends Ea in the Apsû through an episode involving a seal and goat-fish; punishment follows through a fuller striking him with that seal.  
  **Source/date:** LKA 76/VAT 13839 and parallels.  
  **Tier:** **[1M etiological episode]**.
- **Relation:** Ea advises Adapa before his heavenly audience.  
  **Source/date:** *Adapa and the South Wind*, fourteenth-century BCE Amarna manuscript and later copies.  
  **Tier:** **[MB text; later connected to apkallu]**.
- **Caution:** “Ea fathered the seven” is not explicitly stated as sexual genealogy.

#### Anu/An

- **Relation:** Adapa is summoned before Anu after breaking the South Wind’s wing; Anu questions him and has food and water presented.  
  **Source/date:** *Adapa and the South Wind*, Amarna and first-millennium manuscripts.  
  **Tier:** **[MB narrative, later apkallu identification]**.
- **Relation:** Nungalpiriggal’s crafted lyre is installed before Anu at Uruk.  
  **Source/date:** W 20030,7, copied 165 BCE.  
  **Tier:** **[Sel learned historiography]**.
- **Relation:** The Seven establish or safeguard the designs of heaven, Anu’s realm among them.  
  **Source/date:** first-millennium incantations.  
  **Tier:** **[1M generalized theology]**; not a discrete meeting.

#### Ištar/Inanna

- **Relation:** Ištar brings Nungalpiriggal down from heaven to Eanna, or, under another grammatical interpretation, Nungalpiriggal brings Ištar down; the broken text permits disagreement.  
  **Source/date:** Uruk List W 20030,7, 165 BCE.  
  **Tier:** **[Sel regional Uruk tradition]**.
- **Relation:** Lu-Nanna drives an *ušumgallu* dragon from E-ninkarnunna, a temple associated in the text with Ištar and Šulgi.  
  **Source/date:** LKA 76/*Bīt mēseri* tradition, Late Assyrian.  
  **Tier:** **[1M etiological episode]**.

#### Adad/Iškur

- **Relation:** Piriggalnungal angers Adad in heaven; Adad withholds rain and vegetation for three years.  
  **Source/date:** LKA 76/VAT 13839.  
  **Tier:** **[1M etiological episode]**.
- **Relation:** Adad and Šamaš reveal divinatory knowledge to Enmeduranki, whose king-sage pairing intersects the apkallu system.  
  **Source/date:** first-millennium Enmeduranki tradition.  
  **Tier:** **[1M scholarly genealogy]**.

#### Šamaš/Utu

- **Relation:** With Adad, Šamaš seats or appoints Enmeduranki in Ebabbar and reveals the divine tablet and divinatory arts.  
  **Source/date:** first-millennium revelation text.  
  **Tier:** **[1M scholarly genealogy]**.
- **Relation to Utuabzu:** similarity of the element *Utu* in the name does not itself establish parentage or a narrated encounter.

#### Marduk

- **Relation:** The sages once purified Marduk’s body or cult image.  
  **Source/date:** *Erra and Išum* I, early first millennium BCE.  
  **Tier:** **[1M canonical literary]**.
- **Relation:** Marduk says that after the Flood he sent the sages to the Apsû and barred their return.  
  **Source/date:** *Erra and Išum*.  
  **Tier:** **[1M canonical literary]**.
- **Relation:** Their absence leaves Marduk unable to renew his image properly, contributing to the crisis that permits Erra’s destructive activity.  
  **Source/date:** *Erra and Išum*.  
  **Tier:** **[1M canonical literary]**.

#### Erra

- **Relation:** Erra exploits Marduk’s concern about his image and the absent sages, encouraging him to leave his sanctuary. The sages do not meet Erra directly in the surviving episode.  
  **Source/date:** *Erra and Išum*.  
  **Tier:** **[1M canonical literary]**.

#### Išum

- **Relation:** Išum is present in the larger Erra narrative in which the absent sages are discussed, but no direct apkallu–Išum exchange is preserved.  
  **Source/date:** *Erra and Išum*.  
  **Tier:** **[1M co-occurrence, not direct interaction]**.

#### Ninagal

- **Relation:** Nungalpiriggal constructs the bronze-and-lapis lyre according to Ninagal’s craft.  
  **Source/date:** W 20030,7, 165 BCE.  
  **Tier:** **[Sel learned tradition]**.
- **Caution:** this is dependence on a divine craft, not necessarily a narrated meeting.

#### The South Wind

- **Relation:** Adapa curses and breaks the South Wind’s wing after it overturns his boat.  
  **Source/date:** *Adapa and the South Wind*, Amarna manuscript.  
  **Tier:** **[MB narrative; relevant only through later Adapa–Uanna equation]**.

#### Tammuz and Gizzida

- **Relation:** They stand at Anu’s gate and intercede or respond favorably to Adapa after he appears in mourning clothes.  
  **Source/date:** *Adapa and the South Wind*.  
  **Tier:** **[MB narrative; later apkallu association]**.

#### Ušumgallu dragon

- **Relation:** Lu-Nanna expels the dragon from E-ninkarnunna.  
  **Source/date:** LKA 76/*Bīt mēseri*.  
  **Tier:** **[1M etiological episode]**.
- **Classification:** the *ušumgallu* is a named class of mythic dragon, not necessarily a unique god.

### Proposed cross-traditional relations

- **Enochic Watchers:** Annus and Kvanvig argue that the Watcher story polemically inverts Mesopotamian apkallu traditions: antediluvian revealers become transgressive angels whose instruction corrupts humanity. This is a historical-literary hypothesis, not an ancient equation. [Annus, “On the Origin of Watchers”](https://www.ldsscriptureteachings.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Amar-Annnus-On-the-Watchers-A-Comparative-Study-2010.pdf)
- **Enoch:** comparison with seventh sage Utuabzu and Enmeduranki rests on seventh position, heavenly ascent, and revelation. It is plausible influence or counter-tradition, not identity.
- **Nephilim:** the analogy is scholarly and contested. The Mesopotamian sources do not call apkallū giants or offspring of angels.
- **Dagon:** Oannes was identified with Dagon in early-modern comparative mythology largely because both were imagined as fish-associated. The ancient equation is unsupported.
- **Seven Greek sages, Indian Saptarṣis, and worldwide “seven civilizers”:** resemblance alone does not demonstrate transmission.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The antediluvian civilizers

**Fullest version:** Berossus says Oannes emerged from the sea, lived amphibiously, and taught humanity the total apparatus of civilization. Later similar beings appeared under other antediluvian kings.

**Earliest evidence:** not Berossus’s complete story. Earlier cuneiform texts attest sages associated with Ea, sacred knowledge, cosmic designs, and prediluvian kings, but no surviving early Sumerian tablet contains Berossus’s connected narrative.

**Development:**

1. third-millennium *abgal* as sage or office;
2. second-millennium Adapa and fish-cloak imagery;
3. first-millennium named septad and antediluvian system;
4. Seleucid king-sage chronology;
5. Berossus’s Greek ethnographic presentation of universal culture-bringers.

**Function:** it authorizes Babylonian sciences as primeval revelation and presents Babylonian civilization to a Greek-reading world as older than ordinary history.

### The sages and the Flood

In the first-millennium scheme, the Seven belong before the Flood. *Erra and Išum* has Marduk consign them to the Apsû afterward and prevent their return. The Uruk List places a boundary labeled “after the Flood,” then allows one exceptional apkallu, Nungalpiriggal, before changing to human *ummânū*. The Flood therefore explains why divine wisdom is no longer delivered openly by supernatural sages but survives through restricted scholarly lineages.

There is no surviving narrative in which the seven sail through the Flood together, build an ark, or genetically preserve humanity. Those are modern extrapolations.

### Adapa and the loss of immortality

Adapa, Ea’s exceptionally wise servant at Eridu, goes fishing or performs temple duties. The South Wind overturns his boat, so he breaks its wing, stopping the wind for seven days. Anu summons him. Ea orders mourning dress and instructs him to win the sympathy of the gatekeepers Tammuz and Gizzida, but warns him against accepting food and water. In heaven Anu offers them; Adapa refuses. He returns mortal.

The story explores the boundary between divine wisdom and immortality. Adapa possesses extraordinary knowledge but not eternal life. Later scholars identify him with Uan/Uanna, making the first apkallu the paradigmatic mortal-divine mediator. The oldest manuscript does not itself call him the first of seven.

### The absent sages and Marduk’s image

In *Erra and Išum*, Marduk’s cult image has lost its brilliance. Proper renewal requires rare materials and the skill of the seven sages, but they have been confined to the Apsû since the Flood. The inability to restore the image helps force Marduk to leave his station; Erra then unleashes catastrophe.

This myth gives the sages a precise cultic function: they are divine craftsmen and purifiers necessary for maintaining embodied kingship and cosmic order. It also dramatizes the danger of ritual discontinuity.

### The four post-Flood sages

The Late Assyrian etiological cycle presents wisdom after the Flood as diminished and morally fallible:

- Nungalpiriggaldim participates in Uruk’s cultic and musical civilization;
- Piriggalnungal provokes a drought through offense against Adad;
- Piriggalabzu violates a boundary in Ea’s aquatic realm and is punished;
- Lu-Nanna is explicitly fractional—two-thirds sage—yet defeats a temple dragon.

These episodes resist the modern picture of uniformly benevolent teachers. Post-Flood sages can transgress divine order and suffer consequences.

### The builders of Uruk

The Standard Babylonian *Gilgamesh* credits the Seven with the wall’s plan or foundations. The narrative does not pause to identify them; their name functions as an authority claim. Uruk’s monumental fabric is thereby linked to wisdom from before the Flood.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No independent public cult

No temple is securely known as a temple **of the Seven Apkallu**, and no independent apkallu priesthood, regular public festival, major sacrificial calendar, or city patronage is documented. They belonged within the cults and ritual systems of major gods, especially Ea, Marduk, Aššur, Ninurta, and royal palace ideology.

Calling them “worshipped gods” without qualification overstates the evidence. They received ritual activation, invocation, deposition, and probably offerings as protective beings, but the material record is principally **apotropaic**, not a public civic cult.

### Ritual manufacture and installation

The *Bīt mēseri* corpus prescribes:

1. selecting clay, wood, gypsum, bitumen, or other material;
2. fashioning specified classes and numbers of figures;
3. painting or clothing them;
4. reciting individual incantations over them;
5. placing them at doors, corners, walls, foundations, or around a patient’s bed;
6. sealing them beneath floors or in brick boxes.

Sets could include seven fish-cloaked sages, seven bird-headed sages, seven human sages, and other protective figures. The prescriptions vary by recension and architectural location; not every excavated assemblage contains the ideal complete set.

Archaeological deposits at Nimrud, Nineveh, Khorsabad, Assur, and Babylonian sites confirm that such figurines were physically buried beneath elite, temple, palace, and private spaces. The British Museum’s DT 389 group, recovered from a brick receptacle, is especially strong text–archaeology correspondence. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_DT-389)

### Sickroom protection

Some prescriptions station seven cornel-wood sages at the bedroom door near a patient’s head and seven tamarisk or clay sages kneeling at the foot of the bed. Their purpose is purification and defense against disease-producing forces. This belongs to āšipūtu, the Mesopotamian exorcistic-healing discipline, in which ritual, medicine, omen interpretation, and theology were not separated according to modern categories. [Wiggermann](https://mc.dlib.nyu.edu/files/books/brill_awdl000079/brill_awdl000079_lo.pdf)

### Royal-palace use

Ashurnasirpal II’s Northwest Palace at Nimrud repeated winged figures beside the king, doorways, and stylized trees. The images protected the rooms while presenting imperial rule as ritually purified and cosmically ordered. Monumental palace reliefs and small buried figures participate in related ideology, but one should not assume that every winged court figure is an apkallu.

### Offerings, sacrifice, and disputed practices

No evidence associates the apkallū with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, compulsory sexual rites, or cult violence. Mesopotamian ritual texts prescribe figurines, incantations, purification materials, and ordinary offerings, not victims killed for the Seven. Broader claims about institutional “sacred prostitution” in Mesopotamia remain controversial and are not specifically connected to the apkallū.

### Politics and economics

The relevant institutional setting was the palace-temple scholarly establishment. Learned families controlled literacy, divination, medicine, exorcism, calendrics, and royal counsel. By tracing their arts to the apkallū, scholars represented professional knowledge as ancient, divinely licensed, and hereditary. The Uruk List’s colophon—naming its owner’s scholarly ancestry and prohibiting removal—materializes that claim.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Early third millennium BCE: term before mythology

*Abgal* appears as a title or profession. This establishes a social category of sage/priest but not yet a supernatural septad. The later Seven should not be labeled straightforwardly “Sumerian gods” on this evidence alone.

### Late third to early second millennium BCE: literary prestige of sages

Sumerian literary traditions make sages, expert priests, and divine wisdom part of Enki’s ordered world. Old Babylonian schools preserve and reformulate much earlier literature. Collective “Seven Sages” references appear, but the complete named system remains absent.

### Middle Babylonian/Kassite period: Adapa and fish imagery

The Amarna Adapa manuscript proves international transmission of an Eridu wisdom narrative by the fourteenth century BCE. Kassite seals attest fish-cloaked figures. These are major components of the later system, though their precise relationship at this stage is not fully recoverable.

### Ninth–seventh centuries BCE: Assyrian monumental and ritual peak

The Neo-Assyrian Empire gives apkallu imagery its greatest surviving visibility:

- monumental palace reliefs;
- temple and palace entrance figures;
- wall paintings, seals, ivory, and metalwork;
- foundation deposits;
- ritual series such as *Bīt mēseri*;
- scholarly claims of descent from primordial sages.

This is the defensible “peak,” measured by surviving representations and systematic ritual texts, not by evidence of mass popular devotion.

### Early first millennium BCE: literary canonization

*Erra and Išum*, the Catalogue of Texts and Authors, exorcistic manuals, and sage lists integrate the apkallū into histories of the Flood, cult-image care, and scholarly transmission. The distinction between antediluvian apkallū, post-Flood hybrid sages, and historical *ummânū* becomes ideologically productive.

### Neo-Babylonian and Achaemenid periods

Fish-cloaked and related protective beings remain in glyptic and architectural ritual, although Neo-Assyrian bird-headed monumental types become less prominent. Babylonian scholarly families continue copying ritual and historiographic traditions.

### Seleucid Uruk

W 20030,7 and late *Bīt mēseri* manuscripts show that priestly families of Hellenistic Uruk were actively maintaining, reordering, and claiming descent from ancient scholarship. This was continuity through temple schools, not fossilized survival.

### Berossus and Greek transmission

Berossus translates Babylonian primeval scholarship into Greek universal history. Oannes becomes an emblem of Babylonian antiquity and cultural priority. Because Berossus survives only through later excerptors, wording, sequence, and names passed through multiple editorial layers.

### Late antiquity and medieval transmission

Christian chronographers preserved Berossus because his antediluvian kings and Flood could be compared with biblical chronology. Oannes survived as an antiquarian figure, not through a continuous cult. There is no documented medieval community worshipping the apkallū.

Mesopotamian temple institutions progressively disappeared under Hellenistic, Parthian, Roman, Christian, and later Islamic transformations. No specific decree suppressing “the cult of the Apkallu” is known because there was no autonomous cult to abolish.

### Nineteenth-century rediscovery

Excavations at Nimrud, Nineveh, Assur, and Babylon recovered reliefs and figurines. At first, winged figures were broadly called “genii,” gods, demons, or priests. Cuneiform ritual texts eventually permitted more precise—though still debated—identification.

George Smith’s nineteenth-century report on the Nimrud Temple V deposit documented clay figures in a brick receptacle. Subsequent editions by Reiner, Borger, Rittig, Wiggermann, Green, Dalley, and others connected the archaeological types with ritual terminology.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Modern scholarship increasingly treats the apkallu as a meeting point of:

- scribal social history;
- antediluvian historiography;
- apotropaic ritual;
- royal iconography;
- transmission into Berossus;
- possible Jewish counter-traditions.

Modern Mesopotamian reconstructionists sometimes honor the Seven, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes the scale of such worship. It is a revival based on published texts, not demonstrable institutional continuity from antiquity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Early-modern syncretism

Seventeenth-century antiquarians, notably **Athanasius Kircher**, placed Oannes within universal systems linking Egyptian, biblical, Phoenician, and classical religion. Oannes was equated with Dagon or treated as an Osirian fish figure. These combinations reflect early-modern comparative theology; they are not recoveries of Babylonian doctrine.

### Theosophy

Helena Blavatsky and later Theosophical writers made Oannes and the *Annedoti* initiates, adepts, “water dragons,” or equivalents of Indian Nāgas. The amphibious form was allegorized as existence on spiritual and physical planes. This is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century esoteric reinterpretation. [Theosophical glossary](https://www.theosociety.org/pasadena/etgloss/oa-oz.htm)

There is no evidence that Mesopotamian sources identified the apkallū with Nāgas, Atlanteans, root races, or Theosophical masters.

### Occult and magical revival

Modern ceremonial-magical and Luciferian texts sometimes use “Apkallu” as the title of an initiator, guardian, or personal source of antinomian wisdom. These systems selectively reuse Assyrian images and academic translations but create new theology. They should not be cited as evidence for ancient practice.

### Enoch, angels, and demonology

Academic proposals that Enochic Watchers invert apkallu traditions have entered religious and occult publishing. Popular retellings often turn a nuanced intertextual hypothesis into “the Apkallu were the fallen angels of Genesis.” That direct identity is not established. The cuneiform sages do not rebel collectively, mate with women in a surviving narrative, or father giants.

### Ancient-astronaut literature

The most widespread modern reinvention identifies winged apkallu reliefs as:

- Anunnaki astronauts;
- extraterrestrial genetic engineers;
- survivors of Atlantis;
- bearers of electrical “pinecone technology”;
- figures carrying technological “handbags”;
- worldwide teachers connected to Göbekli Tepe, Olmec art, or other unrelated cultures.

These claims depend on visual resemblance and modern speculation. The Assyrian object is textually identified as the **banduddû**, a ritual bucket, while the accompanying **mullilu** is a purifier. Archaeological deposits and ritual prescriptions explain the figures without extraterrestrial technology. The equation **apkallu = Anunnaki** is likewise false as ancient terminology.

### National and political reception

Assyrian reliefs have become symbols of ancient Iraqi and Assyrian heritage. Their use in museums, diaspora art, tattoos, state imagery, and commercial design usually emphasizes civilization, protection, and cultural antiquity. Such use is modern reception, not necessarily religious revival.

The Islamic State’s destruction at Nimrud and the Mosul Museum in 2015 attacked ancient Mesopotamian heritage generally. It was not a historically continuous conflict with apkallu worship, although apkallu-related Assyrian material formed part of the endangered corpus.

### Popular culture

Fish-men, winged sages, and Oannes have influenced fantasy art, games, role-playing bestiaries, comics, and occult fiction. Many works use the name for generic aquatic monsters or angelic guardians, severed from the ancient scholarly and apotropaic setting. No single modern franchise has established a universally dominant image comparable to the ancient-astronaut “handbag” meme.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major discoveries and studies

- **Nineteenth-century Nimrud excavations:** brought monumental winged figures and subfloor clay deposits to scholarly attention.
- **Erich Ebeling, René Labat, Erica Reiner, Rykle Borger:** published and reconstructed ritual and sage-list texts.
- **J. J. A. van Dijk:** published W 20030,7 after its 1959–60 discovery at Uruk.
- **Anne Draffkorn Kilmer:** compared post-Flood sages and biblical Nephilim.
- **F. A. M. Wiggermann:** established the foundational modern synthesis of apotropaic figurine texts and iconography.
- **Stephanie Dalley:** refined the iconographic catalogue and emphasized uncertainty.
- **Alan Lenzi:** interpreted apkallu genealogy in the sociology of secret scholarly knowledge.
- **Helge Kvanvig and Amar Annus:** developed comparisons with Enochic antediluvian traditions.
- **Eckart Frahm and Enrique Jiménez:** produced a new edition of LKA 76 and clarified the post-Flood sage episodes.

### Central controversies

#### Was Adapa the first apkallu?

**For:** later text catalogues join Uan and Adapa; both belong to Eridu and Ea, embody transcendent wisdom, and correspond well to Berossus’s Oannes.

**Against simple identity:** the fourteenth-century Adapa myth does not enumerate seven sages; W 20030,7 writes Uan without simply spelling “Adapa”; first-millennium texts inconsistently place Adapa and Nungalpiriggal with Enmerkar.

**Best conclusion:** Uanna–Adapa–Oannes is a powerful first-millennium/Hellenistic scholarly identification, not a fact securely recoverable for the earliest Adapa tradition.

#### Are palace figures definitely apkallū?

Buried figurines accompanied by ritual parallels are secure identifications. Monumental reliefs without labels are less certain. Human-winged figures may be apkallū, other genii, or minor deities; individual attributes and placement must be considered.

#### What is the cone?

Conifer cone, date-palm inflorescence, ritual sprinkler, and symbolic purifier have all been proposed. The associated Akkadian term and purification context are stronger than claims that the scene depicts literal agricultural pollination.

#### Were the sages gods, humans, or demons?

All three answers flatten the evidence:

- antediluvian sages are supernatural beings;
- *abgal/apkallu* can title humans and gods;
- post-Flood sages are human-descended;
- apotropaic images have powers comparable to protective spirits;
- they are not normally hostile demons.

#### How old is the canonical list?

The concept of exceptional sages is early. Fish-associated divine wisdom is at least second-millennium. The complete list of seven names is securely documented only in first-millennium witnesses. Wiggermann’s linguistic analysis indicates learned composition and reinterpretation rather than unchanged third-millennium inheritance.

#### Apkallu and Watchers

Annus argues for deliberate Jewish counter-narrative: positive Mesopotamian antediluvian revealers become illicit heavenly teachers. Kvanvig similarly sees strong structural inheritance. Critics caution that shared Flood chronology, groups of ancient sages, and revealed knowledge do not prove direct dependence in every detail. The hypothesis is influential, not unanimous. [Kvanvig, *Primeval History*](https://books.google.com/books?id=rOF5DwAAQBAJ)

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- The reliefs depict extraterrestrial Anunnaki.
- The bucket is a machine, battery, DNA container, or spacecraft control.
- Apkallū built Göbekli Tepe or taught Olmec civilization.
- They were universally worshipped across the ancient world.
- All cultures’ groups of seven sages derive from Mesopotamia.
- Berossus preserved a literal eyewitness history of alien intervention.
- Apkallū were dinosaurs, reptilians, or genetically engineered hybrids.
- Adapa is securely the biblical Adam.
- The Seven are straightforwardly the seven Watchers or seven archangels.

None is established by primary evidence.

### State of the evidence

Relevant tablets and objects are dispersed among:

- Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin;
- British Museum, London;
- Iraq Museum and site collections;
- Metropolitan Museum of Art;
- Yale Babylonian Collection and Cuneiform Commentaries Project;
- Istanbul collections;
- university museums holding divided Nimrud reliefs and excavation finds.

Many texts are fragmentary, unprovenanced, known through duplicates, or preserved only in late copies. Berossus survives solely through quotation and epitome. Modern reconstruction must therefore remain openly provisional.
