This document distinguishes the Hebrew *śāṭān*, later Jewish adversarial figures, the Christian Devil, and Qurʾānic Iblīs/al-Shayṭān rather than treating them as one timeless personality. “Satan” below denotes the figure only where a source itself uses that name or where an explicitly identified later tradition equates another figure with Satan.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Satan is not attested as an independently worshipped ancient West Asian god with temples, priests, or a stable genealogy. The earliest relevant evidence is the Hebrew common noun שָׂטָן, *śāṭān*, “adversary, opponent, accuser,” applied to human and celestial opponents. In Numbers 22 the angel of YHWH acts “as an adversary”; in Zechariah 3 and Job 1–2, **the** satan, with the definite article, is a member or functionary of YHWH’s court. Only later does the term increasingly behave like the designation of a particular supernatural enemy.

During the Second Temple period, Jewish texts developed several non-identical chief opponents—Mastema, Belial/Beliar, the Angel of Darkness, the evil spirit, Satan, and fallen Watcher leaders. Some were subsequently assimilated to Satan, but they were not originally interchangeable. Early Christianity consolidated several inherited motifs into “Satan/the Devil”: tempter of Jesus, ruler of demons, deceiver, persecutor, cosmic dragon, ancient serpent, and eschatologically defeated enemy. Patristic interpretation further read Isaiah’s fallen morning star and Ezekiel’s prince or cherub of Tyre as Satan’s primordial fall. This produced the Christian “Lucifer” biography, although neither Hebrew passage names Satan.

The Qurʾān, composed and proclaimed in seventh-century Arabic, uses both **Iblīs** and **al-Shayṭān**. Iblīs refuses God’s command to prostrate before Adam, claims superiority because he was made from fire, is cursed, receives respite until Judgment, and vows to mislead humanity. Qurʾān 18:50 explicitly calls him “one of the jinn,” while the prostration narratives place him among those addressed with the angels; exegetes consequently disputed whether he was an angel, a jinn temporarily admitted among angels, or an exceptional being. The Qurʾān also uses *shayṭān/shayāṭīn* generically for malign human or jinn adversaries. The Garden narrative shifts from the proper designation Iblīs at 2:34 to al-Shayṭān at 2:36, helping later readers identify them. See the source-oriented synthesis in the [St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Islam/Iblis) and the searchable [Qurʾānic Arabic Corpus](https://corpus.quran.com/search.jsp?q=con%3Asatan).

### Classification by tradition

- **Hebrew Bible:** human adversary, obstructing angel, or celestial prosecutorial figure subordinate to YHWH; not securely an independent god or rebel ruler.
- **Second Temple Jewish texts:** no single classification. Satan-like beings may be accusers, princes of hostile spirits, leaders of cosmic lots, or fallen angels.
- **Rabbinic Judaism:** accuser, tempter, evil inclination, and angel of death can be identified functionally; Satan remains subordinate to God. Bava Batra 16a says, “Satan, the evil inclination, and the Angel of Death are one,” meaning three functions or aspects in that discussion, not necessarily a universally fixed ontology ([Sefaria text](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.16)).
- **Christianity:** a personal fallen angel, chief of demons and cosmic opponent, created good but made evil by free choice; powerful but finite. The Catholic formulation is explicit in Catechism §§391–395 ([Vatican text](https://www.vatican.va/archive/catechism_sp/p1s2c1p7_sp.html)).
- **Islam:** Iblīs is explicitly “of the jinn” in Qurʾān 18:50, yet stands amid the angelic prostration scene. He becomes al-Shayṭān, humanity’s tempter; *shayāṭīn* form a wider class.
- **Modern symbolic Satanism:** Satan is commonly a literary symbol of autonomy, carnality, inquiry, or resistance rather than a supernatural being.
- **Modern theistic Satanisms:** Satan, Lucifer, Set, or related figures may be treated as real spirits or gods, but these are diverse modern constructions, not demonstrably continuous ancient cults.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest securely recoverable evidence

There is no securely identified ancient inscription, temple, or image of a god named Satan predating the biblical texts. The earliest evidence is literary and lexical.

1. **Numbers 22:22, 32.** The angel of YHWH stands in Balaam’s road לְשָׂטָן לוֹ, “as an adversary against him.” This is probably among the oldest literary uses, though the dating of Pentateuchal strata remains contested. It does not describe a being named Satan; YHWH’s angel performs the adversarial role.

2. **1 Samuel 29:4 and 1 Kings 5:4; 11:14, 23, 25.** Human political or military opponents are *śāṭān*. These uses establish the word’s ordinary semantic range.

3. **Zechariah 3:1–2.** Usually situated in the early Persian period, with Zechariah’s dated visions beginning in 520 BCE, this is the earliest relatively datable scene of **the satan** as a heavenly accuser. Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of YHWH while the satan stands at his right to accuse him; YHWH rebukes the satan. The passage says nothing about a primordial rebellion, hell, horns, or rule over demons.

4. **Job 1–2.** The prose frame is difficult to date; major proposals range from the Persian into the early Hellenistic period, while its traditions may be older. The satan appears “among the sons of God,” reports roaming the earth, questions Job’s disinterested piety, and receives bounded permission to destroy his property, children, and health. He cannot exceed God’s authorization. The figure disappears after chapter 2 and is not condemned in the epilogue.

5. **1 Chronicles 21:1.** Usually dated to the Persian or early Hellenistic period. “A satan/Satan” incites David to number Israel. The absence of the article can indicate movement toward a personal designation, but a human adversary remains grammatically possible. Its significance is sharpened by 2 Samuel 24:1, where YHWH’s anger incites David. Chronicles may redistribute agency away from God, but the historical and theological mechanism is debated. The [NET textual note](https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?passage=1Ch+21%3A1) presents the alternative reading.

The consonantal texts survive in medieval Masoretic manuscripts, but Hebrew biblical witnesses are older: Dead Sea manuscripts, the Greek Septuagint, Samaritan Pentateuch where applicable, and other versions attest textual transmission. Physical manuscript dates therefore must not be confused with dates of composition.

Second Temple evidence is notably plural. A major recent historical analysis concludes that the period contained texts with no chief supernatural opponent, muted or abstract opponents, and fully mythological opponents; there was no single linear consensus inherited unchanged by Christianity ([Journal of Theological Studies](https://academic.oup.com/jts/article/70/1/21/5404472?guestAccessKey=c37805a7-8042-4cf5-9105-b26db54f1198)).

### New Testament corpus

The critically edited Greek base is available through the [SBL Greek New Testament](https://www.sbl-site.org/resources/digital-texts/sbl-greek-new-testament/). Principal appearances include:

- **Mark, ca. 65–75 CE:** Satan tempts Jesus (1:13), is invoked in the Beelzebul controversy (3:22–27), snatches the word (4:15), receives Jesus’ rebuke when Peter resists the Passion (8:33), and is associated with eschatological deception (13:22–23).
- **Matthew, ca. 80–90:** expands the temptation dialogue (4:1–11), calls Satan the enemy who sows weeds (13:25–39), repeats the Peter rebuke (16:23), and depicts eternal fire prepared for “the devil and his angels” (25:41).
- **Luke–Acts, ca. 80–100:** the Devil offers Jesus authority over kingdoms (Luke 4:1–13); Satan binds a woman (13:16), enters Judas (22:3), demands to sift the disciples (22:31), and fills Ananias’ heart (Acts 5:3). Conversion transfers people from Satan’s power to God (Acts 26:18).
- **John, ca. 90–110:** uses “devil,” “evil one,” and “ruler of this world”; the Devil is father of lies and murderer “from the beginning” (8:44), inspires or enters Judas (13:2, 27), and is judged (12:31; 16:11).
- **Pauline letters, ca. 50s onward:** Satan tempts, obstructs, disguises himself as an angel of light, may receive an offender for corrective destruction, and is promised defeat under believers’ feet (1 Thessalonians 2:18; 3:5; 1 Corinthians 5:5; 7:5; 2 Corinthians 2:11; 11:14; 12:7; Romans 16:20).
- **Deutero-Pauline/Pastoral letters:** “ruler of the power of the air” and cosmic powers in Ephesians are traditionally Satanic; 1 Timothy refers to Satan’s condemnation and snare.
- **Hebrews 2:14:** the Devil possesses “the power of death,” which Christ destroys.
- **James 4:7; 1 Peter 5:8:** believers resist the Devil; he prowls like a roaring lion.
- **1 John:** the Devil sins from the beginning; Cain is “of the evil one”; the Son destroys the Devil’s works.
- **Jude 9:** Michael and the Devil dispute over Moses’ body.
- **Revelation, late first century:** Satan is dragon, ancient serpent, Devil, deceiver, accuser, persecutor, and empowering force behind the beasts. Michael casts him from heaven; he is bound, released, and finally thrown into the lake of fire (Revelation 12; 13; 20).

The New Testament does not supply one sequential account of Satan’s origin. The familiar biography—highest angel, prideful revolt, expulsion, Edenic temptation, rule in hell—results from later synthesis of separate passages and noncanonical traditions.

### Early Christian and medieval doctrinal corpus

- **Ignatius of Antioch, early second century:** employs “prince of this age” language for the hostile power opposing Christian unity.
- **Justin Martyr, mid-second century:** explicitly identifies the serpent with the Devil and treats pagan gods as demons.
- **Irenaeus, ca. 180:** develops Adam/Christ recapitulation and the Devil’s deception, while insisting the Devil is a creature rather than an independent principle.
- **Tertullian, ca. 200:** applies Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28 typologically to the Devil.
- **Origen, third century:** gives the most influential learned synthesis of Isaiah’s fallen morning star and Ezekiel’s proud ruler with Satan’s fall, while speculating on rational creatures’ prehistory. His surviving discussions are embedded in a complex Greek/Latin transmission; see *Contra Celsum* [Book VI](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/04166.htm).
- **Augustine, late fourth–early fifth century:** locates the angelic fall in evil will and pride, rejects any evil substance coeternal with God, and reads Isaiah’s king of Babylon as a figure of the Devil. See *City of God* XI.15 ([primary text](https://www.logoslibrary.org/augustine/city/1115.html)).
- **Fourth Lateran Council, 1215:** dogmatically states that the Devil and demons were created naturally good but became evil through themselves. This remains central to Catholic teaching.
- **Thomas Aquinas, thirteenth century:** systematizes angelic intellect, will, fall, hierarchy, temptation, and demonic limitations in the *Summa theologiae*.
- **Dante, *Commedia*, ca. 1308–1321:** freezes Lucifer at hell’s center as a gigantic three-faced traitor chewing Judas, Brutus, and Cassius. This is literary-theological construction, not biblical geography.
- **Witchcraft treatises:** the *Malleus maleficarum* (1486–1487) and related demonologies systematized alleged pacts, sabbaths, sexual demons, and maleficium. Confessions were often obtained under coercion or torture; they cannot be treated straightforwardly as testimony to an organized Satan cult.
- **Milton, *Paradise Lost*, 1667/1674:** supplies the most influential English literary personality: rhetorically grand rebel, war leader, infernal strategist, serpent-tempter, and self-corrupted tyrant.

### Islamic corpus

- **Qurʾān, early seventh century:** Iblīs occurs in 2:34; 7:11; 15:31–32; 17:61; 18:50; 20:116; 26:95; 34:20; 38:74–75. Prostration cycles also occur at 7:11–18, 15:28–43, 17:61–65, 20:116–123, and 38:71–85. Satan whispers, frightens, promises falsely, makes deeds attractive, causes forgetfulness, and disowns followers at Judgment.
- **Hadith:** expands Satan’s activity into prayer, dreams, eating, sexual relations, birth, social discord, and daily vulnerability; authenticity grades and collections must be cited passage by passage rather than treated as one undifferentiated corpus.
- **Al-Ṭabarī, d. 923:** preserves competing origin stories, including Iblīs as a jinn or as a heavenly being connected with angelic ranks.
- **Al-Thaʿlabī, d. 1035, and later *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*:** elaborate prehistory, names, forms, and encounters far beyond the Qurʾān.
- **Al-Ḥallāj, d. 922, *Kitāb al-Ṭawāsīn*:** presents Iblīs paradoxically as an uncompromising monotheist who refuses to bow to another; this is mystical reinterpretation, not the Qurʾān’s plain evaluative stance. Its influence is summarized by [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hallaj/).
- **Aḥmad al-Ghazālī, ʿAyn al-Quḍāt, Rūzbihān Baqlī, ʿAṭṭār:** continue aspects of the tragic-lover or uncompromising-monotheist Iblīs.
- **Rūmī, thirteenth century:** often uses Iblīs as the paradigmatic sophist whose self-justifying intelligence conceals pride; he should not be homogenized with al-Ḥallāj’s treatment.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic actions

Satan’s domain changes with the corpus:

- **Courtroom accusation:** Zechariah 3; Job 1–2; Revelation 12:10.
- **Testing under authorization:** Job 1–2; later Jubilees assigns comparable testing to Mastema.
- **Incitement:** 1 Chronicles 21:1; Acts 5:3.
- **Temptation and scriptural disputation:** Matthew 4 and Luke 4.
- **Demonic rule:** Mark 3 and parallels; Matthew 25:41.
- **Obstruction of preaching:** 1 Thessalonians 2:18; Mark 4:15.
- **Disease or affliction:** Job 2; Luke 13:16; 2 Corinthians 12:7.
- **Deception and counterfeit revelation:** John 8:44; 2 Corinthians 11:14; Revelation 12–13.
- **Political empire:** the Devil offers worldly kingdoms in Luke 4; Revelation presents the dragon as empowering imperial beasts.
- **Death:** Hebrews 2:14; Bava Batra 16a identifies Satan with the angel of death.
- **Whispering:** Qurʾān 7:20; 20:120; 114:4–6.
- **False promise and beautification of wrongdoing:** Qurʾān 4:120; 8:48; 14:22; 16:63.
- **Limited agency:** Job’s satan requires permission; the Synoptics anticipate Satan’s defeat; Revelation fixes his final destruction; Qurʾān 14:22 has Satan deny coercive power and admit he merely invited followers.

### Appearance

No Hebrew biblical text describes the satan’s bodily appearance. No biblical passage gives him horns, hooves, red skin, a pitchfork, or a goat’s head.

Important stages are:

1. **Serpent identification:** Genesis 3 calls the tempter a serpent, not Satan. Wisdom 2:24, the Life of Adam and Eve, John 8:44, and finally Revelation 12:9 and 20:2 enabled the explicit identification. Revelation’s “ancient serpent” may evoke Eden, Leviathan, or both.

2. **Angel of light:** 2 Corinthians 11:14 says Satan disguises himself as an angel of light. The text describes deception, not necessarily his original or permanent appearance.

3. **Dragon:** Revelation 12 portrays a great red dragon with seven heads, ten horns, and seven diadems. The imagery overlaps Danielic imperial beasts and ancient chaos combat. It is apocalyptic symbolism, not a neutral anatomical description.

4. **Ravenna angel:** The sheep-and-goats mosaic at Sant’Apollinare Nuovo, ca. 520, places a blue or dark angel beside the goats opposite a red angel beside the sheep. It is often called the earliest secure Christian representation of Satan, though the figure is not inscribed with his name and the identification depends on composition and color. The Italian medieval-art survey [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/diavolo_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Medievale%29/) records the conventional identification.

5. **Medieval hybrid monster:** horns, tails, claws, animal ears, hairy bodies, additional faces, dark coloring, and bat-like wings accumulate between late antiquity and the high Middle Ages. These draw on classical satyrs/Pan, grotesques, personifications, apocalyptic beasts, and visual inversion of angelic beauty. No single pagan god simply “became Satan.”

6. **Dantean Lucifer:** three faces parody the Trinity; six bat wings invert seraphic wings; beating wings freeze Cocytus. The three mouths eternally chew archetypal traitors.

7. **Renaissance and Romantic angel:** artists increasingly restore idealized human anatomy and wings. Blake, Fuseli, Cabanel, and the Geefs brothers make Lucifer beautiful, muscular, sorrowful, or sublime.

8. **Goat-headed Baphomet:** Éliphas Lévi designed the “Sabbatic Goat” in *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie* (1854–1856) as an occult emblem of reconciled opposites. LaVey’s Church of Satan later adopted a goat head within an inverted pentagram. Claims that this exact emblem is an ancient Satanic symbol are false.

### Symbols and alleged correspondences

- **Serpent and dragon:** scripturally grounded, but their fusion with Satan is later than Genesis.
- **Lion:** 1 Peter 5:8 uses a predatory simile.
- **Goat:** Matthew 25 separates goats from sheep but does not say Satan is a goat. Goat anatomy becomes iconographic through later demonization and occult art.
- **Pitchfork/trident:** medieval and early-modern infernal art, perhaps influenced by instruments of punishment and classical marine or chthonic deities; not scriptural.
- **Inverted pentagram:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult/Satanic codification, not an ancient biblical attribute.
- **Number 666:** Revelation 13:18 assigns 666 to the Beast, not directly to Satan. Treating “666” as Satan’s personal number is popular conflation.
- **Friday, black/red, sulphur, particular planets or metals:** vary across grimoires and modern occult systems; there is no universal ancient Satanic correspondence table.
- **Hell:** Christian tradition calls Satan hell’s ruler, but several canonical texts portray final fire as his punishment, not his sovereign kingdom (Matthew 25:41; Revelation 20:10).
- **Satanic throne:** Revelation 2:13 locates “Satan’s throne” at Pergamum, a polemical designation whose referent is disputed—imperial cult, local opposition, monumental altar, or a broader concentration of hostile power.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin traditions

Satan has no securely attested parentage, mother, consort, or biological offspring in the Hebrew Bible, New Testament, or Qurʾān.

Competing origin models include:

| Tradition | Account |
|---|---|
| Job and Zechariah | The satan appears among the heavenly court or before YHWH. No creation or fall is narrated. |
| Watcher traditions | Rebellious angels descend, mate with women, teach forbidden arts, and produce giants; evil spirits arise from dead giants. The principal leaders are Semihazah and Azazel, not consistently Satan. |
| Life of Adam and Eve | Satan falls after refusing angelic veneration of newly created Adam, often from envy. |
| Patristic Christianity | Satan was created a good angel and fell through pride, envy, or desire for divine equality; Isaiah 14, Ezekiel 28, Luke 10:18, and Revelation 12 are synthesized. |
| Augustine and scholastic Christianity | The Devil’s evil has no evil creator or substance; it is the privation produced by a created will turning from the highest good. |
| Qurʾān | Iblīs refuses prostration because fire is superior to clay, is cursed, and receives respite. Qurʾān 18:50 identifies him as jinn. |
| Later Islamic narratives | Iblīs may have been named ʿAzāzīl, may have belonged to a class of earthly jinn captured or elevated by angels, or may have held exalted heavenly office. These details are exegetical and folkloric, not stated in the Qurʾān. |
| Kabbalistic traditions | Samael is paired with Lilith and embedded in the “other side.” This is medieval systematization rather than biblical genealogy. |
| Milton | Satan produces Sin from his head; Satan and Sin beget Death, and their incestuous cycle generates infernal hounds. This is a seventeenth-century allegorical genealogy. |

### Named interactions

The following records the principal discrete interactions in foundational Jewish, Christian, Islamic, and culturally decisive reception texts. Analogues such as Mastema and Belial are labeled as such instead of silently renamed Satan.

#### YHWH/God

- **Balaam-road adversary:** The angel of YHWH stands as a *satan* against Balaam; Numbers 22:22, 32. **Tier: early lexical attestation, not personal Satan.**
- **Joshua’s hearing:** The satan prosecutes Joshua; YHWH rebukes him; Zechariah 3:1–2, early Persian period. **Tier: earliest securely contextualized celestial accuser.**
- **Job’s testing:** The satan challenges Job’s motives, receives limited authority from YHWH, and afflicts him; Job 1–2, Persian/Hellenistic dating debated. **Tier: early canonical.**
- **David’s census:** Satan/an adversary incites David in 1 Chronicles 21:1, whereas YHWH does so in 2 Samuel 24:1. **Tier: canonical conflict of attribution.**
- **War and expulsion:** Revelation’s dragon opposes God’s purposes and is cast down; late first century. **Tier: Christian canonical apocalypse.**
- **Final judgment:** God’s fire defeats Satan and the lake of fire receives him; Revelation 20. **Tier: Christian canonical eschatology.**
- **Iblīs’s refusal and reprieve:** God commands prostration before Adam, questions Iblīs, curses him, and grants respite; Qurʾān 7, 15, 17, 38. **Tier: Islamic canonical.**
- **Heavenly dialogue about Job/Ayyūb:** Qurʾān 38:41 has Job say Satan afflicted him; elaborate dialogues modeled on biblical Job belong to later *tafsīr* and prophets’ tales. **Tier: Qurʾānic allusion plus later elaboration.**

#### Angel of YHWH

- The satan stands beside Joshua before the angel of YHWH; Zechariah 3:1–5. The angel participates in the restoration of Joshua after YHWH rebukes the accuser. **Tier: early canonical.**

#### Jesus Christ

- Satan tempts Jesus after the Spirit leads him into the wilderness; Mark 1:12–13, expanded in Matthew 4:1–11 and Luke 4:1–13. **Tier: Christian canonical, with Synoptic variants.**
- Jesus rejects Satan’s offers using Deuteronomy; Matthew and Luke reverse the order of the last two temptations. **Tier: canonical variant.**
- Jesus describes Satan’s kingdom and the binding of the strong man while answering the Beelzebul accusation; Mark 3:22–27 and parallels. **Tier: Christian canonical.**
- Jesus says he saw Satan fall like lightning; Luke 10:18. Whether this refers to primordial fall, defeat during the disciples’ mission, or anticipated eschatological defeat is disputed. **Tier: Christian canonical.**
- Jesus calls Peter “Satan” when Peter opposes the Passion; Mark 8:33 and Matthew 16:23. Peter is acting as an adversary; this need not mean possession. **Tier: Christian canonical.**
- Satan demands to sift Simon Peter, while Jesus intercedes; Luke 22:31–32. **Tier: Christian canonical.**
- Satan enters Judas; Luke 22:3 and John 13:27. **Tier: Christian canonical.**
- The ruler of this world approaches and is judged/cast out through Jesus’ death; John 12:31; 14:30; 16:11. **Tier: Christian canonical Johannine theology.**
- The Son of God appears to destroy the Devil’s works; 1 John 3:8. **Tier: Christian canonical.**
- Christ defeats the one holding death’s power; Hebrews 2:14. **Tier: Christian canonical.**

#### Holy Spirit

- The Spirit drives or leads Jesus into the wilderness where Satan/the Devil tests him; Mark 1:12, Matthew 4:1, Luke 4:1. The Spirit and Satan do not converse directly. **Tier: Christian canonical joint scene.**

#### Michael

- Michael disputes with the Devil over Moses’ body and refuses a self-authorized condemnation, saying “The Lord rebuke you”; Jude 9, late first century, drawing on a lost Moses tradition. **Tier: Christian canonical use of nonextant Jewish narrative.**
- Michael and his angels fight the dragon and his angels; Revelation 12:7–9. Satan is expelled to earth. **Tier: Christian canonical apocalypse.**
- Later art repeatedly depicts Michael spearing or chaining Satan. **Tier: late antique–modern iconographic elaboration.**

#### Gabriel

- No canonical biblical or Qurʾānic narrative contains a direct named Gabriel–Satan combat. Later angelological manuals may oppose them structurally, but a universal ancient interaction should not be invented.

#### Beelzebul

- Jesus’ opponents allege that he expels demons by Beelzebul; Jesus replies that if “Satan casts out Satan,” Satan’s kingdom is divided; Mark 3:22–27, Matthew 12:24–29, Luke 11:15–22. **Tier: Christian canonical functional identification or close equation.**

#### Beliar/Belial

- Christ and Beliar are antithetical in 2 Corinthians 6:15. They do not enact a narrative meeting. **Tier: Christian canonical polarity derived from Jewish Belial traditions.**
- In the Dead Sea Scrolls, Belial opposes the Prince of Light/Michael’s camp in an eschatological war. Identifying him simply as Satan is a comparative reconstruction. **Tier: Second Temple regional/sectarian analogue.**

#### Prince of Light / Angel of Darkness

- The Community Rule assigns humanity to two opposing spirits or lots, governed by the Prince of Light and Angel of Darkness; Belial is associated elsewhere with darkness. The exact relation among these titles is debated. **Tier: Qumran sectarian systematization.**

#### Mastema

- Mastema petitions God to preserve one-tenth of the spirits descended from the giants so that they can exercise authority before Judgment; Jubilees 10. **Tier: Second Temple Satan-like analogue.**
- Mastema urges or participates in Abraham’s testing; Jubilees 17–18. **Tier: Second Temple reinterpretation of Genesis.**
- Mastema seeks to kill Moses and assists Egyptian resistance; Jubilees 48. **Tier: Second Temple expansion.**
- Mastema should not be called Satan without qualification, although their roles overlap.

#### Samael

- Later Jewish texts make Samael the accuser, angel of death, or serpent’s supernatural operator. In Pirqe de-Rabbi Eliezer he participates in Edenic deception. **Tier: late antique/early medieval accretion.**
- Medieval Kabbalah pairs Samael with Lilith. **Tier: medieval learned systematization.**

#### Lilith

- No biblical text presents Lilith as Satan’s wife. Isaiah 34:14 contains *lilit*, probably a night creature or demon. The Satan–Lilith partnership belongs chiefly to medieval demonology and Kabbalah. **Tier: medieval accretion.**

#### Death and Sin

- In *Paradise Lost* II, Sin springs from Satan’s head; Satan fathers Death upon Sin; Death later confronts Satan and Sin intervenes. **Tier: 1667 literary invention, allegorically based on James 1:15 and classical mythology.**

#### Iblīs and the angels

- God orders the angels to prostrate; all do except Iblīs; Qurʾān 2:34 and parallels. The scene creates the angel/jinn classificatory problem but does not name an angel who fights Iblīs. **Tier: Islamic canonical.**
- Later narratives give Iblīs the pre-fall name ʿAzāzīl and angelic rank. **Tier: exegetical/folkloric accretion.**

#### Melek Ṭāʾūs

There is no sound basis for listing Melek Ṭāʾūs as Satan’s manifestation or relation. Outsiders’ equation of the Yazidi Peacock Angel with the Devil is a polemical misidentification that helped expose Yazidis to persecution. [Encyclopaedia Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/yazidis/yazidis-i-general-1/) explicitly characterizes the “devil-worshipper” description as erroneous.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The heavenly accuser and Job

**Earliest complete source:** Job 1–2.

The sons of God present themselves before YHWH, and the satan comes among them. Asked where he has been, he answers that he has roamed the earth. YHWH points to Job’s blamelessness. The satan argues that Job is pious only because divine protection has made him prosperous. YHWH permits an attack on Job’s possessions but forbids touching Job himself. Raiders, fire, and wind destroy Job’s wealth and children. Job mourns without charging God with wrongdoing.

At the second assembly, YHWH notes Job’s persistence and says the satan moved him against Job “without cause.” The satan answers “skin for skin” and demands access to Job’s body. Permission is granted, short of taking his life. The satan strikes Job with sores. He then disappears from the narrative.

The text does not say Satan rebels, lies, rules hell, or independently commands evil. Nor does it absolve God: YHWH authorizes the test and explicitly accepts causal responsibility in 2:3. Later retellings often magnify Satan’s autonomy or turn the story into a simple contest between good and evil.

### Joshua and the celestial prosecutor

**Source:** Zechariah 3, early Persian period.

Joshua the high priest stands in a heavenly judicial or investiture scene, wearing filthy garments. The satan stands at his right to accuse. YHWH rebukes the satan and calls Joshua “a brand plucked from the fire.” Joshua receives clean vestments and a restored priestly charge.

The political and cultic context is postexilic restoration. The satan may represent a heavenly prosecutor, opposition to Joshua’s legitimacy, or the unresolved pollution of exile. Persian administrative analogies have been proposed, but a direct derivation from an Achaemenid office has not been established. The study of the accuser against this administrative background is represented by Jason Silverman’s [Journal of Hebrew Scriptures article](https://jhsonline.org/index.php/jhs/article/download/29337/21357/77565).

### David’s census: shifting agency

**Sources:** 2 Samuel 24:1 and 1 Chronicles 21:1.

Samuel says YHWH’s anger incites David to number Israel; Chronicles says Satan/an adversary does so. Both lead to census, guilt, plague, and the selection of the future temple site.

This is one of the clearest cases in which later transmission appears to relocate troubling agency, but interpretation remains disputed because unarticulated “a satan” may be human or supernatural. The pair must not be harmonized by importing Job’s court scene unless explicitly identified as a theological reading.

### The Watchers and Satan’s supposed “fall”

**Sources:** Genesis 6:1–4; 1 Enoch 6–16; Jubilees.

Genesis briefly says sons of God took human women and produced mighty men. 1 Enoch expands this into a rebellion: Watchers descend, swear an oath, marry women, reveal forbidden arts, and generate violent giants. Azazel teaches weaponry and adornment; Semihazah leads the oath. After the giants die, their spirits become evil spirits.

This cycle profoundly influenced Christian demonology, Jude, and 2 Peter. It is nevertheless not originally the story of an angel named Lucifer leading one-third of heaven in rebellion. Later Christian synthesis merged Watcher traditions, Isaiah’s fallen star, Revelation’s dragon, and Satan.

### Satan’s refusal to honor Adam

**Earliest surviving developed source:** Life of Adam and Eve recensions, probably first centuries CE.

After Adam’s creation, Michael commands angels to honor God’s image. Satan refuses, claims precedence because he was made before Adam, and persuades subordinate angels to join him. Expelled from glory, he takes revenge by causing humanity’s fall, sometimes using the serpent as his instrument.

The story anticipates the Qurʾānic refusal narrative but differs significantly: the Qurʾān attributes the command directly to God, centers the contrast between fire and clay, and calls Iblīs one of the jinn. Direct literary dependence is possible but not demonstrable from extant evidence; both participate in a wider late antique Adamic tradition.

### Temptation of Jesus

**Sources:** Mark 1:12–13; Matthew 4:1–11; Luke 4:1–13.

Mark gives only the framework: the Spirit drives Jesus into the wilderness for forty days, Satan tests him, wild animals are present, and angels serve him. Matthew and Luke add three temptations: turn stones to bread; demonstrate divine protection using Psalm 91; obtain the world’s kingdoms through worship of the Devil.

Matthew ends at a high mountain; Luke ends at Jerusalem and says the Devil departed “until an opportune time.” Jesus answers from Deuteronomy. The episode casts Jesus as tested Israel and faithful son. The Devil’s claim to worldly authority is not explicitly endorsed by the narrator.

### Satan’s defeat in Jesus’ mission

The Synoptic Gospels organize exorcism as an assault on Satan’s kingdom. Jesus binds the strong man; disciples’ successful mission corresponds to Satan falling from heaven; healings release those Satan bound. In the Passion, Satan works through Judas and tests the disciples, yet the Passion becomes the means of his defeat. John recasts the same paradox as the judgment and expulsion of the world’s ruler.

### War in heaven

**Source:** Revelation 12, late first century.

A woman clothed with the sun gives birth to a male child. A seven-headed red dragon waits to devour him. The child is taken to God; the woman flees. Michael and his angels fight the dragon and his angels. The dragon—explicitly “ancient serpent,” “Devil,” and “Satan”—is thrown to earth. Heaven celebrates the accuser’s expulsion, but the earth is warned because his remaining time is short. The dragon pursues the woman and wars against her offspring.

The chapter’s sequence does not straightforwardly describe an event before human creation: it interweaves messianic birth, persecution, celestial battle, and eschatology. Patristic and later readers relocated it into a primordial angelic rebellion. That interpretation became dominant but is not the only possible historical reading.

### Binding and final destruction

**Source:** Revelation 20.

An angel binds the dragon for a thousand years. After release, Satan deceives Gog and Magog and surrounds the saints. Divine fire consumes the army; the Devil is thrown into the lake of fire with the Beast and false prophet.

Christian millennial interpretations differ sharply: premillennial readers place the binding in a future kingdom; amillennial traditions interpret it through the Church age; other readings treat the millennium symbolically. The text makes hell Satan’s final prison, not his kingdom.

### Iblīs and Adam

**Sources:** Qurʾān 2, 7, 15, 17, 18, 20, 38.

God creates Adam from clay and commands the angels to prostrate. Iblīs refuses through pride: he is made of fire and Adam of clay. God curses or expels him. Iblīs requests respite until resurrection; God grants it. Iblīs vows to approach humans from every direction and mislead all except God’s sincere servants. God declares that hell will be filled with him and his followers.

In the Garden, al-Shayṭān whispers to Adam and his spouse, promising immortality or an imperishable kingdom and exposing their nakedness. Unlike much Christian theology, the Qurʾān does not teach hereditary original sin; Adam repents and receives guidance.

### Iblīs as tragic monotheist

**Source:** al-Ḥallāj’s *Ṭawāsīn*, early tenth century; later Persian mystical reception.

Iblīs refuses to bow to Adam because he will bow only to God. His disobedience becomes paradoxically inseparable from exclusive devotion. This does not reverse the Qurʾānic curse into a mainstream Islamic sainthood. It is a daring mystical meditation on command, love, pride, and unity. Later writers alternately admired the paradox or treated Iblīs’s argument as the supreme example of self-deception.

### The Faustian pact

Medieval and early-modern Christian literature developed written or spoken contracts in which a person bargains with the Devil for knowledge, sex, power, or wealth. The historical Faust chapbook appeared in 1587; Christopher Marlowe dramatized it around 1592; Goethe’s *Faust* transformed the tradition from 1808 onward.

The pact became important in witch trials, but judicial allegations are not evidence of an ancient organized Satan cult. Coercive interrogation, demonological expectations, local conflict, and confessional politics structured testimony.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Absence of an ancient Satan cult

No securely documented Israelite, Second Temple Jewish, early Christian, or early Islamic temple was dedicated to Satan as a god. No securely identified ancient priesthood, sacrificial calendar, theophoric naming system, or civic festival belongs to him.

The absence is structurally significant: Satan is normally accused, resisted, expelled, cursed, or invoked as the opponent against which legitimate worship is defined. His evidentiary record is therefore largely textual, iconographic, judicial, apotropaic, and polemical.

### Apotropaic and exorcistic practice

- **Second Temple Judaism:** Qumran compositions curse Belial and hostile spirits; psalms and incantatory compositions invoke divine or angelic protection.
- **Early Christianity:** baptismal rites came to include renunciation of Satan, sometimes facing west, followed by profession of faith while facing east.
- **Exorcism:** developed liturgical forms command demons in the name of Christ. The rites presuppose Satan’s hostile kingdom but are directed toward expulsion, not cult.
- **Islam:** reciters seek refuge in God from *al-shayṭān al-rajīm*. Qurʾān 113–114 are especially apotropaic; the Throne Verse and other passages are also used protectively. The St Andrews study notes the ritual importance of the “Two Prayers of Refuge” ([Iblīs study](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Islam/Iblis.pdf)).
- **Amulets and magic:** Jewish, Christian, and Islamic amulets sometimes name demons to bind or repel them. A hostile name on an amulet does not establish worship.

### Black Mass and alleged Devil worship

The “Black Mass” existed first largely as an inversion imagined by Christian polemic and demonology. Some early-modern libertine, magical, or anticlerical performances may have deliberately parodied the Mass, but sensational allegations—especially infant sacrifice and universal conspiracies—require independent corroboration.

European witch persecutions involved executions, torture, confiscation, and forced confession. Claims of flights to sabbaths, sex with Satan, cannibalism, and infant sacrifice were often generated inside a learned interrogatory framework. The record documents belief in Satan and institutional violence committed under that belief more securely than it documents coherent underground Satanic churches.

Comparable caution applies to twentieth-century “satanic ritual abuse” claims. Investigations found crimes in individual cases but failed to substantiate the alleged transnational networks routinely murdering children. The panic nonetheless produced wrongful prosecutions, family separations, coercive therapy, and enduring conspiracy narratives.

### Modern organized ritual

- **Church of Satan:** founded by Anton LaVey in San Francisco in 1966; rituals function primarily as psychodrama, self-transformation, or symbolic magic. Its own [institutional history](https://churchofsatan.com/history/) dates the foundation to Walpurgisnacht 1966.
- **Temple of Set:** founded by Michael Aquino and other Church of Satan defectors in 1975. It re-centered the ultimate principle as Set and generally treats consciousness or “isolate intelligence” metaphysically; whether it should be classified as Satanist is disputed.
- **The Satanic Temple:** founded in the early 2010s and publicly active by 2013; nontheistic, using Satan as a literary emblem in religious-liberty and secularist activism. Its [official account](https://thesatanictemple.com/pages/about-us) distinguishes it from the Church of Satan.
- **Theistic Satanists/Luciferians:** decentralized practitioners may offer prayer, meditation, candles, art, or devotional acts to Satan or Lucifer. Claims of a continuous lineage to antiquity are unsubstantiated.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### I. Lexical adversaries and divine agents: first millennium BCE

The oldest layer is not a Devil cult but the flexible Hebrew category of an adversary. Human enemies and YHWH’s own angel can be *satan*. Zechariah and Job personify the court function as “the satan.” The figure remains within divine administration.

### II. Diversification in Second Temple Judaism: ca. 300 BCE–100 CE

Apocalyptic, sapiential, sectarian, and Adamic texts distribute evil agency among Watchers, Azazel, Semihazah, Mastema, Belial, Satan, evil spirits, and human inclination. Foreign rule, sectarian division, theodicy, and apocalyptic expectation provide historical contexts, but no single causal theory explains the development.

Iranian influence has often been proposed because Jews lived under Achaemenid rule and later Zoroastrianism developed strong cosmic dualism. Possible influence remains debated: the dating of relevant Iranian texts is difficult, Jewish figures remain subordinate to one creator, and internal biblical reinterpretation supplies major resources. Simple claims that “Satan was borrowed from Zoroastrian Ahriman” exceed the evidence.

### III. Early Christian consolidation: first–third centuries CE

The New Testament makes Satan central to temptation, demonology, persecution, apostasy, empire, and eschatological conflict. “Satan,” “Devil,” “evil one,” dragon, serpent, accuser, and world ruler converge.

Early Christians also used Satan socially: opponents, heretics, persecutors, Jews, pagans, and rival Christians could be placed under diabolical agency. Elaine Pagels’s influential work emphasized this boundary-making function, though critics caution against reducing the figure to social polemic alone.

### IV. Patristic synthesis: second–fifth centuries

Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Origen, and Augustine progressively harmonized:

- Genesis’s serpent;
- Isaiah’s morning star;
- Ezekiel’s proud ruler/cherub;
- Jesus’ vision of Satan falling;
- fallen angels in Jewish tradition;
- Revelation’s dragon.

The resulting account made Satan a formerly glorious angel whose free pride caused his fall and whose envy produced humanity’s temptation. Augustine’s anti-Manichaean theology insisted that Satan was neither an eternal principle nor evil by substance.

### V. Medieval systematization: sixth–fifteenth centuries

Theology classified demons as fallen incorporeal intelligences. Art increasingly rendered them bodily, grotesque, hybrid, and animalized. Preaching, saints’ lives, drama, and miracle stories placed Satan in everyday temptation.

Dante’s Lucifer summarized betrayal and impotent self-imprisonment. Learned demonology assigned infernal hierarchies, names, offices, planetary correspondences, and ranks. These lists conflict because they are constructions by different authors, not reports from a shared archive.

Antisemitic and anti-heretical polemics frequently described religious outsiders as Satan’s agents. Crusading, inquisitorial, and witch-hunting institutions could frame coercion as combat against the Devil. The documentation establishes these political uses even where the supernatural accusations are historically unverifiable.

### VI. Reformation and confessional conflict: sixteenth–seventeenth centuries

Catholic and Protestant writers both treated opponents as diabolically deceived. Print expanded demonological circulation. Possession, exorcism, witchcraft, and pact narratives flourished.

Milton’s *Paradise Lost* decisively reorganized the figure for Anglophone culture. His Satan is initially sublime and politically eloquent but degenerates through chosen deceit. Later reception often isolates the rebel speeches and overlooks the poem’s narrative exposure of self-enslavement.

### VII. Enlightenment and Romantic inversion: eighteenth–nineteenth centuries

Enlightenment skeptics naturalized Satan as superstition, allegory, or clerical instrument. Romantic writers transformed him into a rebel against tyranny:

- William Blake’s writings, especially *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*;
- Byron’s Cain and related “Satanic” heroes;
- Percy Bysshe Shelley’s Promethean and antiauthoritarian uses;
- Victor Hugo and later French literary Satanisms;
- Baudelaire’s ironic, political, and theological invocations.

This “Romantic Satanism” was mainly literary rather than organized cult. The historical route into modern religious Satanism is analyzed in Per Faxneld’s [*Satanic Feminism* and related Oxford scholarship](https://academic.oup.com/book/11834/1000).

### VIII. Occult and organizational formation: nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Éliphas Lévi’s Baphomet, Stanislas de Guaita’s inverted-pentagram imagery, decadent literature, ceremonial magic, and fabricated exposés created an occult visual vocabulary.

The Taxil hoax of the 1890s falsely claimed Freemasonry secretly worshipped Lucifer; Léo Taxil publicly admitted the fraud in 1897. The hoax remains a major source of recycled pseudo-history.

Aleister Crowley used transgressive titles and Christian adversarial imagery but did not found a religion centered on biblical Satan; identifying Thelema simply as Satanism is inaccurate.

LaVey’s 1966 Church of Satan was the first durable, publicly self-identified Satanic church of major historical influence. *The Satanic Bible* appeared in 1969. Academic scholarship treats modern organized Satanism as substantially innovative rather than a survival from medieval covens ([Oxford, “The Age of Satanism”](https://academic.oup.com/book/2880/chapter-abstract/143494071)).

### IX. Satanic Panic and conspiracy: ca. 1980s–1990s

Recovered-memory therapy, evangelical media, law-enforcement seminars, sensational journalism, and misunderstanding of youth subcultures helped produce claims of hidden ritual-abuse networks. Some isolated crimes used Satanic imagery, but this did not validate the alleged worldwide cult.

The panic revived much older conspiracy templates: secret nocturnal assemblies, sexual inversion, child murder, cannibalism, and infiltration of institutions. Those templates resemble medieval accusations against heretics, Jews, witches, and imagined Devil worshippers more than verified organizational history.

### X. Twenty-first century

Satan remains simultaneously:

- a literal personal being in many Christian and Muslim communities;
- a subordinate accuser or metaphorically interpreted force in different Jewish readings;
- a literary and political symbol in nontheistic Satanism;
- a devotional being in decentralized theistic Satanism;
- an entertainment figure in film, music, comics, and games;
- a conspiratorial label applied to political enemies;
- an element in violent extremist bricolage.

The Order of Nine Angles combines occultism, neo-Nazism, transgression, and promotion of violence. It is not representative of Satanism generally. Academic and governmental sources document its influence on some violent far-right networks: [Open University research record](https://oro.open.ac.uk/88531/) and the [UK Intelligence and Security Committee report](https://isc.independent.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/E02710035-HCP-Extreme-Right-Wing-Terrorism_Accessible.pdf?trk=public_post_comment-text).

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and visual art

- **Dante:** immobilized, three-faced arch-traitor.
- **Milton:** charismatic rebel and self-deceiving tyrant.
- **Blake:** reinterpreted Milton’s energies and produced multiple, non-identical Satan figures.
- **Goethe:** Mephistopheles is a distinct literary devil, not simply the biblical Satan.
- **Romantic art:** Fuseli, Blake, Cabanel, and the Geefs brothers popularized the beautiful or sublime fallen angel.
- **Gustave Doré:** illustrations of Dante and Milton became mass-reproduced templates.
- **Twentieth-century fiction:** Bulgakov’s Woland, Lewis’s infernal tempters, and numerous horror novels separate, combine, or rename the Devil in incompatible ways.
- **Film:** *The Exorcist*, *Rosemary’s Baby*, *The Omen*, and related works profoundly influenced popular expectations of possession, Antichrist, and Satanic conspiracy. They are modern fiction, not documentary evidence of ancient doctrine.
- **Comics and television:** DC’s Lucifer Morningstar, derived partly from Neil Gaiman; Marvel’s Satan-like entities; and television adaptations recast the adversary as detective, antihero, lover, bureaucrat, or emancipator.
- **Games:** role-playing and digital games systematize hells, demon species, alignments, and infernal rankings. These taxonomies should not be retrojected into scripture.

### Esotericism

Renaissance ceremonial magic generally sought to constrain spirits through divine names; it was not necessarily Satan worship. Grimoires list demons and infernal kings with inconsistent hierarchies. Later occultists combined this material with Kabbalah, astrology, alchemy, Tarot, and invented antiquity.

Important modern constructions include:

- Lévi’s Baphomet, 1850s;
- de Guaita’s goat-pentagram, 1897;
- LaVey’s Sigil of Baphomet and ritual system, 1960s;
- Aquino’s Setian revelation claim, 1975;
- chaos-magical use of Satanic forms as deliberately mutable symbols;
- internet demonolatry and “ancient deity” claims derived from modern correspondence lists.

Claims that Satan was secretly worshipped under the name Baphomet by the Templars, that the exact Sigil of Baphomet is ancient, or that modern “Qliphothic” charts reproduce an unchanged biblical system are not supported.

### Political appropriation

Satan has been used to characterize:

- imperial persecutors and rebellious subjects;
- heretics and rival confessions;
- Jews, Muslims, Christians, atheists, and indigenous religions;
- revolutionary movements and reactionary regimes;
- abolitionists and slaveholders;
- communists, capitalists, nationalists, and globalists;
- musicians, sexual minorities, feminists, and political candidates.

Such accusations document the accuser’s boundary-making more directly than the target’s belief.

Modern nontheistic groups reverse the symbolism. The Satanic Temple invokes Satan as a figure of principled dissent in church–state controversies. The Church of Satan instead emphasizes individual sovereignty and rejects TST’s activist model. Neither should be represented as worshipping the Christian Devil in the ordinary theistic sense.

### Misidentification of Yazidis

Yazidis venerate Ṭāʾūsê Melek, the Peacock Angel. Muslim and Christian outsiders have often equated his refusal/prostration-related traditions with Iblīs and called Yazidis “devil worshippers.” This identification is rejected by Yazidis and by modern specialists. It contributed to persecution and was explicitly exploited by ISIS in its violence against Yazidis. A study of accountability for ISIS crimes notes that ISIS used the alleged Satan equation to classify Yazidis as idolaters outside protected categories ([ICCT report](https://icct.nl/sites/default/files/2024-06/Holding%20ISIL%20Accountable%20Prosecuting%20Crimes%20In%20Iraq%20and%20Syria%20full%20book.pdf)).

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is the Hebrew satan already “the Devil”?

Older confessional readings frequently treated every supernatural *satan* as the same fallen angel. Historical-critical scholarship stresses grammar and narrative role:

- the definite article in Job and Zechariah suggests an office;
- Numbers uses the term functionally for YHWH’s angel;
- Chronicles may represent a transition but remains ambiguous;
- none of these texts narrates primordial rebellion.

Some scholars still see sufficient continuity to speak of an early form of Satan, provided later attributes are not imported. Others argue that reconstructing one stable Hebrew-biblical being is methodologically impossible. The debate is surveyed in Derek Brown’s [research review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/1476993X10363030).

### Persian influence

The theory that Jewish Satan derives from Zoroastrian Angra Mainyu has had enormous popular influence. Responsible versions propose stimulus or analogy rather than simple borrowing. Problems include:

- uncertain dating of Iranian sources;
- major structural differences between traditions;
- continued subordination of Jewish opponents to God;
- the diversity of Jewish Satanologies;
- internal development from accusation, divine council, chaos combat, and theodicy.

The influence remains possible at particular points but is not an established one-to-one genealogy.

### Serpent, Lucifer, and Satan

Three distinctions are essential:

1. **Genesis 3:** serpent, not named Satan.
2. **Isaiah 14:** fallen morning star in a taunt against Babylon’s king.
3. **Ezekiel 28:** lament over Tyre’s ruler using Edenic and cherubic imagery.

Jewish and Christian reception connected these with Satan at different times. Revelation supplies the canonical Christian equation of dragon, ancient serpent, Devil, and Satan. Origen, Tertullian, Jerome, Augustine, and later theology consolidated Isaiah and Ezekiel into the fall narrative.

Thus “Lucifer was Satan’s angelic birth name” is a late interpretive convention, not a statement in the Hebrew Bible.

### Is Iblīs an angel or jinn?

Qurʾān 18:50 says Iblīs “was of the jinn.” Prostration passages say the angels prostrated “except Iblīs.” Muslim interpreters developed several reconciliations:

- he was a jinn included in the command;
- “jinn” here refers to a hidden angelic class;
- he originated as jinn but rose among angels;
- he was an angel by rank or association, not species.

Modern translations often conceal the distinction by translating both Iblīs and al-Shayṭān as “Satan.” The primary Arabic must therefore be checked.

### Historic Satanism

Until the modern period, “Satanist” was overwhelmingly an accusation imposed on others. Some individuals performed deliberate blasphemy, magical invocation, or diabolical self-identification, but evidence for a continuous, organized, hereditary Satanic religion is absent.

Modern religious Satanism emerges from Romantic literature, secularization, occultism, and self-conscious inversion, culminating institutionally in LaVey. Oxford’s historical synthesis describes the development from Romantic and esoteric antecedents to modern organization ([*Children of Lucifer*](https://academic.oup.com/book/11834/1000)).

### State of the evidence

- Biblical compositions survive through later manuscripts and ancient translations, not authorial originals.
- Qumran transformed knowledge of Second Temple angelology by supplying pre-Christian Hebrew and Aramaic manuscripts.
- 1 Enoch survives complete in Ethiopic; Aramaic Qumran fragments confirmed its Jewish antiquity.
- Jubilees likewise survives fully in Ethiopic but is anchored by Hebrew fragments.
- The Life of Adam and Eve survives in divergent recensions; reconstructing an “original” is hazardous.
- Patristic works sometimes survive only through translations, excerpts, hostile quotation, or manuscript traditions centuries later.
- Medieval judicial records preserve scribal and interrogatorial constructions, not unmediated speech.
- Modern Satanic organizations have websites, publications, films, legal documents, and participant testimony, but membership statistics are often self-reported or unavailable.

### Persistent discredited or unsupported claims

- Satan is directly named in Genesis 3.
- Lucifer is Satan’s Hebrew personal name.
- Isaiah 14 is straightforwardly a biography of Satan.
- Baphomet is an ancient Satanic god.
- Pan was deliberately copied to create Satan in a single historical act.
- All horned deities are Satan.
- Yazidis worship Satan.
- Freemasonry is institutionally Luciferian.
- medieval Europe contained one centrally organized Satanic witch church.
- modern organized Satanism descends continuously from ancient pagan cults.
- 666 is unambiguously Satan’s personal number.
- Dante invented hell or Milton invented Satan. Both transformed pre-existing traditions; neither originated the entire complex.
