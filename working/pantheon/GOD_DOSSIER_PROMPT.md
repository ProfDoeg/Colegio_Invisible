# GOD_DOSSIER_PROMPT.md

Standing prompt for commissioning a pantheon research dossier — gods, angels, and specific named divine creatures (not saints; saints are human and take the person prompt at `working/journeys/dossiers/PROMPT_TEMPLATE.md`). Prose Markdown, stage one of the pipeline (dossier first; any structured extraction comes later as a separate step). Sibling to the person prompt and to `working/networks/NETWORK_DOSSIER_PROMPT.md`. A specific named being qualifies (Buraq qualifies; "dwarves" in general does not, though a specific named dwarf would). Drafted by Fable 5, 2026-09-06.

---

[DEITY/BEING NAME] ([TRADITION(S)])
[ONE-LINE DESCRIPTION]

Please conduct a comprehensive, source-driven research dossier on this divine being. I want the fullest reconstructable history — not a biography, since the subject has no ordinary birth-to-death life, but the complete documented record of the being as it appears in texts, images, cult, and belief, from earliest attestation to the present. The being's existence in myth is the subject; the evidence for that existence — inscriptions, hymns, scriptures, temples, images, testimony — is the material. Reconstruct both: what the traditions say the being is and does, and what the historical record shows about when, where, and how people have said it.

The evidentiary discipline here works differently than for a human subject. For a god, essentially everything is "myth" in the ordinary sense, so the useful question is never "did this really happen" but always textual and historical: what does the earliest attested source actually say, in what language, on what physical support, datable how; how did the story, name, image, or attribute change across later sources, regions, and eras; what is early core and what is late accretion, learned systematization, or modern invention. Hold that discipline throughout. For every significant claim about the being, say which source carries it and from when. Distinguish clearly: securely attested early material, later canonical elaboration, regional variant, learned or priestly systematization, syncretistic identification, medieval or early-modern accretion, folklore, modern scholarly reconstruction, and modern invention (including material that circulates today as "ancient" but is demonstrably recent). Where traditions or sources conflict — on genealogy, on the being's nature, on a myth's outcome — give the competing versions with their sources and dates rather than silently harmonizing them into one smooth story. Do not flatten regional and historical variants into a single composite identity; the variants ARE the history. Mark anything that cannot be established explicitly. Do not sanitize inconvenient material — human sacrifice, temple prostitution as alleged or documented, cult violence, the political uses of the god, suppression and forced conversion, and modern extremist appropriation are part of many beings' actual records and must be stated plainly where documented, with the same care about what is documented versus alleged.

Organize the dossier with sections along the following lines, adapting as the being's actual character requires:

1. **Identification, Names, and Epithets.** Who or what the being is, in one dense orienting passage; then the full documented onomastics: names in every relevant language and script (with transliterations and, where useful, the original script), etymologies including disputed ones, epithets and cult titles with their meanings and where each is attested, name variants across regions and periods, and identifications with other beings under interpretatio graeca/romana or comparable practices in other traditions — treating each such identification as a datable historical event with a source, not a timeless equivalence. State the being's class (god, goddess, angel, archangel, jinn, demon, psychopomp, mount, monster, spirit) as each tradition classifies it, noting where the classification itself shifted over time.

2. **Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus.** The oldest securely dated evidence for the being — inscription, papyrus, tablet, hymn, iconography, theophoric personal names — with dates, findspots, and what exactly that earliest evidence does and does not say. Then the full corpus of primary texts in which the being appears: scriptures, hymns, epics, ritual texts, magical papyri, sagas, commentaries, apocrypha — each with its language, approximate date of composition (and of the surviving manuscripts, where the gap matters), and the being's role in it. These texts are the primary evidence itself, not background reading; treat them as a person-dossier would treat archival documents. Pair them with the modern critical scholarship on each — editions, translations, and the studies that establish their dating and transmission.

3. **Domain, Attributes, and Iconography.** What the being governs, protects, embodies, or threatens, and how that domain shifted over time; physical form and its variants; standard iconography and how to recognize the being in art, with the earliest and most important surviving depictions named and dated; symbols, emblems, weapons, tools, regalia; sacred animals, plants, colors, numbers, days, celestial bodies; associated objects and substances; and the being's characteristic actions, powers, and limitations as the sources give them. Note where an attribute now considered defining is in fact a late or regional development.

4. **Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings.** Parentage, birth or origin narratives, consorts, offspring, siblings, rivals, allies, servants, mounts, and enemies — and the being's position in the hierarchy of its pantheon or celestial order, including how that position changed (promotion, demotion, absorption of other gods' functions). Mythological genealogy is almost never single: where different texts, cities, or eras give conflicting family trees, lay the versions side by side with their sources and dates, and say which version became dominant and why, rather than presenting one tree as canonical. Include syncretistic fusions and equations with beings from other traditions as relations in their own right, each dated and sourced.

   **Every documented god-god interaction gets its own entry.** Beyond genealogy, give a dedicated subsection listing every specific recorded interaction between this being and any OTHER named divine being — a meeting, combat, seduction, deception, alliance, judgment, contest, theft, rescue, punishment, or joint appearance in a single scene or episode. For each: name the other being, state the nature of the interaction in one line, give the source text and its date, and label it with the same attested/canonical/regional-variant/late-accretion/modern-retelling tiers used elsewhere in this dossier. Treat this the way a genealogy chart treats a family tie — as a discrete, checkable claim, not folded into general narrative prose. Do not force an interaction that isn't textually attested merely because two beings belong to the same pantheon or era; absence of a documented interaction is itself worth noting plainly.

5. **Major Myths and Narrative Cycles.** The principal stories, told fully — myth is the primary content here, not an appendix. For each major myth or cycle: what the story says in its fullest form; its earliest attested source and date; the significant textual and regional variants, with what each changes and where each appears; what can be said about the story's development (early core versus later elaboration, borrowings from or into other traditions); and its function where scholars have established one (etiological, ritual, political). Apply the same labeling discipline as elsewhere — early attested / later canonical / regional variant / late accretion / modern retelling — but understand that here the labels track the history of the story, not its truth.

6. **Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice.** The institutional and material record: major cult centers and temples with locations, dates of foundation, flourishing, destruction or conversion, and what survives archaeologically; priesthoods and religious offices, their organization, recruitment, and privileges; festivals and their calendars; sacrifice, offering, prayer, divination, oracle, pilgrimage, and magical practice associated with the being; votive and epigraphic evidence of ordinary worship; theophoric names as evidence of popularity; the economics and politics of the cult where documented. Distinguish what is archaeologically or epigraphically attested from what is inferred from texts, and both from later tradition about the cult.

7. **Historical Evolution of the Cult and the Figure.** A periodized account from first attestation to the present: emergence; growth and geographic spread; peak, with the evidence for calling it that; transformations under new regimes, empires, and religions; syncretism, absorption, or identification with other beings as historical processes with dates and mechanisms; suppression, Christianization, Islamization, or other displacement where it occurred, with the documented events; survival in folk practice, folklore, magic, or under another name; and any continuous or revived worship today. Make the periodization explicit and note where scholars periodize differently. This section is the dossier's spine: it is where the being's "life" actually is.

8. **Modern Reception and Reinvention.** The being's afterlife in the modern world, treated as seriously as the ancient evidence: esoteric, occult, and hermetic reinterpretation (Renaissance syncretism, theosophy, the Golden Dawn and successor orders, chaos magic, modern grimoire traditions), with the key texts and figures dated; neopagan and reconstructionist worship, with its actual history and scale where establishable; New Age and psychological readings (Jungian archetypes and the like) as datable interpretive traditions; appearances in literature, art, film, comics, and games that have materially shaped the being's modern image; nationalist or political appropriation; and commercial use. Say plainly where a modern "tradition" about the being is a documented modern invention, and by whom.

9. **Historiography and Scholarly Debate.** How the being has been studied: the major scholarly works, schools, and controversies (etymology disputes, origin theories, comparativist claims and their critiques, dating fights over key texts); the state of the primary evidence — what survives, in what archives, museums, and editions, what was lost or destroyed; key archaeological or manuscript discoveries that changed the picture; and discredited theories that still circulate popularly, identified as such.

10. **Chronology.** A compact timeline — year-by-year, century-by-century, or period-by-period as the evidence allows — of the being's ATTESTATION and cultic history, not a life: first evidence, key texts composed, temples founded and destroyed, syncretisms, suppressions, rediscoveries, publications, revivals, down to the present. Every entry is an event in the historical record about the being, each anchored to its evidence.

11. **Sources.**

Search broadly and deeply; prefer the primary texts themselves (in critical editions and reliable translations), epigraphic and papyrological corpora, archaeological reports, and academic scholarship over encyclopedia summaries — Wikipedia and general encyclopedias may orient, but follow their citations into the underlying sources. Search in every language relevant to the being's traditions — the ancient source languages where feasible, and the modern languages of the scholarship (German, French, Italian, Spanish, Arabic, and others as relevant), not only English. Look for obscure material, conflicting variants, local cults, and details that disappear from short summaries. Write without editorial overlay — no moralizing, no apologetics for or against any religion, no telling the reader what to believe, and no treating any living tradition's claims as either true or false; report what is attested, by whom, and when. Favor documentary presentation. Do not compress for elegance; detail over brevity.

End with one deduplicated list of every URL actually used (full https:// URLs, one per line, no shortened links, no duplicates). Output as a downloadable .md file named [slug]_pantheon_dossier.md, where [slug] is the being's name in lowercase with underscores.
